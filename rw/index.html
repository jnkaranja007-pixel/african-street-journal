Rwanda vies for OIF leadership as DRC threatens to quit

The Democratic Republic of Congo may leave the Organisation Internationale de la Francophonie if its candidate does not win the secretary general post.

Rwanda and the Democratic Republic of Congo are competing for the secretary general position at the Organisation Internationale de la Francophonie (OIF) in Cambodia. Kinshasa has stated that if its candidate, Juliana Lumumba, does not win, the DRC will withdraw from the organisation.

Diplomatic sources indicate that Paris is increasing engagement with both Kinshasa and Kigali as the OIF summit in Phnom Penh approaches. The DRC has sent a 100-strong delegation to the Phnom Penh elections, scheduled for November 16, which contravenes OIF rules limiting accompanying persons to three per president.

Organisers in Phnom Penh are questioning the purpose of the large Congolese delegation. The rivalry between Rwanda and the DRC has extended to this international diplomatic stage, with significant implications for the OIF's future.

Why it mattersRwanda is competing for a leadership role within an international organisation, a contest that has led to political tension with the Democratic Republic of Congo.

International NGOs received $228.3 million in Rwanda

182 organisations manage development funding across 30 districts, facing questions on impact and distribution.

In Rwanda's 30 districts, 182 international non-governmental organisations (INGOs) manage programmes in healthcare, education, agriculture, poverty reduction, environmental protection, and governance.

During the 2024/25 financial year, these INGOs received approximately US$228.3 million in funding, following US$239.9 million in the preceding year, totalling about US$468.2 million over two years.

These resources, equivalent to roughly Rwf619 billion at the 2024 exchange rate, are managed while 1,487 local NGOs compete for funding, raising questions about distribution and local participation.

Why it mattersInternational NGOs operating in Rwanda manage hundreds of millions of dollars in development funding annually, impacting various sectors.

Kigali plants 2,238 trees to start forestry season

More than 5,000 residents joined the Rwanda Forestry Authority and Ministry of Environment on Saturday, November 7.

Kigali planted 2,238 tree seedlings across eight hectares in Gahanga Sector, Kicukiro District, marking the start of Rwanda's 2026/2027 tree-planting season on Saturday, November 7. The community work programme, held in Nyabigugu Village, involved over 5,000 residents, security personnel, and partners.

The Rwanda Forestry Authority and the Ministry of Environment organised the event under the theme “My Tree, My Legacy,” encouraging community responsibility for tree maintenance and protection. Fidèle Bingwa, Permanent Secretary at the Ministry of Environment, urged commitment to the trees' growth, stating their legacy will benefit lives. Concorde Nsengumuremyi, Director General of the Rwanda Forestry Authority, highlighted that expanding forest cover addresses soil erosion and land degradation.

The planted species, including Imisave and Jacaranda, are expected to conserve soil, enhance hillside appearance, and support biodiversity near the Nyabarongo River wetland. This initiative is part of Rwanda's broader strategy to increase forest cover, manage natural resources sustainably, and bolster climate change response.

Why it mattersResidents of Gahanga Sector are directly affected by the tree planting, which aims to protect agricultural land and secure a healthier environment by reducing soil erosion and controlling pests.

First Lady watches play on Rwandan history

Carte d’Identité uses humour and personal memory to explore family displacement and exile.

First Lady Mrs Jeannette Kagame attended a play titled Carte d’Identité at the Kigali Convention Centre on Saturday evening. The performance, written and performed by artist Diogène Ntarindwa, known as Atome, revisits difficult chapters of Rwanda’s history.

The play centres on the experiences of Atome’s father, Philippe Niwemuto, detailing his exile and search for identity. Ntarindwa portrays multiple characters, employing humour to convey stories of loss and displacement across Rwanda, Burundi, and the Democratic Republic of Congo.

Carte d’Identité begins with earlier Rwandan history before focusing on Ntarindwa’s family experiences. Atome shared his father's advice to identify as Rwandan above all else, shaping the narrative's exploration of national identity.

Why it mattersRwandan citizens, particularly the youth, can learn about their country's challenging past through a relatable artistic performance.

Agriculture · 2026-11-09

Trócaire helps Rwandan farmers adapt to climate change

Sustainable farming practices and irrigation systems are improving harvests and incomes for vulnerable communities.

Trócaire and its partners are implementing agroecological approaches in Rwanda to help vulnerable farming communities produce more food and build livelihoods resilient to climate shocks. This method connects sustainable farming with climate resilience, healthy soils, and local knowledge, enabling farmers to adapt to a changing climate and sustain their farms. In Manwari Cell, Mbazi Sector, Nyamagabe District, these interventions are visibly improving harvests and increasing agricultural productivity.

Farmers who previously struggled with insufficient water now benefit from gravity-fed and solar-powered irrigation systems, allowing year-round cultivation. Marleen Masclee, Country Director of Trócaire Rwanda, stated that agriculture is inseparable from climate resilience, household income, nutrition, and environmental protection. The organization aims for farmers to produce food sustainably, ensuring healthy soils for future generations. Trócaire has worked in Rwanda since 1994, shifting focus to climate resilience and livelihoods.

Through partnerships with organizations like DUHAMIC-ADRI and DUTERIMBERE, supported by the Scottish Government, Trócaire has facilitated terracing of hundreds of hectares. Farmers' cooperatives such as UNICOPAGI and advocacy groups like CCOAIB also contribute to supporting farmers and informing them about agricultural policies. These interventions are designed to provide lasting benefits to communities.

Why it mattersVulnerable farming communities in Rwanda are improving their food production, nutrition, and incomes through sustainable agricultural practices and better water access.
