Morocco extends fuel subsidies for transport operators

Government offers financial support for passenger and freight carriers as diesel prices rise.

Morocco's government will provide direct financial support to road transport operators, including passenger and freight carriers, due to increasing fuel costs. The Head of Government’s Office announced the additional subsidy will cover a 15-day period.

Applications for the subsidy open on Tuesday, November 10, 2026, and eligible transport professionals can submit them via the government's online platform. This measure follows a rise in diesel prices by 50 centimes to MAD 1 per liter at the start of November, with some stations nearing MAD 17.

The government stated the support aims to protect transport operators from fuel price volatility and minimize its impact on their businesses.

Moroccan diesel prices rise, tomato costs exceed MAD 12

Consumers face higher fuel and food costs as November begins, with wholesale tomato prices reaching MAD 9 per kilogram.

Moroccan consumers began November with increased diesel prices, which rose by up to one dirham per liter, nearing MAD 17 at some stations. Gasoline prices saw a slight decrease. Tomatoes sold for over MAD 12 a kilogram in certain markets, adding to existing food inflation.

Abderrazak Chabi, president of the Casablanca Wholesale Fruit and Vegetable Traders Association, cited declining seasonal production, delayed harvests, and unfavorable weather as causes for high tomato prices. He suggested suspending or restricting tomato exports to address domestic shortages. However, producers dispute this, pointing to plant disease, expensive inputs, and weak yields as the primary issues.

Aziz El Maanaoui, president of the Chtouka Agricultural Producers Association, stated that export earnings help producers cover rising costs and maintain domestic supply. He reported wholesale tomato prices ranging from MAD 3 to MAD 10 per kilogram based on quality. Chabi anticipates tomato prices may decrease by the end of November with new market supplies.

Why it mattersMoroccan households face increased costs for essential goods like fuel and tomatoes, impacting their budgets.

Moroccan youth take precarious jobs to support families

Young workers in Meknes leave school or study while working odd jobs, impacting their personal development.

Young people in Morocco are leaving school or taking on precarious jobs to support themselves and their families. Some juggle odd jobs with their studies, often sacrificing personal development. These youths come from various regions of the kingdom and settle temporarily in cities like Meknes to find work.

In Meknes, these young workers shared their experiences, highlighting the difficult choices they face. The jobs they take are often unstable, offering little security. This situation affects their ability to focus on education and personal growth, creating a cycle of struggle.

The primary evidence indicates that these young workers are driven by the need for financial support. The temporary nature of their settlement suggests a search for immediate income rather than long-term career prospects.

Why it mattersYoung Moroccans are forced into precarious labor, impacting their education and personal development.

Morocco justice minister pledges illicit enrichment law

Abdellatif Ouahbi faces parliamentary pressure for delayed anti-corruption reforms including asset declarations and whistleblower protections.

Morocco's Justice Minister Abdellatif Ouahbi pledged on November 2 to introduce new laws targeting illicit enrichment, conflicts of interest, and money laundering. He stated these measures must uphold the presumption of innocence and fair-trial rights during a House of Representatives session.

Lawmakers, including PJD's Abdessamad Haiker, demanded stronger asset declarations and whistleblower protections, criticizing the minister's response as "pitiful" and referencing a previously withdrawn illicit enrichment bill. Ouahbi countered that unexplained wealth should not automatically imply criminal wrongdoing, raising concerns about the right to remain silent.

The ministry is considering a separate anti-corruption code to consolidate offenses like illicit enrichment and money laundering. Ouahbi invited further contributions from parliamentary groups, including the PJD, for these proposed reforms.

Why it mattersMoroccan lawmakers are pushing for stronger anti-corruption measures that could impact public officials and their financial dealings.

Morocco faces buffer zone uncertainty after MINURSO withdrawal

The Polisario Front has declared itself no longer bound by a 1997 military agreement governing the buffer zone.

Morocco faces uncertainty over the buffer zone east of its security wall following the withdrawal of MINURSO. This five-kilometer-wide strip, governed by a 1997 military agreement, has been used by the Polisario Front to launch attacks. The agreement bans military forces and equipment from entering the zone and prohibits firing within or over it, rules the Polisario Front has violated.

Sahrawi affairs researcher Rahal Salek stated that the Polisario Front declared itself unbound by Military Agreement No. 1 after November 2020 events at the Guerguerat crossing. He noted the UN Security Council describes the situation as scattered clashes, not full-scale war. Salek finds it difficult to predict future events as the Polisario continues to claim it is at war and rejects UN calls to cease provocations.

Ramadan Massoud Al Arabi of CORCAS emphasized that Russia's veto marked the political end of the Polisario Front. He believes this gives Morocco an opportunity to regain control of areas like Tifariti and Bir Lehlou. Al Arabi added that if Polisario members remain in Algeria, any attacks would originate from Algerian territory, making Algeria directly responsible.

Why it mattersMorocco faces potential security challenges and territorial disputes following the withdrawal of MINURSO and the Polisario Front's disregard for military agreements.
