Morocco energy import bill climbs 28.9% amid war

The nation's energy and lubricant imports reached MAD 68.58 billion in the first half of 2026, up from MAD 53.19 billion a year earlier.

Morocco is among four Middle Eastern and North African economies particularly exposed to rising international fuel and shipping costs due to the war in the Middle East, according to the International Energy Agency (IEA). The conflict has increased the cost of crude oil, petroleum products, and natural gas, adding to the energy import bills of Morocco, Egypt, Tunisia, and Lebanon.

The combined monthly energy import bill for these four countries rose by nearly 60% in March 2026, reaching $4.6 billion, and climbed to $4.8 billion by June, a 66% increase from January. Provisional figures from Morocco's Foreign Exchange Office show energy and lubricant imports reached MAD 68.58 billion in the first half of 2026, an increase of MAD 15.39 billion compared to the same period in 2025.

The IEA warned that these economies are vulnerable to higher international fuel and shipping costs, which push up transport, electricity, and household energy expenses. The agency noted this crisis tests existing subsidy systems and highlights their limitations in cushioning economies against external price shocks.

Morocco Interior Ministry budgets MAD 58.7 billion for 2027 development

The draft budget aims to accelerate human and territorial development and strengthen strategic sovereignty.

Morocco's Interior Ministry has allocated MAD 58.7 billion for its 2027 budget to accelerate human and territorial development.

Presenting the draft budget to the Lower House on Thursday, Interior Minister Abdelouafi Laftit stated it aligns with High Royal Directives to strengthen strategic sovereignty in water, energy, food, and digital technology.

The budget also supports advanced regionalization, administrative devolution, and new integrated development programs, while continuing institutional reforms and modernization projects.

Morocco secures US$112 million loan for port strategy

The French Development Agency loan will fund infrastructure, renewable energy, and fishing port modernization until 2030.

Morocco's National Ports Agency has secured US$112 million in financing from the French Development Agency (AFD) for its 2023-2030 investment programme. The 13-year loan, signed in Rabat on November 3, 2026, will support upgrades to port infrastructure, renewable energy and desalination facilities, fishing ports, and port quay electrification.

The financing specifically targets ports in southern Morocco, including Tan-Tan, Tarfaya, Laâyoune, Boujdour, Dakhla, and Lamhiriz, aiming to boost regional economic development and strengthen the country's Atlantic coast position. This loan is structured as a non-sovereign loan, placing repayment risk on the agency rather than the Moroccan government.

Moroccan ports processed 262.6 million tonnes of cargo in 2025, an increase of 8.9% from 241.2 million tonnes in 2024, with Tanger Med handling 10.3 million TEUs. The development of southern ports like Dakhla and Laâyoune is intended to capture transit trade with Sahel economies, a market currently served by West African ports.

Why it mattersMoroccan businesses and the national economy will benefit from improved port infrastructure and expanded trade routes.

Morocco hotel attack targeted tourists in 1994

Three men opened fire on visitors at a Marrakesh hotel, marking the country's first Islamist terrorism incident.

In 1994, three men attacked tourists at a hotel in Marrakesh, Morocco. This incident marked the first time the country experienced violence from Islamist terrorism.

The attack occurred in Marrakesh, a city known for its tourism. This event contrasted with Algeria's experience of a civil war between 1991 and 2002, which involved the government and Islamist groups.

Morocco had been spared Islamist terrorism until this 1994 event. The attack on tourists at the Atlas Asni hotel in Marrakesh was a significant moment for the nation's security.

Why it mattersMoroccan tourists were directly affected by the 1994 terrorist attack on a hotel in Marrakesh.

Moroccan household bad loans hit MAD 44.3 billion

Non-performing loans rose 5% year-on-year, exceeding one-tenth of total household credit.

Moroccan households owed MAD 44.3 billion in non-performing loans by the end of September 2026, a 5% increase from the previous year. Bank Al-Maghrib data shows these bad loans represent 10.9% of all credit extended to individuals, indicating borrowers' ongoing struggles to meet financial commitments. Total bank lending to households reached MAD 405.5 billion, with real estate loans at MAD 262.8 billion and consumer loans at MAD 63.8 billion.

The central bank's November report highlighted that household non-performing loans grew faster than overall lending to this group, which saw a 3.5% annual increase. While the annual growth rate of bad loans slowed to 5% in September from 6.7% in August, their total value saw a slight 0.8% decrease month-on-month. The figures raise concerns about households' capacity to manage repayments alongside daily expenses, especially for those with both housing and consumer debts.

Average lending rates for individuals fell to 5.56% in the third quarter of 2026, with housing loan rates at 4.50% and consumer loan rates at 6.68%. Despite lower average rates, the actual impact on monthly repayments varies based on individual loan terms. Total non-performing loans across both households and businesses reached MAD 105.5 billion by the end of September, a 2.3% year-on-year rise.

Why it mattersMoroccan households are facing difficulties in repaying their loans, with bad debts reaching MAD 44.3 billion.
