Libyan professor links wage reform to spending control

Ali Al-Sharif stated that closing wage gaps requires transparent management of oil revenues.

An economics professor from the University of Benghazi, Ali Al-Sharif, stated that reforming salaries in Libya necessitates controlling public spending and rationalizing expenses. He told Al-Araby Al-Jadeed newspaper that improving the governance of oil revenues is also required for addressing wage imbalances.

Al-Sharif emphasized the need for transparent collection and management of oil revenues to ensure sustainable wage gap closure. He indicated that these measures are required to address salary imbalances effectively.

The professor's comments focus on the economic reforms needed to stabilize Libya's wage structure.

Why it mattersLibyan citizens' salaries and public spending are directly affected by the proposed economic reforms.

Libya spent $10 billion annually on development

Minister Suhail Bousheha stated that funds were allocated for infrastructure and growth opportunities.

Libya spent an average of $10 billion annually on development over the past four years, according to Economy and Trade Minister Suhail Bousheha. These funds targeted infrastructure development and improving opportunities for growth and investment.

Bousheha made these remarks at the World Investment Forum 2026 in Doha, where he highlighted Libya's strategic importance due to its oil reserves. He stated the government aims to transform resources into productive investments and sustainable economic diversification, moving from recovery to development.

The minister added that the spending aims to enhance the business environment, attract quality investments, and expand partnerships with global companies. New initiatives starting in 2026 reflect Libya's efforts to improve its global investment position and diversify its economy.

Why it mattersLibya's development spending aims to improve the business environment and attract quality investments.

Tripoli drainage stations halt, 130 buildings at risk

Rainfall preparations continue amid warnings of infrastructure decay and building collapse in Libya's capital.

Several sewage lift stations in Tripoli's Central Municipality are out of service, causing stagnant rainwater in low-lying areas. Sami Hammadi, a member of the Central Municipal Council, warned of the deteriorating infrastructure after repeated appeals to authorities for repairs have gone unanswered.

The municipality is coordinating with the Tripoli Security Directorate, the Water and Sanitation Company, and other agencies for current preparations, which include deploying drainage trucks. Recent rainfall measured between 1 and 5 millimeters, significantly less than previous levels that reached 92 millimeters.

Additionally, 130 buildings within the municipality are at risk of collapse, with the Tripoli City Development Authority responsible for repairs or redevelopment. Plans are in place to relocate residents of dangerous buildings to hotels if necessary, coordinated with the National Safety Authority and the municipality.

Why it mattersResidents in Tripoli face potential flooding and the danger of collapsing buildings due to neglected infrastructure.
