Ethiopia and Eritrea trade drone and artillery strikes near Badme

Fighting near the disputed border town has forced civilians from their homes, according to diplomatic sources.

Ethiopian forces have reportedly used drones while Eritrean troops fired heavy artillery near the disputed border town of Badme, and civilians have fled their homes, diplomatic sources told Addis Standard on Saturday. Clashes escalated over several days, according to two diplomats briefed on intelligence assessments. Badme sits about 800 kilometres north of Addis Ababa and 130 kilometres southwest of Asmara, and was the centre of the 1998-2000 border war that killed tens of thousands.

Residents describe nighttime explosions, shuttered businesses and families packing up as Ethiopian federal troops build up around the town. The Armed Conflict Location & Event Data Project (ACLED) said on November 13 that Ethiopian troops sat between Sheraro and Badme while Eritrean forces held positions near its edge, and that neither side controlled the town. It also recorded Ethiopian artillery fire near Badme and fresh clashes between federal troops and Tigrayan fighters.

Ethiopian forces had pushed toward the border before taking Sheraro on November 3, according to conflict monitors. Ties between the two countries have soured over claims that each backs armed groups and over Ethiopia's wish for Red Sea access. G7 foreign ministers, meeting in Paris on November 9-10, voiced great concern and urged both sides to exercise maximum restraint and pursue diplomatic solutions.

Why it mattersFamilies living near Badme are leaving their homes as the artillery and drone exchanges continue, and the fighting has already displaced civilians in northern Ethiopia.

Politics · 2026-11-17

Eritrean officials mock reconciliation calls

Senior PFDJ officials dismissed questions about national reconciliation and dialogue.

Two senior officials of Eritrea's ruling PFDJ party, Yousif Saiq and the late Ambassador Mohammed Ismail Omer, reportedly reacted with mockery to questions about national reconciliation. The exchange occurred during a meeting where President Isaias Afwerki was present, following his opening speech.

A journalist paraphrased a question to Afwerki asking why the nation had not reconciled or why the ruling party had not initiated reconciliation or allowed other organizations to participate in government. Mohammed Ismail Omer was seen whispering to Afwerki, who then dismissed the concern with a smirk, stating, "No problem, let him be."

Afwerki responded by claiming the government does not discriminate between its combatants and others, even prioritizing the latter for housing and jobs. The journalist expressed disdain, questioning if veterans receiving entitled benefits should be seen as a favor and if some combatants deserved priority over others.

Why it mattersSenior PFDJ officials dismissed questions about national reconciliation and dialogue, impacting the potential for open discussion within the nation.

Eritrean American writer urges Eritrea to learn from democratic self-correction

Semere T Habtemariam, writing in Awate, says the looming threat of war makes lessons on peaceful change urgent for Eritrea.

Semere T Habtemariam, an Eritrean American writer and former campaigner for public office, has published an article in Awate arguing that Eritrea and Ethiopia can learn from the democratic experience of America and Britain. He says the looming threat of war is the most urgent issue facing Eritrea and that he cannot remain silent on it.

He writes that democracy's greatest strength is its capacity for self-correction, allowing societies to debate, reconsider and change course through peaceful and constitutional means rather than violence or coercion. He says he had doubted democracy after the reelection of President Trump, but his confidence was renewed by later political developments in the United States.

He concludes that democracies may stumble but contain the means for renewal, allowing societies to make mistakes without becoming prisoners of them. He applies this principle to Eritrea and the wider Horn of Africa.

Why it mattersReaders weighing the risk of war in the Horn of Africa are offered a case for peaceful, constitutional ways of changing course.
