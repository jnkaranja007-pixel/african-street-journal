Ethiopia accuses Eritrea of moving troops into Tigray

Ethiopia says Eritrean forces entered Tigray, days after it broke diplomatic ties with Eritrea and expelled an Egyptian diplomat in early October.

Ethiopia has accused Eritrea of moving troops into Tigray, a claim Eritrea denies. Eritrea framed the accusation as a declaration of war. The allegation follows Ethiopia's decision in early October to break ties with Eritrea and expel an Egyptian diplomat.

Fighting in Tigray resumed in early August and has escalated, with drone strikes becoming more frequent, according to the Congressional Research Service. On September 20, the Tigray People's Liberation Front and six other groups announced the Alliance for Survival, an armed alliance against Prime Minister Abiy Ahmed's government. Eritrean and Amhara forces were not party to the 2022 Cessation of Hostilities Agreement, and the federal government did not enforce their withdrawal.

A Congressional Research Service report says overlapping rebellions and tensions with Eritrea, Egypt and Sudan could widen into a regional conflict. U.S. officials have urged both countries to respect each other's sovereignty and territorial integrity. Secretary of State Marco Rubio said, "The last thing the world needs right now is another war."

Why it mattersEthiopians in Tigray and across the north face renewed fighting and drone strikes, while Eritrea faces a direct accusation of troop movements and a declared war.

Ethiopia-Eritrea fighting could disrupt Red Sea shipping, analysts warn

A US think-tank paper says fighting near Eritrea's Assab port could raise war-risk insurance costs on the Bab el-Mandeb route even without attacks on ships.

Renewed hostilities between Ethiopia and Eritrea could threaten Red Sea shipping even without direct attacks on merchant vessels, according to an article in the Small Wars Journal. Eritrean troops allegedly crossed into Ethiopia's northern Tigray region and clashed with Ethiopian forces, raising the prospect of the first war between the two countries in more than 25 years.

The author warns that if fighting spreads toward Eritrea's port of Assab, military activity near the coast could increase war-risk costs and worsen the existing Houthi threat to the Bab el-Mandeb Strait. The paper urges the United States to use its ties with both governments to secure an Eritrean withdrawal from Tigray and keep Ethiopian forces away from Assab.

The article notes that Saudi Arabia is rerouting about 4 million barrels of oil a day through the Red Sea while Iran chokes the Strait of Hormuz, and that tankers must pass 600 miles of Eritrean coast before exiting Bab el-Mandeb. Weekly transits through the strait have fallen from about 38 to 25 since the Houthis renewed attacks in July.

Why it mattersShipping companies and insurers operating on the Red Sea route would face higher war-risk costs if fighting reaches the Eritrean coast, according to the article.

Yale student groups host second annual Ethiopian and Eritrean jazz evening

Students from Yale's Ethiopian and Eritrean association and its jazz collective spotlighted Habesha jazz traditions in a Davenport College common room event.

Student groups at Yale University held their second annual evening of jazz on Saturday, highlighting the music traditions of Ethiopia and Eritrea. Two campus groups, the Yale Ethiopian and Eritrean Students Association and the Yale Undergraduate Jazz Collective, staged the event in the Davenport College common room.

Liana Markos, president of the students association, said jazz runs deep in the Habesha community, the shared cultural identity of Ethiopians and Eritreans. Solomon Geleta, a member of the jazz collective's board, said the first evening was a big hit and that the groups wanted to highlight a lesser-known branch of the genre's history. The association handled booking the space and arranging food and drinks, while the collective promoted the event and provided the music.

The evening paired the jazz performance with an open mic where students shared spoken-word poetry and songs. Organizers hope to grow the event with a bigger venue and wider outreach beyond the Habesha students it has mainly targeted so far.

Why it mattersHabesha students and families at Yale and in the wider Yale community are the people directly involved in and drawn to this cultural event.
