Eritrea and Ethiopia trade drone and artillery strikes

Fighting near the disputed town of Badme has intensified, driving civilians from their homes.

Eritrean and Ethiopian forces have reportedly exchanged drone and artillery strikes near the disputed border town of Badme. The fighting has intensified over several days, driving civilians from their homes, according to diplomatic sources cited by local outlet Addis Standard on Saturday. Ethiopian forces reportedly deployed drones, while Eritrean troops used heavy artillery.

Two diplomats familiar with intelligence assessments told the Ethiopian outlet that the clashes had escalated. One source stated, “We are aware of drone strikes, particularly from the Ethiopian side, and the use of heavy artillery from the Eritrean side, and we are monitoring the situation.” A second source warned that the exchanges could lead to a major military confrontation between the neighboring countries.

Residents reported nighttime explosions, closed businesses, and families leaving their homes as Ethiopian federal troops increased their presence around Badme. The Armed Conflict Location & Event Data Project (ACLED) reported on November 13 that neither side controlled Badme. The renewed confrontation threatens to deepen instability in the Horn of Africa.

Eritrean American writer urges Eritrea and Ethiopia to learn from democracy

Semere T Habtemariam, an Eritrean American writer, argues that democratic institutions offer ways to manage the looming threat of war between the two countries.

Semere T Habtemariam, a writer for the Eritrean news site Awate, has argued in an essay published on 15 November 2026 that Eritrea and Ethiopia could draw lessons from the political histories of America and Britain. He says the looming threat of war is the most urgent issue facing Eritrea and that he will keep returning to it until it is resolved.

Habtemariam, who has taught American and Texas politics to college students and once ran for public office in the United States, says he had doubts about democracy after the re-election of President Trump. He writes that his faith was renewed by later political developments, which showed that voters can reassess their choices and hold leaders accountable through peaceful, constitutional means.

He argues that democracies can make mistakes without being trapped by them, because they contain the means to correct course. The essay presents this as the basis for his view that democratic institutions are among the most effective ways to manage conflict and bring about peaceful change.

Why it mattersEritrean readers at home and abroad are being offered a political framework for responding to the risk of war with Ethiopia, which the author calls the most urgent issue facing the country.
