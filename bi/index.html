Burundi farmers block road over fertiliser shortage

Residents of Kabanga barricaded the Rutana-Gihofi road with tree trunks to protest unequal fertiliser distribution.

Residents of Kabanga, Rutana province, blocked the Rutana-Gihofi road on October 30, 2026, using tree trunks to protest what they called unfair fertiliser distribution. The protest, organised by farmers who had not yet received chemical fertilisers from FOMI, an agricultural input company, temporarily halted traffic. Police and army units intervened to clear the road, which was only reopened after a fertiliser truck arrived.

The demonstration followed reports that neighbouring hills and surrounding areas had already received their fertiliser allocations. Farmers in Kabanga expressed anger that their hill had not been served. The situation escalated when local officials, including an agricultural monitor and a zone chief, were briefly held by angry residents before police intervention. Similar protests occurred on October 26 in Matongo, Butanyerera province, where residents blocked a road due to insufficient fertiliser supplies.

These repeated protests highlight tensions surrounding fertiliser distribution and farmers' difficulties accessing expected quantities. While farmers voice discontent, Burundi's authorities, speaking on national radio, have claimed widespread citizen satisfaction with the fertiliser distribution process. This official narrative contrasts with the protests recorded in Burunga and Butanyerera.

Why it mattersFarmers in Burundi are protesting the unequal distribution of essential fertilisers, impacting their ability to cultivate crops.

Burundi sends one referee to CAF continental matches

Pacifique Ndabihawenimana will represent Burundi as the sole referee in the upcoming CAF Champions League and Confederation Cup group stages.

Pacifique Ndabihawenimana is Burundi's only referee selected for the upcoming CAF Champions League and Confederation Cup group stages. 35 referees from 12 African nations will officiate these continental matches, starting November 18 in Cairo.

This selection is a consecration for Burundi's football officiating. Ndabihawenimana will not be involved with the Video Assistant Referee (VAR) system, which is being introduced to the group stages for the first time. He will officiate using only his eyesight.

Ndabihawenimana joins an elite group of 76 officials chosen by the Confederation of African Football. Burundi is among 12 countries whose referees will not use the VAR cabin, focusing instead on judging with the naked eye.

Why it mattersBurundian football fans will follow Pacifique Ndabihawenimana's performance in major continental matches.

Burundi agency director questioned over staff death

Révérien Nizigiyimana, head of the Development Agency of Burundi, was questioned by prosecutors about the death of Bruce Sebanza.

The director general of the Development Agency of Burundi (ADB), Révérien Nizigiyimana, was questioned on November 2, 2026, by the Ntahangwa prosecutor's office regarding the death of Bruce Sebanza, an agency official who died on October 13 in Bujumbura. Nizigiyimana appeared at the prosecutor's office accompanied by family members and left later that afternoon. Judicial sources have not yet released the conclusions of this hearing.

The questioning follows ongoing questions about the circumstances of Sebanza's death. One of two security guards suspected in the case has been arrested, while the other remains at large. The victim's family has raised concerns about the absence of the second guard in the ongoing proceedings. Witness accounts suggest distress calls were heard from ADB premises on the night of October 12-13.

Bruce Sebanza's death certificate, signed by the Gihosha zone chief, lists his place of death as "home," with a parenthetical clarification: "within the ADB premises." The director general is named as the declarant of the death, a detail that has also raised questions. Sebanza and Nizigiyimana had a prior conflict in 2025, leading to Sebanza's imprisonment following a complaint of signature imitation.

Why it mattersThe death of Bruce Sebanza and the subsequent questioning of the ADB director general raise concerns about the circumstances surrounding his demise and the integrity of the investigation.

Bonesha FM seeks 500 million francs to avoid closure

The independent radio station launched a 45-day fundraising campaign on October 30 to cover financial shortfalls.

Independent radio station Bonesha FM is facing financial collapse and needs 500 million Burundian francs to continue operating. Director Léon Masengo launched a 45-day fundraising campaign on October 30 to gather the necessary funds. The station, which resumed broadcasting after a six-year silence following the 2015 political crisis, is appealing to its listeners, former staff, partners, and the diaspora for support.

Masengo stated that the radio informs the public, promotes citizen debate, and broadcasts community interest programs, but economic difficulties hinder its ability to regularly broadcast and maintain services. He promised transparent communication regarding the collected sums and their allocation, acknowledging the significant amount sought and the importance of contributor trust for the campaign's success.

Bonesha FM, originally named Radio Umwizero meaning 'Hope', was established on February 19, 1996, with the aim of contributing to dialogue, reconciliation, and peace during a period of political and security crisis. It changed its name to Radio Sans Frontières Bonesha FM on January 29, 2001.

Why it mattersBonesha FM's financial struggles directly impact the public's access to independent information and community programming in Burundi.

Burundi faces food crisis from fuel and fertilizer shortages

El Niño and a lack of foreign currency worsen the impact of dwindling farm inputs on harvests.

Burundi is facing a growing threat to its food security due to fuel and fertilizer shortages, compounded by a lack of foreign currency and El Niño-related climate risks. As harvests decline and stocks dwindle, the price of beans is rising, raising concerns that rationing of agricultural inputs could lead to food rationing.

The country's economy, heavily reliant on agriculture which accounted for 34.3% of GDP in 2025, is struggling. 85 percent of Burundian households depend on farming, but farmers now lack essential fertilizers. "Without fertilizer, we can no longer sow and hope to get something," said a resident of Rwibaga commune, noting that past seasons B and C of 2026 have already seen weakened harvests. Residents of Matongo blocked the RN1 on October 26 to protest their dependence on these chemical products.

The combined effects of fuel and fertilizer rationing, alongside a chronic shortage of foreign currency and the El Niño climate phenomenon, threaten to create an unprecedented food crisis. The situation is exacerbated by the fact that Burundi's agriculture still relies on rudimentary, pre-industrial methods.

Why it mattersBurundian farmers are struggling to produce food due to shortages of essential fertilizers, impacting harvests and household livelihoods.
