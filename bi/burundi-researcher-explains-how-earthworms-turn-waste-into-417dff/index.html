Burundi researcher explains how earthworms turn waste into compost

Eric Kazitsa says vermicomposting uses farm and food-processing residues, while well-raised worms can produce more than 20 offspring a month.

Source: Iwacu · 2026-11-22 · 1 min read

Eric Kazitsa, a researcher and lecturer in natural sciences at Burundi’s Higher Teacher Training College, says vermicomposting uses earthworms and microorganisms to break down organic matter into compost. The process has three stages: pre-composting, active decomposition and maturation.

Kazitsa says agricultural and agro-processing residues can serve as the material, which is dried to raise its carbon content; cow dung can be added to increase nitrogen. Earthworms prefer acidity levels between 6 and 8, so acidic waste such as citrus should be avoided or treated to neutralise it. As worms consume and digest the material, they produce castings that mature into a stable, nutrient-rich compost ready for use.

Kazitsa says a worm can lay eggs two or three times a week. When raised well, it can produce more than 20 individuals by the end of a month.

Why it mattersFarmers with suitable crop or food-processing residues have guidance on making compost, and Kazitsa says a well-raised worm can produce more than 20 offspring monthly.
