Sierra Leone Vice Presidency Offers Little Path to Presidency

The office, constitutionally powerful, has historically served as a political cul-de-sac rather than a stepping stone to the presidency.

The Vice Presidency in Sierra Leone, though constitutionally powerful, has consistently failed to provide a clear path to the presidency for its occupants. Public policy analysts note that successive governments have used the office to balance regional, religious, or ethnic considerations, appease party blocs, neutralize rivals, or reward loyalty, rather than cultivate future leaders. This practice has often left Vice Presidents without independent political bases or clear succession pathways.

Historical examples illustrate this pattern, with figures like Sorie Ibrahim Koroma and Francis Minah serving under Siaka Stevens without presidential prospects. Solomon Ekuma Berewa, considered one of the most prepared Vice Presidents, lost decisively in the 2007 presidential election, demonstrating that competence in the role did not guarantee electoral success. The electorate's desire for change over continuity was cited as a key factor in his defeat.

More recent cases, such as Victor Bockarie Foh's brief and constrained tenure, and Samuel Sam-Sumana's estrangement and expulsion from his party after being elected Vice President, further highlight the precariousness of the office. These instances suggest that proximity to power does not equate to political security or a guaranteed future leadership role for the Vice President.

Why it mattersThe Vice Presidency's lack of a clear succession pathway and its use as a political balancing tool affects current and future Vice Presidents by limiting their opportunities for higher office.
