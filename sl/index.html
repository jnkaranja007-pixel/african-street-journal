Charcoal burning degrades environment, threatens food security

Sierra Leone's reliance on charcoal for cooking, due to limited electricity access, is driving deforestation and greenhouse gas emissions.

The widespread practice of charcoal burning in Sierra Leone, particularly in Freetown, is leading to significant deforestation and environmental degradation. With only 36% of the population having access to electricity and 15% to clean cooking, charcoal remains a primary energy source for many households. This demand necessitates the cutting of trees, including valuable economic species like orange and mango, leaving land barren and vulnerable to climate change.

Experts from the Environmental Protection Agency acknowledge the severe environmental hazards, noting that the slow regrowth of trees exacerbates the problem. The burning process itself releases substantial greenhouse gases, contributing to air pollution and human health risks. The Food and Agricultural Organization estimates that traditional wood energy accounts for 2-7% of global anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions.

This environmental strain directly impacts food security as economic trees are destroyed and land is degraded. The Director of Natural Resource Governance highlighted that the continued unsustainable production and use of charcoal could worsen climate change, further affecting forest productivity and future wood-energy supplies.

Why it mattersFarmers are affected as the destruction of economic trees and land degradation threaten food security and agricultural productivity.

Sierra Leone Vice Presidency Offers Little Path to Presidency

The office, constitutionally powerful, has historically served as a political cul-de-sac rather than a stepping stone to the presidency.

The Vice Presidency in Sierra Leone, though constitutionally powerful, has consistently failed to provide a clear path to the presidency for its occupants. Public policy analysts note that successive governments have used the office to balance regional, religious, or ethnic considerations, appease party blocs, neutralize rivals, or reward loyalty, rather than cultivate future leaders. This practice has often left Vice Presidents without independent political bases or clear succession pathways.

Historical examples illustrate this pattern, with figures like Sorie Ibrahim Koroma and Francis Minah serving under Siaka Stevens without presidential prospects. Solomon Ekuma Berewa, considered one of the most prepared Vice Presidents, lost decisively in the 2007 presidential election, demonstrating that competence in the role did not guarantee electoral success. The electorate's desire for change over continuity was cited as a key factor in his defeat.

More recent cases, such as Victor Bockarie Foh's brief and constrained tenure, and Samuel Sam-Sumana's estrangement and expulsion from his party after being elected Vice President, further highlight the precariousness of the office. These instances suggest that proximity to power does not equate to political security or a guaranteed future leadership role for the Vice President.

Why it mattersThe Vice Presidency's lack of a clear succession pathway and its use as a political balancing tool affects current and future Vice Presidents by limiting their opportunities for higher office.

Artisanal miners in Kono face systemic sexual violence at diamond and gold sites

Women scavenging in abandoned mining pits in Sierra Leone report frequent sexual assaults and a lack of accountability from local camp authorities.

Female artisanal miners in the diamond-rich district of Kono are reporting high levels of sexual violence while working in abandoned pits. Janet, a 32-year-old miner, recounted being raped at age 15 while scavenging for gold and diamonds in Jaiama Nimikor. She stated that she reported the incident to the village head, but the perpetrator remained unidentified and she subsequently faced community stigmatization.

Other women working in the sector describe similar experiences of harassment and assault. Haja, a 48-year-old widow, reported being raped while working in the mines. She claimed that when she reported the assault, the camp master dismissed her allegations, suggesting the attacker was her partner. Haja stated that she fled her community due to shame before eventually returning at her daughter's request.

These women turned to artisanal mining as a primary source of income following the civil war or the death of spouses. Many scavenge in pits to support their families after dropping out of school or losing other means of survival. Despite the risks, these mining sites remain a critical, albeit dangerous, economic lifeline for vulnerable residents in the region.

Why it mattersWomen working in artisanal mining sites in Kono face physical danger and sexual violence while attempting to earn an income through scavenging.
