Charcoal burning deforests Sierra Leone, threatens food security

Environmental officials acknowledge limited alternatives as the practice of burning wood for charcoal accelerates deforestation and greenhouse gas emissions.

Charcoal burning, a common practice across Sierra Leone, is leading to significant deforestation and environmental degradation, according to the Environmental Protection Agency. The process, which involves cutting down trees, including valuable fruit-bearing ones like mango and orange, emits greenhouse gases and contributes to habitat loss. Director of Natural Resource Governance, Paul Lamin, stated that the practice leaves land treeless and vulnerable to climate change, while also threatening food security.

The demand for charcoal is high, especially in Freetown and other regional towns, because only 36% of the population has access to electricity and a mere 15% have access to clean cooking. This lack of alternative energy sources forces many households, particularly those with lower incomes, to rely on charcoal despite its environmental impact. Experts warn that the burning of wood for charcoal and its subsequent use as fuel releases substantial greenhouse gases, harming both human health and the environment.

The Food and Agricultural Organization estimates that traditional wood energy, including charcoal, emits 2.4 GT of carbon dioxide equivalent annually, representing 2-7% of global anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions. The agency warns that continued unsustainable charcoal production and use, driven by increasing demand, will exacerbate climate change. This, in turn, could negatively impact forests and woodlands, reducing future wood-energy supplies.

Why it mattersFarmers are affected as the cutting of economic trees and deforestation threatens food security and exposes land to climate change effects.

Sierra Leone Vice Presidents Face Political Dead End

The constitution grants significant power to the vice presidency, but historical precedent shows it rarely leads to the top office.

The office of Vice President in Sierra Leone, constitutionally second only to the president, has historically served as a political cul-de-sac rather than a pathway to leadership. Analysis suggests that vice presidents are often appointed to balance regional, religious, or ethnic considerations, appease party factions, neutralize rivals, or reward loyalty, rather than to cultivate future presidential ambitions. This practice frequently leaves vice presidents without a clear succession plan or an independent political base upon entering office.

Post-independence history provides numerous examples of this trend. Vice presidents like Sorie Ibrahim Koroma and Francis Minah were powerful figures but not groomed for succession, with Minah's case illustrating the dangers of proximity to power without political insulation. Solomon Ekuma Berewa, despite being well-prepared and involved in governance, lost the 2007 presidential election, demonstrating that competence in the vice presidential role does not guarantee electoral success.

More recent examples, such as Victor Bockarie Foh's brief and politically constrained tenure, and Samuel Sam-Sumana's experience of being estranged, expelled, and politically orphaned after his election, further underscore the precarious nature of the vice presidency. These instances collectively suggest that the office, while constitutionally significant, often fails to provide political security or a viable route to the presidency for its occupants.

Why it mattersThe vice presidency in Sierra Leone is a political liability for its occupants, as historical patterns show they are rarely groomed for succession and often face political insecurity.

Women Face Rape and Harassment in Sierra Leone Mines

Artisanal miners in Kono district recount sexual violence and exploitation as they seek income from abandoned sites.

Women in Sierra Leone's Kono district are facing severe sexual violence, including rape and harassment, while working in abandoned mining sites. Janet, 32, shared her experience of being raped at 15 while scavenging for diamonds and gold in Jaiama Nimikor. She became pregnant and later stigmatized, with a relative raising her child. For many women, including widows like Haja, 48, mining became a primary source of income after the civil war and the death of their husbands, forcing them into dangerous environments.

Haja fled her community after being raped by a man in the village, leaving her children behind due to shame. She reported the incident, but the camp master dismissed her claim, suggesting the perpetrator was her boyfriend. Haja eventually returned after her daughter's plea. These accounts highlight the pervasive dangers and lack of recourse for women in artisanal mining, where they often work alone or in precarious conditions.

The pursuit of income from abandoned mines in the diamond-rich Kono district, a consequence of the civil war, has exposed women to exploitation. Many, like Janet, dropped out of school due to poverty and relied on scavenging in old pits. The trauma of sexual assault and its aftermath, including unwanted pregnancies and community stigma, has had a lasting impact on their lives and livelihoods.

Why it mattersWomen miners in Sierra Leone are affected by rape and harassment in mining sites, which are their primary source of income.

SLEWRC revenue jumps to NLE 7.4 million

The Sierra Leone Electricity and Water Regulatory Commission increased internal revenue from NLE 2.3 million in 2023 to NLE 7.4 million in 2024 through levies and licenses.

The Sierra Leone Electricity and Water Regulatory Commission (SLEWRC) reported a significant increase in its internal revenue generation, reaching NLE 7.4 million in 2024. This figure represents a substantial rise from the NLE 2.3 million collected in 2023, primarily from levies and licenses. Despite not receiving budgeted funds from the Ministry of Finance for operational expenditures in both 2023 and 2024, the commission successfully completed key deliverables.

These achievements included sanitizing the packaged water industry through enforcement actions that led to increased licensing and improved service quality. The SLEWRC also consistently published performance monitoring reports, resolving an average of 80% of complaints within a week. Enforcement actions were taken against 64 non-compliant water companies, and efforts were made to expand the reach to more electricity and water sector operators.

For the upcoming fiscal year 2025, the SLEWRC plans to strengthen its organizational capacity, ensure market entry and universal access, and uphold international quality of service standards. The commission also intends to introduce a safety and warranty batching system for water products and monitor tariff rate implementation to foster sustainability. A budget of NLE 5,590.29 has been submitted for FY 2025 to 2027.

Why it mattersInvestors are affected by the increase in revenue generation and the commission's plans to monitor tariff rates, which could impact operational costs and profitability.
