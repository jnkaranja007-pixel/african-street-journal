Sierra Leone charcoal burning causes deforestation

The practice of burning wood for charcoal is destroying economic trees and threatening food security, officials say.

Charcoal burning is a common source of livelihood in Sierra Leone, particularly in Freetown, as only 36% of the population has access to electricity and 15% to clean cooking.

The Environmental Protection Agency acknowledged the practice's hazards, noting that burners are cutting down fruit trees like orange and mango, leaving land exposed to climate change and threatening food security.

Greenhouse gas emissions from charcoal production and use contribute to climate change, which could reduce future wood supplies, according to the Food and Agricultural Organization.

Why it mattersSierra Leone's population relies on charcoal for cooking due to limited access to electricity and clean energy alternatives, leading to environmental damage.

Sierra Leone students face withheld exam results

One in four candidates had results withheld for suspected malpractice, fewer than one in 10 passed English.

Sierra Leone's 2026 West African Senior School Certificate Examination (WASSCE) results show that 47,454 candidates had their results withheld for suspected malpractice. The Minister of Basic and Senior Secondary Education, Mr Conrad Sackey, announced the results on Tuesday, October 27, 2026.

The Justice Reform Initiative-SL (JRI-SL) has condemned the mass withholding of results, calling it a threat to students' academic futures and university admission chances. The organisation gave the West African Examinations Council (WAEC) and the Ministry of Basic and Senior Secondary Education (MBSSE) a 21-day ultimatum to address the issue or face legal action.

JRI-SL urged WAEC and MBSSE to establish a transparent verification process for alleged infractions and release results for candidates without direct evidence of cheating. The organisation also called for a national dialogue on recurring concerns about student performance in English Language.

Why it mattersThousands of Sierra Leonean students face uncertainty regarding their academic progression and university admissions due to withheld examination results.

Politics · 2026-11-01

Sierra Leone vice presidents fail to reach presidency

The constitution grants power, but political appointments sideline vice presidents from leadership.

In Sierra Leone, the Vice Presidency, constitutionally powerful, has historically served as a political cul-de-sac. Since independence, no Vice President has been elected President, raising questions about the current officeholder's fate. Vice Presidents are often appointed to balance regional, religious, or ethnic considerations, appease party blocs, neutralize rivals, or reward loyalty, rather than cultivate ambition.

This practice means most Vice Presidents enter office without a clear succession pathway or an independent political base. Examples include Sorie Ibrahim Koroma and Francis Minah under Siaka Stevens, and Solomon Ekuma Berewa, who lost the 2007 presidential election despite extensive experience. Victor Bockarie Foh's short tenure and Samuel Sam-Sumana's expulsion from his party also illustrate the office's political precarity.

The 1991 Constitution intended the Vice Presidency to ensure continuity and executive depth. However, successive governments have treated it as a political balancing mechanism. This has led to a pattern where proximity to power does not translate into political security or a viable path to the presidency.

Why it mattersThe Vice Presidency in Sierra Leone has been a political dead-end, with no holder ever elected president, suggesting a pattern that affects the current Vice President.

Health · 2026-11-01

NPHA assesses airport health security

The National Public Health Agency visited Freetown International Airport to boost surveillance and preparedness.

The National Public Health Agency (NPHA) conducted a health preparedness assessment at Freetown International Airport on Friday, October 30, 2026. The visit to the airport in Lungi aimed to strengthen public health surveillance, screening, and emergency readiness at Sierra Leone's primary international gateway.

During the assessment, the NPHA team reviewed Port Health operations and the Sierra Leone Health Travel Portal. They identified potential gaps and discussed improvements with airport stakeholders to enhance border health security and the detection of public health threats.

This initiative is part of the NPHA's ongoing work to improve health surveillance at entry points and protect travellers and the wider population.

Why it mattersThe NPHA's assessment at Freetown International Airport aims to improve health surveillance and preparedness for travellers and the wider population.

Female artisanal miners report sexual violence in Kono district mines

Women working in diamond-rich pits in Jaiama Nimikor face systemic rape and harassment while struggling to earn a living in Sierra Leone.

Women scavenging for gold and diamonds in the Jaiama Nimikor mining area of Kono district report frequent incidents of rape and sexual harassment. Janet, a 32-year-old miner, stated she was raped at age 15 while working alone in a pit eight miles from Koidu. She became pregnant following the assault and faced community stigmatization, eventually losing custody of her child to a relative.

Haja, a 48-year-old widow and mother of three, said she was also raped while working in the mines. She reported the assault to a camp master, who dismissed her claim by alleging the attacker was her boyfriend. Haja fled to the village of Guitor to escape the shame of the incident, leaving her children behind before eventually returning to her community.

Many women turned to artisanal mining to support their families following the end of the civil war in Sierra Leone. These miners often work in abandoned pits to find gold or diamonds after dropping out of school or losing their spouses. Despite reporting these crimes to village authorities and camp officials, victims say they have received little to no support or justice for the violence they endure.

Why it mattersThe report details the specific risks of sexual violence faced by women working in the artisanal mining sector in Kono district.
