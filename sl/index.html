Sierra Leone president warns kush sellers to stop

President Bio visits a drug rehabilitation facility and urges parents to find honest work.

President Julius Maada Bio has ordered sellers of the synthetic drug Kush to cease their illicit trade, which is causing widespread harm in Sierra Leone. During a visit to the Government’s Harmful Drug Rehabilitation Facility in Hastings on October 30, 2025, he assured recovering users of his administration’s commitment to stopping the sale of dangerous substances. He specifically implored parents involved in selling Kush to stop immediately and seek legitimate livelihoods.

The president addressed drug users at the rehab, stating that their future is essential for Sierra Leone’s development and that the government is working to break the drug supply chain. He condemned those producing and selling Kush, a drug linked to the deaths of many young people since 2019. Bio highlighted the establishment of rehabilitation centers in Hastings, Gondama, and Daru, with plans for another in Teko, Makeni.

Parents shared personal struggles, including Elizabeth Ansumana whose lawyer son became addicted to Kush and required government treatment. A former user, Margaret Jawara, now an ambassador against drug use, credited the Hastings facility and the Ministry of Social Welfare for her recovery. Minister of Social Welfare, Melrose Karminty, reported successful family reintegration for many victims. President Bio also launched a Presidential Task Force on Drugs and Substance Abuse.

Why it mattersYoung people and their families in Sierra Leone are directly affected by the pervasive use and sale of the dangerous drug Kush.

Sierra Leone charcoal burning causes deforestation

Burning wood for charcoal is destroying economic trees and leaving land exposed to climate change effects.

Charcoal burning, a common practice in Sierra Leone for cooking fuel, is leading to widespread deforestation and environmental degradation. 65-year-old Pa Alimamy, a charcoal burner for over two decades, stated his family relies on this trade for survival, highlighting its prevalence in Freetown and other towns due to limited access to electricity and clean cooking options. The National Energy Compact reports only 36% of the population has electricity and 15% clean cooking access.

The process of making charcoal involves cutting down trees, including valuable economic species like orange and mango, which exacerbates environmental hazards. Paul Lamin from the Environmental Protection Agency acknowledged the practice's negative impact, noting that the slow regrowth of trees reduces the land's capacity to absorb carbon. He also admitted that viable alternative energy sources are currently scarce for the population.

Experts warn that greenhouse gas emissions from charcoal production and use harm human health and the environment. A Food and Agricultural Organization report estimates traditional wood energy emits 2.4 GT of carbon dioxide equivalent annually, with Sub-Saharan Africa contributing a third of these emissions. Continued unsustainable charcoal practices are expected to worsen climate change, potentially reducing future wood-energy supplies.

Why it mattersDeforestation and greenhouse gas emissions from charcoal burning threaten the environment and food security for Sierra Leoneans.

Sierra Leone seeks Russian mining expertise

Minister Mattai wants Russian help with exploration, processing and training to boost mineral sector.

Sierra Leone is seeking Russian expertise to improve its mineral exploration and processing capabilities. Minister of Mines, Julius Daniel Mattai, spoke at the Russia-Africa Summit in Moscow on Wednesday, October 28, 2026, calling for cooperation beyond raw material export. The country holds iron ore, diamonds, lithium, cobalt, nickel, and coltan.

Mattai stated that Sierra Leone needs better geological surveys, drilling, and modern geophysical investigations to understand its mineral deposits. He highlighted a June 2024 memorandum of understanding on mineral resource inventory for attracting investment. The minister also stressed the importance of local processing and manufacturing to create jobs and retain value, which requires infrastructure like electricity, roads, and ports.

The minister expressed interest in partnerships with Russian universities for scholarships, academic exchanges, and laboratory development in fields like geology and mining engineering. Cooperation on artificial intelligence for geological modelling and energy development to support industrial activities was also proposed.

Why it mattersSierra Leone aims to increase its economic benefits from mineral wealth through enhanced exploration and local processing.

Female artisanal miners report sexual violence in Kono district

Women working in the diamond-rich mines of Jaiama Nimikor face persistent threats of rape and harassment while scavenging for gold and diamonds.

Women working in the diamond-rich mines of Jaiama Nimikor in Sierra Leone's Kono district report frequent sexual violence while scavenging for minerals. Janet, a 32-year-old miner, recounted being raped at age 15 while working alone in the pits. She stated that she reported the assault to her village head at the time, but the perpetrator remained unidentified.

The violence has forced many women to flee their homes or face social stigma. Haja, a 48-year-old widow, said she was raped while working in the mines and subsequently fled to the village of Guitor. She reported the incident to a camp master, who dismissed her claim by suggesting the attacker was her partner. Haja stated that the man who assaulted her later left the village voluntarily.

Many women turned to artisanal mining as a primary source of income following the civil war. Janet, who dropped out of school as a teenager, continues to work in the sector to support her child. Other female miners in the area confirmed experiencing similar incidents of sexual harassment and assault while working in the pits.

Why it mattersWomen in the Kono district face physical danger and sexual violence while working in abandoned mining pits to support their families.

Sierra Leone secures $60.97 million for immunisation

Gavi funding will expand malaria and hepatitis B vaccines through 2030.

Sierra Leone has secured US$60.97 million from Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance, to boost childhood vaccinations until 2030. This funding under Gavi's 6.0 programme will support routine immunisation, including malaria vaccination and the hepatitis B birth-dose vaccine.

The Ministry of Health, led by Minister Dr Austin Demby, will use the funds to expand malaria vaccination to Western Area Urban, aiming to protect an additional 40,000 children annually. The hepatitis B vaccine will be given to infants within 24 hours of birth to prevent chronic infection.

The government has committed to meeting its co-financing obligations to sustain the programme. Sierra Leone has maintained routine vaccination coverage above 90% for the past five years.

Why it mattersChildren in Sierra Leone will receive expanded protection against malaria and hepatitis B through new vaccination programmes funded until 2030.
