Sierra Leone charcoal burners cut down fruit trees, EPA official says

Only 36% of Sierra Leoneans have access to electricity and just 15% have clean cooking, according to the government's National Energy Compact.

Pa Alimamy, a 65-year-old charcoal burner, has worked the trade for more than two decades and says his whole family depends on it. Charcoal is common across Sierra Leone, especially in Freetown and other regional headquarter towns, as demand grows against other cooking options.

Paul Lamin, Director of Natural Resource Governance at the Environmental Protection Agency, told Politico SL that burners now cut down economic trees such as orange and mango, leaving land bare and exposed to climate change and threatening food security. He said the trees take a long time to regrow and help reduce carbon, and admitted that people have limited alternative energy options.

A Food and Agriculture Organization report estimates that traditional wood energy, including fuelwood and charcoal, emits 1 to 2.4 gigatonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent a year, or 2 to 7% of total human-caused greenhouse gas emissions, with sub-Saharan Africa accounting for a third of that from wood energy. It warns that continued unsuitable charcoal production could worsen climate change and reduce future wood-energy supplies.

Why it mattersCharcoal burners who cut down orange and mango trees leave land exposed to climate change and threaten the food supply of the families who depend on it.

Female artisanal miners in Kono report systemic sexual violence

Women working in abandoned diamond and gold pits in Jaiama Nimikor face persistent threats of rape and harassment while seeking a livelihood.

Women scavenging for minerals in the diamond-rich district of Kono, Sierra Leone, are facing frequent sexual violence while working in abandoned mining pits. Janet, a 32-year-old miner, reported being raped at age 15 while searching for gold and diamonds in Jaiama Nimikor, a site located 8 miles from Koidu. She stated that she became pregnant following the assault and faced community stigmatization, forcing her to give up her child to a relative.

Another miner, 48-year-old Haja, described fleeing her community to Guitor after being raped while working in the mines. Haja said she reported the incident to the camp master, who dismissed her claim by alleging the attacker was her boyfriend. She noted that the perpetrator eventually left the village voluntarily, but she faced significant social shame and was separated from her three children during her displacement.

Many women turned to artisanal mining as a primary income source following the end of the civil war. These miners often work alone in remote pits to support their families after losing guardians or spouses. Despite reporting these incidents to village authorities, the women stated that they received little support or justice for the attacks.

Why it mattersWomen working in artisanal mines in Kono face ongoing sexual violence and lack institutional protection, forcing some to flee their homes.

Civil society group demands dismissal of Sierra Leone's finance minister

A coalition calling itself the CSOs Consortium on Public Accountability says Finance Minister Sheku Ahmed Fantamadi Bangura should be removed immediately.

The CSOs Consortium on Public Accountability has called for the immediate dismissal of Sierra Leone's Minister of Finance, Sheku Ahmed Fantamadi Bangura. Foday M. Daboh, writing as a guest columnist for Politico SL, argues the call confuses economic hardship with economic mismanagement.

Daboh points to overlapping global shocks between 2020 and 2024, including COVID-19 supply-chain disruptions and food and fuel inflation linked to the Russia-Ukraine war. He says Sierra Leone did not default on its external debt, unlike Ghana, while Zambia entered debt restructuring and Nigeria's naira depreciated sharply.

On inflation, Daboh says the Consortium ignores the trend in Bank of Sierra Leone monetary policy statements, which show a decline after coordinated fiscal and monetary tightening. He cites the IMF's view that reduced central bank financing of deficits has moderated inflation in an economy importing over 70 percent of its consumption basket.

Why it mattersIf the minister were removed, the debt, inflation and financing choices he is credited with would pass to a new team during a period when the source says the country remains at high risk of debt distress.

Sierra Leone's vice presidency has never led to the presidency

Analyst Foday M. Daboh argues the office has repeatedly failed as a route to power, and questions whether the current vice president is already too late.

No vice president in Sierra Leone has ever been elected president, according to political economist Foday M. Daboh, writing in Politico SL. He describes the office as constitutionally powerful, second only to the presidency, yet often a cul-de-sac rather than a route to higher leadership.

Daboh says successive governments have appointed vice presidents to balance regional, religious or ethnic interests, appease party blocs, neutralise rivals or reward loyalty, rather than to groom successors. Solomon Ekuma Berewa, seen as the most prepared vice president the country has produced, lost the 2007 presidential race to Ernest Bai Koroma. Samuel Sam-Sumana was later expelled from his party after a falling-out with the president.

Daboh asks whether the pattern has already sealed the fate of the current vice president, a question his article raises but does not answer in full.

Why it mattersVice presidents in Sierra Leone enter office without a clear succession path or an independent political base, as the analysis reports.

Sierra Leone seeks $1 billion AU Peace Fund

The nation also proposed a unified programme-based budget and a 0.2 percent import levy for member states.

Sierra Leone has urged the African Union (AU) to establish a $1 billion endowment for its Peace Fund and adopt a unified programme-based budget. The proposals were presented by the Sierra Leonean delegation at the 27th Extraordinary Session of the AU Executive Council in Addis Ababa.

The delegation, led by Alan C.E. Logan, Director General of the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and International Cooperation, called for stricter compliance and transparency from member states regarding contributions. They also recommended a firm timeline for implementing the 0.2 percent import levy and indexing contribution ceilings to inflation.

The AU session, themed “Towards a Financially Sustainable and Self-Reliant African Union,” focused on achieving long-term financial independence for AU programmes and peace operations. Sierra Leone stated its readiness to contribute to the reform process and uphold expected standards.

Why it mattersSierra Leone's call for a $1 billion AU Peace Fund and a 0.2 percent import levy impacts how continental security will be financed.
