Charcoal burning deforests Sierra Leone, threatens food security

Environmental officials warn that the widespread practice of charcoal production is accelerating deforestation, destroying economic trees, and contributing to climate change.

Charcoal production, a primary energy source for many Sierra Leoneans due to limited electricity access, is driving significant deforestation and environmental degradation. The process involves cutting down trees, including valuable fruit-bearing species like oranges and mangoes, leaving land barren and vulnerable to climate change impacts. This practice not only contributes to greenhouse gas emissions but also threatens biodiversity and food security across the nation.

The Environmental Protection Agency acknowledges the severe environmental hazards posed by charcoal burning. Director Paul Lamin stated that the trees cut for charcoal take a long time to regrow and are essential for absorbing carbon dioxide. With only 36% of the population having electricity access and 15% clean cooking access, alternative energy sources remain critically limited, perpetuating the reliance on charcoal.

Experts warn that the continued demand for charcoal, coupled with unsustainable production methods, will exacerbate climate change. This, in turn, could negatively affect forest health and productivity, potentially reducing future wood-energy supplies. Traditional wood energy, including charcoal, is estimated to emit 2.4 GT of carbon dioxide equivalent annually, contributing significantly to global greenhouse gas emissions.

Why it mattersFarmers are affected as the destruction of economic trees and land degradation threaten food security and agricultural productivity.

Sierra Leone Vice Presidents Fail to Reach Presidency

The office, constitutionally powerful, has historically served as a political cul-de-sac rather than a pathway to the presidency, leaving current and past vice presidents without.

The Vice Presidency in Sierra Leone, though constitutionally second only to the president, has consistently failed to produce a successor. Analysis suggests the office is often used for political balancing, regional appeasement, or neutralizing rivals, rather than cultivating future leadership. This practice has resulted in vice presidents lacking independent political bases and clear succession pathways, hindering their presidential ambitions.

Historical examples illustrate this pattern. Vice presidents like Sorie Ibrahim Koroma and Francis Minah under Siaka Stevens were insiders but not groomed for succession, with Minah facing execution. Solomon Ekuma Berewa, considered well-prepared, lost the 2007 election, demonstrating that competence in office did not guarantee electoral success. Victor Bockarie Foh's brief tenure and Samuel Sam-Sumana's estrangement further highlight the office's political vulnerability.

The current Vice President faces a similar historical trajectory, with the office's design and political utilization creating a recurring obstacle to presidential aspirations. The evidence suggests that the Vice Presidency, rather than being a stepping stone, has often been a political liability, as seen in past elections where voters opted for perceived change over continuity.

Why it mattersThe historical pattern of Vice Presidents in Sierra Leone not becoming President directly affects current and future Vice Presidents by limiting their perceived political viability and succession prospects.

Women Face Rape and Harassment in Sierra Leone Mines

Artisanal miners in Kono district report sexual violence and lack of recourse, impacting their livelihoods and safety.

Women in Sierra Leone's Kono district are enduring rape and sexual harassment while scavenging for diamonds and gold in abandoned mine sites. Janet, 32, recounted being raped at 15 in a mine, an experience that led to an unwanted pregnancy and community stigma. She continues to mine to support her child, despite the risks.

Haja, 48, a widow and mother of three, fled her village after being raped by a man in her community. She had turned to gold mining for income after her husband's death. When she reported the incident, the mine camp master dismissed her claims, suggesting the perpetrator was her boyfriend.

These women often turn to mining as one of the few available income sources following the civil war, especially after losing parents. Many have dropped out of school due to poverty, making mining, even in dangerous, abandoned pits, a necessity for survival and to provide for their families.

Why it mattersWomen engaged in artisanal mining for income are subjected to rape and sexual harassment, impacting their safety and economic stability.

Finance Minister's Actions Defended Against Civil Society Criticism

A civil society group's call for the finance minister's dismissal is based on a misunderstanding of global economic shocks versus local policy, according to an analysis.

A statement by the "CSOs Consortium on Public Accountability" demanding the dismissal of Finance Minister Sheku Ahmed Fantamadi Bangura has been characterized as a flawed intervention. The analysis argues the consortium confuses economic hardship with mismanagement and populist frustration with policy failure. It asserts that while civil society has a vital role, its advocacy requires analytical rigor and empirical grounding, which it claims are absent in this instance.

The defense highlights that between 2020 and 2024, Sierra Leone navigated unprecedented global shocks, including COVID-19 supply chain issues, the Russia-Ukraine war's impact on food and fuel prices, and aggressive global monetary tightening. Despite these challenges, the country avoided external debt default, maintained its IMF program, and preserved access to concessional financing, outcomes attributed to disciplined macro-fiscal management.

Regarding inflation, the analysis points to Bank of Sierra Leone data showing inflation peaked due to global shocks but subsequently declined through coordinated fiscal and monetary tightening, with central bank financing of deficits curtailed. IMF reports acknowledge improved fiscal discipline and reduced central bank financing as factors moderating inflationary pressures in an economy that imports over 70 percent of its consumption.

Why it mattersInvestors are affected by the defense of the finance minister's management, which suggests continued fiscal discipline and stability, potentially influencing investment decisions.

SLEWRC Revenue Jumps to NLE 7.4 Million

The Sierra Leone Electricity and Water Regulatory Commission reports a significant increase in internal revenue from levies and licenses, despite receiving no funds.

The Sierra Leone Electricity and Water Regulatory Commission (SLEWRC) generated NLE 7.4 million in internal revenue from levies and licenses in 2024, a substantial rise from the NLE 2.3 million collected in 2023. This increase was achieved through robust monitoring and enforcement actions, particularly in the packaged water industry, leading to more licenses and improved quality of service. The commission also resolved an average of eighty percent of complaints within a week.

Despite not receiving budgeted funds for recurrent expenditures from the Ministry of Finance in both FY 2023 and FY 2024, SLEWRC completed key deliverables. These included sanitizing the packaged water industry, publishing quarterly performance reports, and taking enforcement actions against sixty-four non-compliant water companies. For FY 2024, the commission also expanded its operational network and developed a Multi-Year Tariff Order model for solar mini-grids.

Looking ahead to FY 2025, SLEWRC plans to strengthen its organizational capacity, ensure market entry and universal access, and uphold international quality of service standards. The commission also intends to roll out a safety and warranty batching system for water products and monitor tariff rate implementation. A budget of NLE 5,590.29 for FY 2025 to 2027 was submitted.

Why it mattersInvestors are affected by the increased revenue generation and the commission's plans to monitor tariff rates, which could impact the profitability of electricity and water service providers.
