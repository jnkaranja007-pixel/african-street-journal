Climate · 2026-11-11

Sierra Leone charcoal burning causes deforestation

The practice of burning wood for charcoal is depleting forests and harming the environment, according to the Environmental Protection Agency.

Charcoal burning, a common practice in Sierra Leone for cooking fuel, is leading to widespread deforestation and environmental degradation. Many families, like that of 65-year-old Pa Alimamy, rely on this trade for their survival, as only 36% of the population has access to electricity and 15% to clean cooking.

The Environmental Protection Agency acknowledged the hazards, noting that burners are cutting down economic trees like orange and mango. Director of Natural Resource Governance Paul Lamin stated that this deforestation leaves land exposed to climate change effects and threatens food security, as trees take a long time to regrow and absorb carbon.

Greenhouse gas emissions from charcoal production and use contribute to climate change, with Sub-Saharan Africa accounting for one-third of global emissions from wood energy. Experts warn that continued unsustainable practices will worsen climate change, impacting forest health and future wood-energy supplies.

Why it mattersDeforestation and greenhouse gas emissions from charcoal burning threaten Sierra Leone's environment and food security.

Sierra Leone teachers face discipline for census training

The Teaching Service Commission warns educators abandoning classrooms for census work will face action.

Teachers in Sierra Leone's Koinadugu and Falaba districts have abandoned classrooms for census training, prompting a warning of disciplinary action from the Teaching Service Commission (TSC). School heads reported teacher absenteeism during the 12-day training for census enumerators and supervisors, leading the TSC to deploy monitoring teams. Deputy Director Paul Issa Kamara stated that absent teachers must return to their posts or face serious consequences under professional regulations.

Rev. David Y. Marah, Education Secretary for MCA Schools in Falaba District, expressed concern that these absences could disrupt school administration and negatively impact pupils' academic performance, especially in remote areas with limited educational support. The TSC acknowledged the role of community teachers, who often supplement government staff in underserved regions, and encouraged their continued support for schools during the census training period.

This exodus follows a 2022 incident where the TSC suspended salaries for 169 teachers in Bombali District for similar unauthorized absences. The National Teacher Policy outlines measures like salary withholding, suspension, and dismissal for code of conduct breaches. The TSC is appealing for cooperation from civil society, education officers, and communities to ensure census activities do not undermine pupils' right to education.

Why it mattersPupils in Koinadugu and Falaba districts risk disrupted education as teachers prioritize census training over classroom instruction.

Politics · 2026-11-11

Civil society group demands Sierra Leone finance minister's sacking

The CSOs Consortium on Public Accountability wants Finance Minister Sheku Ahmed Fantamadi Bangura removed immediately, a call a Politico SL guest writer says is misplaced.

The CSOs Consortium on Public Accountability has called for the immediate dismissal of Sierra Leone's Minister of Finance, Sheku Ahmed Fantamadi Bangura. A Politico SL guest writer, Foday M. Daboh, argues the statement confuses economic hardship with mismanagement.

Daboh says the 2020 to 2024 period brought COVID-19 supply disruptions, food and fuel inflation linked to the Russia-Ukraine war, global monetary tightening and capital flight from low-income economies. He points to Ghana's external debt default in 2022, Zambia's protracted restructuring and Nigeria's naira depreciation, while Sierra Leone did not default and kept its IMF programme.

He credits the minister with disciplined macro-fiscal management, citing Bank of Sierra Leone statements that inflation fell after tightening and central bank deficit financing was curtailed. He also says the country has no Eurobond exposure, its debt is mostly concessional, and it remains at high risk of debt distress.

Why it mattersSierra Leone's borrowers and lenders hold their view of the government's debt position in the balance, since the packet says the country remains at high risk of debt distress despite its concessional borrowing.

Politics · 2026-11-11

Sierra Leone vice presidents have never won the presidency

An analyst says the office has repeatedly served as a political dead end, and asks whether the current Vice President's path is already closed.

Foday M. Daboh, a public policy analyst and political economist, argues that the Vice Presidency in Sierra Leone has rarely led to the top job. He writes that no Vice President has ever been elected President, even though the 1991 Constitution makes the office second only to the presidency.

Daboh says successive governments have used the post to balance regional, religious or ethnic interests, appease party blocs, neutralise rivals and reward loyalty, rather than to groom successors. He cites Solomon Ekuma Berewa, widely seen as the best-prepared Vice President, who lost the 2007 SLPP presidential race to Ernest Bai Koroma.

Daboh also points to Victor Bockarie Foh, who served briefly after Samuel Sam-Sumana was removed, and to Sam-Sumana himself, who was later expelled from the party. He asks whether the pattern has already sealed the fate of the current Vice President, a question the article raises but does not answer.

Why it mattersSierra Leone's Vice President and the parties around the office are directly affected, as the analysis argues the post has blocked succession to the presidency.

Health · 2026-11-11

Sierra Leone presents Mental Health Bill 2026 to Parliament committee

The Health Ministry seeks to replace the 1902 Lunacy Act with a modern legal framework for mental healthcare.

Sierra Leone's Ministry of Health presented a proposed Mental Health Bill 2026 to a Parliamentary Committee on Monday, 9 November 2026, at Committee Room 1. The bill would replace the Lunacy Act of 1902, a law that is 124 years old, with a modern legal framework for mental healthcare.

The Ministry's delegation was led by Deputy Minister of Health I, Prof. Dr. Charles Senesie, and included the Acting Permanent Secretary, the Director of Primary Health Care and Dr. Abdul Jalloh, Director of Non-Communicable Diseases and Mental Health. The bill is designed to protect the rights and dignity of people with mental health conditions, address stigma and discrimination, and improve access to quality services.

Discussions also covered strengthening the mental health workforce, expanding community-based care, improving access to affordable treatment and essential medicines, and regulation and oversight of services. After the committee's review, the bill is expected to go to the floor of Parliament for debate.

Why it mattersPeople living with mental health conditions would gain legal protection and better access to affordable treatment and services if the bill passes, replacing a law that dates to 1902.
