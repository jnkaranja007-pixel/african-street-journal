Charcoal burning devastates Sierra Leone's environment and economy

Widespread charcoal production, driven by limited energy access, leads to deforestation, habitat loss, and greenhouse gas emissions.

Charcoal burning, a primary source of livelihood for many in Sierra Leone, is causing significant environmental damage. The practice, prevalent in urban areas like Freetown due to high demand and limited alternatives, involves cutting down trees, including fruit-bearing species, leading to deforestation and habitat loss. This process not only emits greenhouse gases but also threatens food security and exposes land to climate change effects, according to the Environmental Protection Agency.

The limited access to electricity, with only 36% of the population connected, and even lower access to clean cooking at 15%, fuels the demand for charcoal. Experts note that the burning of wood for charcoal and its subsequent use as an energy source releases substantial greenhouse gases, impacting both human health and the environment. A report estimates that traditional wood energy contributes significantly to global carbon dioxide equivalent emissions.

The environmental consequences of charcoal production are severe, including biodiversity loss and the long-term degradation of natural resources. While acknowledging the practice's harmful effects, officials admit there are currently few viable energy alternatives for the population. The continued demand for charcoal is expected to worsen climate change, potentially reducing future wood energy supplies.

Why it mattersThe widespread practice of charcoal burning is affecting farmers through deforestation and the loss of economic trees, while also impacting investors due to environmental degradation and the need for alternative energy solutions.

Sierra Leone Vice Presidency Hinders Succession, Analyst Says

The office, constitutionally powerful, has historically served as a political balancing tool rather than a leadership incubator.

The Vice Presidency in Sierra Leone is constitutionally a powerful office, second only to the presidency, but has historically been a political cul-de-sac rather than a pathway to higher leadership. Analysts note that successive governments have appointed Vice Presidents to balance regional, religious, or ethnic considerations, appease party blocs, neutralize rivals, or reward loyalty, rather than to cultivate ambition. This practice often leaves Vice Presidents without a clear succession path or an independent political base.

Post-independence history provides cautionary examples, such as Sorie Ibrahim Koroma and Francis Minah under Siaka Stevens, who were insiders but not groomed successors. Francis Minah's execution after an alleged coup attempt highlights the peril of proximity to power without political insulation. Solomon Ekuma Berewa, considered one of Sierra Leone's most prepared Vice Presidents, lost decisively in the 2007 presidential election, demonstrating that competence and continuity were insufficient against a desire for change.

More recent cases include Victor Bockarie Foh, whose tenure was short and politically constrained, and Samuel Sam-Sumana, who was elected Vice President but later estranged, expelled from his party, and politically orphaned. These instances suggest that proximity to the presidency has not translated into political security or a guaranteed path to the top office for Vice Presidents.

Why it mattersThe Vice Presidency in Sierra Leone is a political liability for those holding the office, as past Vice Presidents have not been elected President and some have faced political downfall.

First Lady's Tour Uses State Resources for Political Ambition

The First Lady's nationwide tour, utilizing state vehicles, security, and public funds, is criticized as a state-funded political machine for her 2028 ambitions.

The First Lady of Sierra Leone is conducting a nationwide political tour, utilizing state vehicles, security personnel, public funds, and government media. This tour is described as a state-funded political machine, masquerading as official activities, to promote her 2028 ambitions. Ministers and appointees are reportedly prohibited from early campaigning, creating a contradiction where the First Lady operates with privileges unavailable to other aspirants.

This political activity is occurring while Sierra Leoneans face economic hardship, including inflation and unemployment. The use of public resources for the First Lady's tour is seen as a symbol of double standards and misplaced priorities, sending a message that public suffering is irrelevant compared to political ambition. The tour includes fleets of government vehicles, hundreds of state security personnel, and publicly funded media coverage.

Critics allege that the First Lady's actions are an ethical collapse and a misuse of public funds, potentially fracturing the ruling party and deepening resentment. Loyalists have reportedly been installed in strategic party positions, leading to accusations of political colonization of party structures. The party chairman's role in directing delegates is also questioned, with claims of monarchical decree disguised as party discipline.

Why it mattersThe First Lady's use of state resources for her political ambitions while citizens face economic hardship highlights a disconnect between public welfare and political pursuits.

Finance Minister Defends Economic Management Amidst Criticism

A coalition of civil society groups called for the finance minister's dismissal, but the ministry argues global shocks, not mismanagement, caused hardship.

A statement by the "CSOs Consortium on Public Accountability" demanding the dismissal of Sierra Leone’s Minister of Finance, Sheku Ahmed Fantamadi Bangura, has been labeled a "deeply flawed intervention" that conflates economic hardship with mismanagement. The ministry asserts that global events like the COVID-19 pandemic, the Russia-Ukraine war, and aggressive monetary tightening significantly impacted economies worldwide, including Sierra Leone.

According to the ministry, Sierra Leone, unlike other nations such as Ghana, Zambia, Nigeria, and Kenya, avoided debt default and maintained engagement with the IMF. This outcome is attributed to disciplined macro-fiscal management. Data from the Bank of Sierra Leone indicates that inflation, while peaking due to global shocks, has since declined through coordinated fiscal and monetary tightening, with central bank financing of deficits curtailed.

The ministry also refutes claims of reckless debt accumulation, pointing to IMF-World Bank Debt Sustainability Analysis which shows debt is predominantly concessional and commercial borrowing has been restrained. Reforms have improved reporting and transparency, with no Eurobond exposure or hidden debt. The minister is credited with resisting pressure for unchecked borrowing that could have led to default.

Why it mattersThe finance minister's actions and the country's economic stability are directly relevant to investors assessing the nation's financial health and risk.

Women Face Rape, Harassment in Sierra Leone Mines

Artisanal miners in Kono district report sexual violence and lack of recourse amid economic hardship.

Women in Sierra Leone's Kono district are facing rampant rape and sexual harassment while working in artisanal mines, often as their only means of income after the civil war. Janet, 32, recounted being raped at 15 while scavenging in abandoned pits in Jaiama Nimikor, an experience that led to an unplanned pregnancy and community stigma. She continues to mine, sending meager support to the relative raising her child.

Haja, 48, a widow with three children, also shared her experience of being raped by a man in her village after leaving her children to work in the mines. She fled her community due to shame, but returned when her daughter pleaded. When she reported the assault, the camp master dismissed her claims, suggesting the perpetrator was her boyfriend, despite her denial.

These accounts highlight the severe dangers faced by women in the mining sector, where economic necessity forces them into precarious situations. The lack of accountability and support from mine authorities exacerbates their vulnerability, leaving survivors with deep emotional scars and limited options for justice or safety.

Why it mattersWomen miners in Kono district are affected by rape and sexual harassment, facing stigma and a lack of support from authorities.
