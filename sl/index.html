Charcoal burning deforests Sierra Leone, threatens food security

The practice, driven by limited energy access, leads to habitat loss and the cutting of economic trees, according to environmental officials.

Charcoal production in Sierra Leone is accelerating deforestation and environmental degradation, as limited access to electricity and clean cooking options forces many to rely on burning wood. This practice, common in Freetown and other urban centers, involves cutting down trees, including valuable fruit-bearing species, leaving land exposed and threatening biodiversity. Environmental Protection Agency Director Paul Lamin noted that the slow regrowth of trees exacerbates the problem, impacting carbon reduction efforts.

The burning of wood for charcoal and its subsequent use as an energy source releases significant greenhouse gases, contributing to air pollution and climate change. Experts estimate that traditional wood energy accounts for a substantial portion of global anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions. The Food and Agricultural Organization warns that continued unsustainable charcoal production will worsen climate change, potentially reducing future wood-energy supplies.

For many families, like that of 65-year-old Pa Alimamy, charcoal burning is a primary source of livelihood, with his entire family depending on it. Despite the acknowledged environmental hazards, the government's National Energy Compact indicates that only 36% of the population has access to electricity and a mere 15% have access to clean cooking, highlighting the scarcity of viable alternatives for households unable to afford gas or electricity.

Why it mattersThe widespread practice of charcoal burning is causing deforestation and environmental damage, directly impacting food security and livelihoods for many in Sierra Leone.

Sierra Leone Vice Presidents Fail to Reach Presidency

The office, constitutionally powerful, has historically served as a political cul-de-sac rather than a path to the presidency.

The Vice Presidency in Sierra Leone, though constitutionally second only to the presidency, has consistently failed to produce a successor president. Analysis suggests the office is often used for political balancing, regional appeasement, or neutralizing rivals, rather than cultivating future leadership. This practice has left many Vice Presidents without a clear succession pathway or an independent political base, hindering their presidential ambitions.

Historical examples illustrate this pattern. Sorie Ibrahim Koroma and Francis Minah, powerful insiders under Siaka Stevens, never became president, with Minah facing execution after a failed coup. Solomon Ekuma Berewa, considered highly prepared, lost decisively in 2007, demonstrating that competence in office did not guarantee electoral success. Victor Bockarie Foh's brief tenure and Samuel Sam-Sumana's estrangement and political isolation further highlight the office's limitations.

The current Vice President faces a historical precedent where the office has been a political liability rather than an advantage. The electorate's desire for change, even over experience, has repeatedly thwarted Vice Presidents' presidential bids. This recurring outcome raises questions about whether the current Vice President's fate is already sealed by this established political dynamic.

Why it mattersThe Vice Presidency in Sierra Leone has historically been a political dead end, with no Vice President ever elected president, potentially impacting the career trajectory of the current officeholder.

Women mining for income face rape and harassment

Artisanal miners in Kono district recount sexual assaults and community stigma after seeking income in abandoned pits.

Women in Sierra Leone's Kono district are facing sexual violence and harassment while working in abandoned mines, a dangerous occupation they turned to for income after the civil war. Janet, 32, recounted being raped at 15 while scavenging for diamonds and gold in Jaiama Nimikor, an experience that led to an unplanned pregnancy and community stigma. She now sends limited support to the child raised by a relative.

Haja, 48, a widow with three children, also shared her experience of being raped in the mines after her husband's death. She fled her community due to shame, leaving her children behind. When she reported the assault, the camp master dismissed her claims, suggesting the perpetrator might have been her boyfriend, which she denied.

These women, like many others, turned to mining as one of the few available avenues for income following the end of the civil war. The perilous work in abandoned pits, often undertaken alone, exposes them to significant risks, including sexual assault and a lack of recourse when reporting such incidents.

Why it mattersWomen are affected by rape and harassment in mines, which are a primary source of income for them.

Finance Minister's Policies Withstand Global Shocks, Report Claims

A consortium's call for the finance minister's dismissal is flawed, confusing hardship with mismanagement, according to an analysis.

A statement by the "CSOs Consortium on Public Accountability" demanding the dismissal of Finance Minister Sheku Ahmed Fantamadi Bangura has been labeled a "deeply flawed intervention" that conflates economic hardship with mismanagement. The analysis argues that global shocks like the COVID-19 pandemic, the Russia-Ukraine war, and aggressive monetary tightening significantly impacted economies worldwide, leading to inflation exceeding 20-30 percent in many Sub-Saharan African nations.

Despite these global pressures, Sierra Leone, unlike Ghana, Zambia, Nigeria, and Kenya, did not default on its debt, maintained its IMF program, and preserved access to financing. This outcome, the analysis contends, is a direct result of disciplined macro-fiscal management under Minister Bangura. Data from the Bank of Sierra Leone shows inflation peaked due to global shocks but subsequently declined through coordinated fiscal and monetary tightening, with central bank financing of deficits curtailed.

The consortium's claims of "poor inflation control" and "reckless debt accumulation" are disputed by IMF and World Bank data. While Sierra Leone remains at high risk of debt distress, its debt is predominantly concessional, commercial borrowing has been restrained, and debt management reforms have improved transparency. The analysis highlights the absence of Eurobond exposure, hidden debt, or debt-fueled spending, attributing the minister's resistance to unchecked borrowing for subsidies and wage hikes as crucial to avoiding default.

Why it mattersThe analysis suggests that the Finance Minister's management of global economic shocks and debt has prevented a fiscal freefall, contrasting Sierra Leone's situation with other African nations.

SLEWRC Revenue Jumps to NLE 7.4 Million

The Sierra Leone Electricity and Water Regulatory Commission more than tripled its internal revenue from levies and licenses in 2024 compared to the previous year.

The Sierra Leone Electricity and Water Regulatory Commission (SLEWRC) reported a significant increase in revenue generation, reaching NLE 7.4 million in 2024 from NLE 2.3 million in 2023. This growth stems from levies and licenses collected by the commission, which regulates electricity and water service providers. Despite not receiving budgeted funds from the Ministry of Finance for recurrent expenditures in both 2023 and 2024, SLEWRC achieved key deliverables.

In 2023, SLEWRC sanitized the packaged water industry through monitoring and enforcement, leading to more licenses and improved service quality. The commission resolved an average of eighty percent of complaints within a week and took enforcement actions against sixty-four non-compliant water companies. For 2024, SLEWRC expanded its operational network and developed a Multi-Year Tariff Order model for solar mini-grids, even without receiving allocated funds for recurrent expenditures.

Looking ahead to FY 2025-2027, SLEWRC plans to strengthen its organizational capacity, ensure market entry and universal access, and maintain international quality standards. The commission also intends to roll out a safety and warranty batching system for water products and monitor tariff rate implementation for sustainability. SLEWRC submitted a budget of NLE 5,590.29 for FY 2025 to 2027.

Why it mattersThe Sierra Leone Electricity and Water Regulatory Commission's increased revenue generation affects investors by demonstrating improved regulatory oversight and potential for sector growth.
