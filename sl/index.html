Sierra Leone warns of plastic waste health risks

Government officials highlight environmental and tourism threats from plastic pollution, urging public action.

Government officials in Sierra Leone have raised serious concerns about the environmental and health dangers posed by plastic waste, directly impacting citizens and marine life. Judith C. Jones, Senior Permanent Secretary at the Ministry of Tourism and Cultural Affairs, stated that plastic waste threatens the nation's beaches, a key tourism asset. The Go Circular 2026 initiative, themed “Green Actions, New Jobs,” aims to reduce, reuse, and recycle plastic waste for a cleaner country.

Jones described the growing enthusiasm for circularity as "heartwarming and deeply encouraging," noting that the movement, which started in 2024, now involves national and international actors. She stressed the need for greener businesses to combat plastic waste and urged schoolchildren to become agents of change. The ministry is collaborating with universities and innovators to establish a mini-recycling facility along the coastline.

The Deputy Minister of Environment and Climate Change, Mima Yema Mimi Sobba-Stephens, encouraged Sierra Leoneans to adopt waste-reducing habits for the benefit of health, tourism, and national pride. She explained that "going circular" means rethinking how everyday items are used and disposed of, emphasizing that individual behavior in responsible waste disposal is essential for protecting the ecosystem and positioning Sierra Leone as an attractive destination.

Why it mattersPlastic waste poses direct environmental and health threats to Sierra Leoneans, affecting livelihoods and the nation's tourism assets.

Sierra Leone and Kenya join creative economy partnership

The two nations signed Statements of Intent with The Akuna Group at CHOGM 2026 to connect talent with markets.

Sierra Leone and Kenya became founding champions of the Commonwealth Creative Economy Partnership by signing Statements of Intent with The Akuna Group at CHOGM 2026. This initiative aims to link talent with skills, investment, enterprise, and markets across the Commonwealth. Chief Minister Dr David Moinina Sengeh signed for Sierra Leone, and Principal Secretary for Foreign Affairs Dr A. Korir Sing’Oei signed for Kenya, with Sir Idris Elba representing The Akuna Group.

The agreement aligns with President Bio’s human capital agenda for Sierra Leone. Chief Minister Sengeh stated, “Our young people are not a problem to be managed; they are the resource to invest in.” In Kenya, the collaboration complements the Talanta Hela initiative, which Dr Sing’Oei said would give “youth creative enterprises a route to market.” Sir Idris Elba, Chairman of The Akuna Group, aims to help people earn from their talent.

Akuna’s wider work includes a US$1 million Google.org grant for a program training filmmakers in Ghana, Kenya, Nigeria, Sierra Leone, and South Africa. In Ghana, Akuna Wallet Ltd is exploring financial innovation within a Bank of Ghana regulatory sandbox. Discussions also occurred regarding a Barbados-led initiative to connect African and Caribbean talent.

Why it mattersSierra Leonean and Kenyan youth in creative industries will gain new routes to markets and opportunities to earn from their talents.

Sierra Leone charcoal burning causes deforestation

Environmental agency confirms practice destroys economic trees and worsens climate change effects.

Charcoal burning is a common livelihood across Sierra Leone, providing cooking fuel for families who cannot afford electricity or gas. Pa Alimamy, 65, has burned charcoal for over two decades, stating his family relies on it for survival. The practice is prevalent in Freetown and other towns due to high demand and limited access to alternative energy sources, with only 36% of the population having electricity and 15% clean cooking access.

The burning of wood for charcoal leads to deforestation, habitat loss, and environmental degradation. Paul Lamin, Director of Natural Resource Governance at the Environmental Protection Agency, acknowledged the hazards, noting that burners now cut down economic trees like mango and orange. This leaves land treeless, exposed to climate change, and threatens food security, as the trees cut take a long time to regrow and absorb carbon.

Greenhouse gas emissions from charcoal production and use harm human health and the environment. Globally, traditional wood energy emits 2.4 GT of carbon dioxide equivalent annually, with Sub-Saharan Africa contributing one-third of these emissions. The Food and Agricultural Organization estimates that continued unsustainable charcoal practices will worsen climate change, potentially reducing future wood-energy supplies.

Why it mattersSierra Leoneans who rely on charcoal for cooking face health risks from greenhouse gas emissions and environmental degradation, while the practice contributes to global climate change.

Sierra Leone MPs to debate three new bills

Lawmakers will convene on November 9, 2026, to scrutinize legislation on military veterans, explosives, and mental health.

Sierra Leone's Parliament has called all Members of Parliament to a pre-legislative hearing on Monday, November 9, 2026, at 10:00 AM in Freetown's Parliament Building. The session will be held in Committee Room One.

Lawmakers are strongly encouraged to attend promptly to deliberate on three major legislative proposals: The Military Veterans Agency Act 2026, The National Explosives Regulatory Authority Act 2026, and The Mental Health Act 2026. This hearing is a crucial step for reviewing and shaping the bills before their formal parliamentary readings.

The deliberations will allow members to examine the proposed laws, consider their potential implications, and identify areas needing clarification or amendment. The outcome is expected to strengthen legislation concerning military veterans' welfare, explosives regulation, and mental health services.

Why it mattersThe hearing will shape new laws affecting military veterans, explosives regulation, and mental health services in Sierra Leone.

Female artisanal miners report sexual violence in Kono district

Women working in diamond and gold pits in Jaiama Nimikor describe a pattern of rape and harassment while scavenging for income in abandoned mines.

Women working as artisanal miners in the diamond-rich Kono district of Sierra Leone face high risks of sexual violence while scavenging in abandoned pits. Janet, now 32, reported being raped at age 15 while searching for gold and diamonds in Jaiama Nimikor, a site located 8 miles from Koidu. She stated that she became pregnant following the assault and faced community stigmatization, forcing her to give up her child to a relative.

Other women in the area report similar experiences of abuse. Haja, a 48-year-old widow, said she was raped while working in the mines and fled her community due to shame. She reported the incident to a local camp master, who dismissed her claim by suggesting the perpetrator was her boyfriend. Haja stated that no official action was taken against the man, who eventually left the village on his own accord.

Many women in Kono turned to mining as a primary source of income following the end of the civil war. Janet and Haja both noted that they entered the sector to support their families after losing parents or spouses. Despite reporting incidents to village authorities, the women describe a lack of accountability for the perpetrators of these crimes.

Why it mattersThe story highlights the physical and social dangers faced by women working in the artisanal mining sector in Kono, specifically regarding sexual violence and lack of legal recourse.
