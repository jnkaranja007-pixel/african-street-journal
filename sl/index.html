Charcoal burning degrades environment, threatens food security

Sierra Leone's reliance on charcoal for cooking, due to limited electricity access, is driving deforestation and greenhouse gas emissions.

Charcoal production, a vital livelihood for many in Sierra Leone, is accelerating deforestation and environmental degradation. The practice, common in urban centers like Freetown, stems from limited access to electricity, with only 36% of the population connected, and clean cooking options available to just 15%. This forces households to rely on charcoal, a product of burnt wood, for cooking, despite its harmful environmental impact.

Environmental officials acknowledge the severe consequences, noting that burners are increasingly cutting down economic trees like mangoes and oranges. This practice leaves land barren, exacerbates climate change effects, and threatens food security. Experts warn that the burning process releases significant greenhouse gases, impacting both human health and the environment, and contributing to global carbon dioxide equivalent emissions.

The Food and Agricultural Organization estimates that traditional wood energy contributes substantially to global greenhouse gas emissions. Continued unsustainable charcoal production and use are expected to worsen climate change, which in turn can reduce future wood-energy supplies. This creates a cycle where environmental damage negatively affects the very resources people depend on for survival.

Why it mattersSierra Leoneans who cannot afford gas or electricity for cooking rely on charcoal, a practice that harms the environment and threatens future resource availability.

Sierra Leone Vice Presidents Fail to Reach Presidency

The constitution grants significant power to the vice presidency, yet no holder has ever been elected president.

The office of Vice President in Sierra Leone, constitutionally positioned as second only to the presidency, has historically failed to serve as a pathway to the nation's highest office. Analysts note that vice presidents are often appointed for political balancing, regional appeasement, or to neutralize rivals, rather than to cultivate future leadership. This practice has resulted in many vice presidents lacking a clear succession plan or an independent political base, hindering their presidential aspirations.

Past administrations have treated the vice presidency as a tool for internal party management rather than a leadership incubator. Examples include Sorie Ibrahim Koroma and Francis Minah under Siaka Stevens, who were powerful but not groomed for succession. Solomon Ekuma Berewa, considered highly prepared during the Kabbah era, lost the 2007 presidential election, demonstrating that competence alone was insufficient. Victor Bockarie Foh's brief tenure and Samuel Sam-Sumana's estrangement further illustrate the precariousness of the office.

The pattern suggests a recurring political reality where the vice presidency, despite its constitutional weight, often becomes a cul-de-sac for ambition. The electorate's desire for change, as seen in Berewa's defeat, can override considerations of continuity or experience. This historical trend raises questions about the future prospects of the current vice president, whose position may be more politically constrained than strategically advantageous for presidential succession.

Why it mattersThe vice presidency in Sierra Leone is a political office where individuals are often appointed for reasons other than presidential succession, limiting their ability to reach the presidency.

Women Face Rape, Harassment in Sierra Leone Mines

Artisanal miners in Kono district recount sexual violence and lack of recourse after reporting incidents.

Women in Sierra Leone's Kono district are reporting widespread rape and sexual harassment within mining sites, with many turning to scavenging in abandoned pits for income after the civil war. Janet, 32, recounted being raped at 15 while searching for diamonds in Jaiama Nimikor, an experience that led to an unplanned pregnancy and community stigma. She continues to mine to support her child, who was raised by a relative.

Haja, 48, a widow and mother of three, also shared her experience of being raped by a man in her village after leaving her children to work in the mines. She fled her community due to shame but later returned after her daughter's plea. When she reported the incident, the camp master dismissed her claims, suggesting the perpetrator was her boyfriend.

These accounts highlight the dangers faced by women in artisanal mining, a sector that became a primary source of income following the civil war. The lack of identification of perpetrators and the dismissal of complaints by authorities leave victims vulnerable and without justice, forcing many to endure the trauma and its lasting consequences.

Why it mattersWomen involved in artisanal mining are affected by sexual violence and a lack of accountability from authorities.

SLEWRC Revenue Rises to NLE 7.4 Million

The Sierra Leone Electricity and Water Regulatory Commission collected NLE 7.4 million in levies and licenses in 2024, a significant increase from the NLE 2.3 million generated.

The Sierra Leone Electricity and Water Regulatory Commission (SLEWRC) reported a substantial rise in internal revenue generation, reaching NLE 7.4 million in 2024 from levies and licenses. This marks a considerable increase compared to the NLE 2.3 million collected in 2023. Despite not receiving budgeted funds from the Ministry of Finance for operational expenditures in both 2023 and 2024, the commission successfully implemented key initiatives.

These initiatives included sanitizing the packaged water industry through enforcement actions, leading to more licenses and improved service quality. SLEWRC also completed quarterly publications of complaint and performance monitoring reports, resolving an average of 80% of complaints within a week. Enforcement actions were taken against 64 non-compliant water companies, encouraging broader licensing within the electricity and water sectors.

For the upcoming fiscal year, SLEWRC plans to strengthen its organizational capacity, ensure market entry and universal access, and roll out a safety batching system for water products. The commission also aims to monitor tariff rate implementation to promote sustainability. SLEWRC regulates electricity and water service providers, issuing and renewing licenses, and providing guidelines on service rates.

Why it mattersInvestors are affected by the increase in revenue generation and the commission's plans to monitor tariff rates, which could impact the profitability and operational costs of electricity and water sector companies.
