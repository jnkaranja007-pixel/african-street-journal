Sierra Leone charcoal burning causes deforestation and health risks

The widespread use of charcoal for cooking, driven by limited access to electricity and gas, is leading to significant deforestation and greenhouse gas emissions in Sierra Leone.

Charcoal burning, a primary source of livelihood for many in Sierra Leone, is contributing to severe environmental degradation and health concerns. With only 36% of the population having access to electricity and 15% to clean cooking, charcoal remains a dominant cooking fuel, particularly in Freetown and other urban centers. This practice involves cutting down trees, including valuable economic species like mango and orange, which exacerbates deforestation, habitat loss, and biodiversity decline.

Experts at the Environmental Protection Agency in Sierra Leone acknowledge the detrimental effects, noting that the slow regrowth of trees means the land is left exposed to climate change. The burning process itself releases significant amounts of greenhouse gases, contributing to air pollution and posing risks to human health. A report by the Food and Agricultural Organization estimates that traditional wood energy, including charcoal, accounts for a substantial portion of global greenhouse gas emissions.

The continued demand for charcoal, coupled with unsustainable production methods, is expected to worsen climate change impacts. This, in turn, could negatively affect forest health and productivity, potentially reducing future wood energy supplies. The lack of readily available and affordable alternative energy sources perpetuates this cycle, threatening both the environment and the livelihoods dependent on it.

Why it mattersThe environment and human health in Sierra Leone are negatively impacted by deforestation and greenhouse gas emissions resulting from charcoal production and use.

Sierra Leone vice presidents lack presidential succession path

The office, constitutionally powerful, has historically served as a political cul-de-sac rather than a stepping stone to the presidency.

The Vice Presidency in Sierra Leone, though constitutionally powerful, has consistently failed to produce a presidential successor. Analysts note that vice presidents are often appointed to balance regional, religious, or ethnic considerations, appease party blocs, or neutralize rivals, rather than to cultivate future leadership. This practice ensures most vice presidents enter office without a clear succession pathway or an independent political base.

Post-independence history illustrates this pattern. Vice presidents like Sorie Ibrahim Koroma and Francis Minah were powerful but not groomed for succession, with Minah facing execution after a failed coup. Solomon Ekuma Berewa, considered well-prepared, lost the 2007 presidential election, demonstrating that competence in the vice presidential role did not guarantee electoral success. His defeat suggests the office can be a political liability.

More recent examples include Victor Bockarie Foh's short and politically constrained tenure, and Samuel Sam-Sumana's experience of being estranged, expelled, and politically orphaned after his term. These cases highlight how proximity to power in the vice presidency does not translate into political security or a guaranteed path to higher office.

Why it mattersThe current and future vice presidents of Sierra Leone are affected by a political system that does not prepare them for presidential succession.

Female artisanal miners in Kono report systemic sexual violence

Women working in diamond and gold pits in Sierra Leone face persistent threats of rape and harassment while struggling to earn a daily livelihood.

Female artisanal miners in the Kono district of Sierra Leone are reporting frequent incidents of rape and sexual harassment while working in abandoned mining pits. Women scavenging for diamonds and gold in Jaiama Nimikor describe a dangerous environment where they are vulnerable to assault while attempting to support their families.

Janet, a 32-year-old miner, recounted being raped at age 15 while working alone in a pit. She reported the incident to her village head, but the perpetrator remained unidentified. Another miner, 48-year-old Haja, stated she was raped by a man in her village while working to support her three children. She claimed that when she reported the assault to a camp master, he dismissed her complaint by suggesting the attacker was her boyfriend.

These women turned to artisanal mining as a primary source of income following the civil war or the death of spouses. Many report that their experiences have led to social stigma, pregnancy, and the forced separation from their children. Despite these reports, victims say that local authorities and camp leaders have failed to take meaningful action against the perpetrators.

Why it mattersThe story highlights the physical danger and social stigma faced by women working in the informal mining sector in Kono district.

Politics · 2026-10-22

African youths question growth amid unrest

Protests erupted across the continent demanding better governance and economic relief as elections fueled anger and conflicts escalated.

Africa experienced a year of contrasts in 2025, with economic growth of 3.8-4.1% driven by infrastructure and market reforms. However, this progress was overshadowed by deepening political dissatisfaction, escalating conflicts, and social challenges. Youth-led protests demanding better governance and economic relief occurred in Kenya, Tanzania, Morocco, and other nations, fueled by economic hardships, floods displacing millions, and debt impacting social spending.

The political landscape was marked by high-stakes elections that stirred anger rather than trust, leading to violence and crackdowns in Tanzania and unrest in Cameroon. Fragile transitions occurred in Côte d'Ivoire and Gabon, while Guinea-Bissau returned to military control. Security threats multiplied, with worsening conflict in Sudan displacing over 11 million people, expanding armed groups in the Sahel, and intense fighting in eastern DRC. Nigeria faced large-scale violence, and Somalia's offensives encountered funding gaps.

Continental institutions struggled, with the AU's financial independence agenda stalled and peace operations facing funding shortages. ECOWAS weakened following withdrawals from Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger. New actors like the UAE deepened their influence through arms deals and ports. Social cohesion weakened as poverty deepened, jobs remained scarce, and climate disasters like cyclones and floods exposed slow progress in preparation.

Why it mattersAfrican youths are affected by deepening political dissatisfaction and economic hardships, leading them to protest for better governance and relief.

Sierra Leone regulator's revenue rises to NLE 7.4 million

The Sierra Leone Electricity and Water Regulatory Commission (SLEWRC) saw internal revenue climb from NLE 2.3 million in 2023 to NLE 7.4 million in 2024.

The Sierra Leone Electricity and Water Regulatory Commission (SLEWRC) generated NLE 7.4 million in internal revenue from levies and licenses in 2024, a significant increase from the NLE 2.3 million collected in 2023. Despite receiving no funds from the Ministry of Finance for recurrent expenditures in both 2023 and 2024, the commission successfully implemented key initiatives. These included sanitizing the packaged water industry through monitoring and enforcement, leading to more licenses and improved service quality, and resolving approximately 80% of complaints within a week.

SLEWRC also conducted enforcement actions against 64 non-compliant water companies, which encouraged increased licensing. The commission expanded its operational network and developed a Multi-Year Tariff Order (MYTO) model for solar mini-grids, which is nearing completion. For the upcoming fiscal years, SLEWRC plans to strengthen its organizational capacity, facilitate market entry and universal access, ensure international quality standards, and implement a safety and warranty batching system for water products.

The commission's strategic plan for 2025 includes monitoring tariff rate implementation to promote sustainability. The Deputy Director of SLEWRC presented a budget of NLE 5,590.29 for FY 2025 to 2027 during bilateral discussions with the Ministry of Finance. SLEWRC is responsible for regulating electricity and water service providers, including issuing and renewing licenses and setting guidelines for service rates.

Why it mattersBusinesses in the electricity and water sectors are affected by SLEWRC's increased enforcement actions and licensing requirements.
