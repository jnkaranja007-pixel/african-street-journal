Charcoal burning depletes Sierra Leone's forests and harms health

The widespread use of charcoal for cooking, driven by limited electricity access, is causing deforestation and emitting greenhouse gases.

Charcoal production, a primary livelihood for many in Sierra Leone, is accelerating deforestation and environmental degradation. The practice, common in Freetown and other towns, stems from the high demand for cooking fuel and the limited access to electricity, with only 36% of the population having power and 15% access to clean cooking. Charcoal burners are increasingly cutting down economic trees like orange and mango, leaving land barren and vulnerable to climate change.

Environmental officials acknowledge the hazards, noting that the slow regrowth of trees exacerbates the problem. The burning process itself releases significant greenhouse gases, contributing to air pollution and impacting human health. Experts estimate that traditional wood energy, including charcoal, emits 1/2.4 GT of carbon dioxide equivalent annually, with Sub-Saharan Africa accounting for a third of these emissions.

The Food and Agricultural Organization warns that continued unsustainable charcoal production and use will worsen climate change. This, in turn, can negatively affect forest health and productivity, potentially reducing future wood-energy supplies. The lack of viable alternative energy sources for households perpetuates this cycle, despite the known environmental and health consequences.

Why it mattersThe practice of charcoal burning affects farmers by destroying economic trees and degrading land, while also impacting the health of all Sierra Leoneans through air pollution.

Sierra Leone Vice Presidents Face Political Dead End

The nation's second-highest office has historically served as a political cul-de-sac, preventing any occupant from ascending to the presidency.

The Vice Presidency in Sierra Leone, constitutionally a powerful position, has consistently failed to produce presidential successors. Analysis suggests that vice presidents are often appointed for political balancing, regional appeasement, or to neutralize rivals, rather than to cultivate future leadership. This practice has left many without a clear pathway or independent political base for higher office.

Historical examples illustrate this pattern, from Sorie Ibrahim Koroma and Francis Minah under Siaka Stevens, to Solomon Ekuma Berewa under Kabbah, and Victor Bockarie Foh under the APC. Berewa, despite being well-prepared, lost the 2007 election, demonstrating that competence in the role did not guarantee electoral success. Samuel Sam-Sumana's experience also highlights the vulnerability of the office, as he was removed from power and politically isolated.

The current Vice President may face a similar fate, as the office's history suggests it is more of a political liability than a stepping stone. The constitution envisions a stabilizing role, but successive governments have utilized it as a tool for internal party management, hindering genuine succession planning and leaving occupants politically exposed.

Why it mattersThe Vice Presidency's historical role as a political dead end affects current and future officeholders by limiting their opportunities for presidential succession and political security.

Women Mining for Income Face Sexual Violence

Artisanal miners in Sierra Leone's Kono district report rape and harassment as they seek diamonds and gold for survival.

Women in Sierra Leone's Kono district are turning to artisanal mining for income following the civil war, but many face severe risks. Janet, now 32, recounted being raped at 15 while scavenging in abandoned mines in Jaiama Nimikor, an experience that led to an unplanned pregnancy and community stigma. She had dropped out of school and, after her parents' death, saw mining as a sole option to support herself and her family.

Haja, 48, a widow with three children, also shared her experience of being raped while working in the mines. She fled her community due to shame, leaving her children behind, and was dismissed by a camp master who suggested the attacker was her boyfriend. The man who raped her later left the village voluntarily. Both women reported their assaults, but received no effective recourse.

These accounts highlight the dangers faced by female artisanal miners who often work in isolated conditions. For many, mining is a critical, albeit perilous, means of livelihood in a region where economic opportunities are scarce. The lack of safety and support systems within the mining areas leaves these women vulnerable to exploitation and violence.

Why it mattersWomen in Kono district are affected by sexual violence in mining sites as they seek income, leading to trauma and social stigma.

Finance Minister's Policies Withstood Global Shocks, Report Claims

A statement calling for the finance minister's dismissal is flawed, confusing hardship with mismanagement, according to an analysis.

A recent call by the "CSOs Consortium on Public Accountability" for the dismissal of Sierra Leone’s Finance Minister, Sheku Ahmed Fantamadi Bangura, has been labeled a flawed intervention. The analysis argues that the consortium confuses economic hardship with mismanagement and populist frustration with policy failure, asserting that global shocks, not local fantasy, drove inflation.

The report highlights that between 2020 and 2024, global economic shocks including COVID-19 supply chain disruptions and the Russia-Ukraine war led to food and fuel inflation across advanced and developing economies. Sierra Leone, by contrast, did not default on its debt, maintained IMF program engagement, and preserved access to concessional financing, outcomes attributed to disciplined macro-fiscal management.

Regarding inflation, the analysis points to Bank of Sierra Leone data showing a peak following global shocks, followed by a decline through coordinated fiscal and monetary tightening. IMF reports note improved fiscal discipline and reduced central bank financing helped moderate inflationary pressures. The country imports over 70 percent of its consumption basket, meaning inflation can only be managed, not eliminated.

Why it mattersInvestors are affected by the government's disciplined macro-fiscal management and restrained commercial borrowing, which have helped moderate inflationary pressures and maintain access to financing.

Business · 2026-09-03

SLEWRC revenue jumps to NLE 7.4 million

The Sierra Leone Electricity and Water Regulatory Commission more than tripled its internal revenue from levies and licenses in 2024.

The Sierra Leone Electricity and Water Regulatory Commission (SLEWRC) reported a significant increase in internal revenue generation, reaching NLE 7.4 million in 2024. This figure represents a substantial rise from the NLE 2.3 million collected in 2023, primarily from levies and licenses. Despite not receiving budgeted funds from the Ministry of Finance for recurrent expenditures in both 2023 and 2024, the commission successfully completed key deliverables.

These achievements included sanitizing the packaged water industry through enforcement actions, which led to increased licensing and improved service quality. The SLEWRC also published quarterly reports on complaints and performance, resolving an average of eighty percent of complaints within one week. Enforcement actions were taken against sixty-four non-compliant water companies, further encouraging broader licensing across the electricity and water sectors.

For the upcoming fiscal year 2025, SLEWRC plans to strengthen its organizational capacity, facilitate market entry and universal access, and ensure service quality meets international standards. The commission also intends to roll out a safety and batching system for water products and monitor tariff rate implementation to promote sustainability. The Deputy Director presented a budget estimate of NLE 5,590.29 for FY 2025 to 2027.

Why it mattersInvestors are affected by the increase in revenue generation from levies and licenses, indicating potential growth and regulatory activity in the electricity and water sectors.
