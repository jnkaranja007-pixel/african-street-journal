Sierra Leone charcoal burning fuels deforestation

The practice, driven by limited access to electricity and clean cooking, leads to environmental degradation and threatens food security.

Pa Alimamy, a 65-year-old charcoal burner in Sierra Leone, has relied on the trade for over two decades to support his family. Charcoal is widely used for cooking in Freetown and other towns because only 36% of the population has electricity and 15% have clean cooking access, according to the government's National Energy Compact.

The Environmental Protection Agency in Sierra Leone acknowledged the environmental hazards of charcoal production, noting that burners are cutting down economic trees like orange and mango. Director of Natural Resource Governance Paul Lamin stated that this deforestation leaves land exposed to climate change effects and threatens food security, as the trees cut take a long time to regrow.

The burning of wood for charcoal and its use as an energy source emits significant greenhouse gases, contributing to climate change. A Food and Agricultural Organization report estimates that traditional wood energy emits 2.4 GT of carbon dioxide equivalent annually, with Sub-Saharan Africa accounting for one-third of these emissions.

Why it mattersCharcoal burning in Sierra Leone leads to deforestation and greenhouse gas emissions, impacting the environment and food security for the population.

Health · 2026-11-13

UNFPA and Iceland fund fistula surgeries in Makeni and Bo

About 10 women have already been operated on at Makeni Regional Hospital, and Bo Government Hospital is next in line for 10 to 15 more.

The United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA), working with the Government of Iceland and Sierra Leone's Ministry of Health, has begun obstetric fistula repair surgeries at Makeni Regional Hospital under the National Obstetric Fistula Surgery Camp. On Wednesday, 11 November 2026, UNFPA Sierra Leone Representative Kunle Kaden Adeniyi visited the hospital with officials from the Embassy of Iceland in Freetown and the Ministry of Health's Directorate of Reproductive and Child Health, and spoke with patients, survivors, surgeons, nurses and midwives.

According to UNFPA, about 10 women have already received treatment in Makeni. Theatre renovations and medical equipment, funded by Iceland through UNFPA, made the surgeries possible. The programme is expected to extend to Bo Government Hospital, where another 10 to 15 women are scheduled for surgery.

Since 2022, the partnership has supported about 545 fistula repair surgeries, screened 1,936 women for suspected fistula and given psychosocial rehabilitation, livelihood training and family reintegration support to 294 survivors. It has also trained 234 community health workers and 1,075 frontline providers in emergency obstetric and newborn care.

Why it mattersWomen with obstetric fistula in Makeni and Bo can now get surgery at no cost, and the programme is due to reach Bo next.

Sierra Leone launches €2 million school feeding upgrade

The European Union and WFP will support the initiative for two years to improve monitoring and transparency.

Sierra Leone's Ministry of Basic and Senior Secondary Education (MBSSE), the European Union (EU), and the United Nations World Food Programme (WFP) have launched a €2 million initiative to enhance the National School Feeding Programme. This two-year project aims to improve monitoring, transparency, and accountability in managing school feeding activities across the country. The initiative, funded by the EU and supported by WFP, will strengthen the government's ability to gather and use reliable information for programme management.

The intervention is expected to benefit over one million children by ensuring better resource allocation and evidence-based decision-making. The official launch occurred on Monday, 9 November 2026, in Freetown, bringing together senior officials from MBSSE, the National School Feeding Secretariat, the EU, WFP, and other development partners. This effort addresses the need for stronger monitoring systems due to existing manual reporting and fragmented data collection that hinder effective planning.

New digital technologies like Open Data Kit and School Connect will be introduced to facilitate real-time data gathering and management. The project prioritizes government ownership and capacity development for long-term sustainability. Minister of Basic and Senior Secondary Education, Conrad Sackey, stated that the National School Feeding Secretariat will transition to systematically managing evidence for policy and operational decisions over the next 24 months.

Why it mattersThe National School Feeding Programme will receive improved monitoring and transparency, benefiting over one million children with better resource allocation.

Politics · 2026-11-13

Sierra Leone's vice presidency has never produced a president

Analyst Foday M. Daboh argues that the office is often used to balance factions rather than to groom successors, leaving holders without a clear path to power.

No Vice President in Sierra Leone has ever been elected President, according to a February 2026 essay by Foday M. Daboh, a public policy analyst and political economist. Daboh says the office, second only to the presidency under the 1991 Constitution, has more often been a dead end than a route to the top job.

Governments have typically appointed vice presidents to balance regional, religious or ethnic interests, appease party blocs, neutralise rivals or reward loyalty, rather than to prepare a successor. Daboh cites Solomon Ekuma Berewa, a seasoned vice president who lost the 2007 SLPP presidential bid to Ernest Bai Koroma, and Samuel Sam-Sumana, who was expelled from the APC after falling out with the president.

Daboh also points to Francis Minah, who was executed after an alleged failed coup in 1987, as evidence that proximity to power without political protection can be dangerous. He argues that the question of whether the current Vice President can still reach the presidency has become more urgent in light of this history.

Politics · 2026-11-13

Sierra Leone's applause economy silences truth

Political loyalty is often mistaken for public service in Sierra Leone, with praise becoming a currency and silence the price for inclusion.

In Sierra Leone, political power is often surrounded by applause that grows louder with appointments and fades as leaders near the end of their term. This phenomenon, termed the 'applause economy' by Yoni Emmanuel Sesay, involves praise as currency, loyalty as investment, and access as a dividend. Silence becomes the price paid for continued inclusion, drowning out truths and costing the country.

Sesay noted that this culture of mistaking personal loyalty for public service is not new to Sierra Leone and did not begin with President Julius Maada Bio, whose presidency concludes in 2028. He recalled the administration of former President Ernest Bai Koroma, where agreement with the president was often treated as the highest loyalty, and criticism was sometimes seen as hostility.

The 2018 election demonstrated the transferable nature of political allegiance when political rewards shifted. Sesay observed that while changing parties is not inherently dishonourable, allegiance can become remarkably transferable when political advancement depends solely on access to power. He advised those currently surrounding President Bio to remember this historical lesson.

Why it mattersPolitical allegiance in Sierra Leone is becoming transferable based on access to power, affecting the country's governance and public service.
