Sierra Leone charcoal burning causes deforestation, threatens food security

Charcoal production in Sierra Leone, a primary energy source for many households, is leading to widespread deforestation and environmental degradation, impacting food security.

Charcoal burning, a trade supporting many families in Sierra Leone, is causing significant environmental damage. The practice, common in Freetown and other regional towns due to high demand and limited access to alternative energy, involves cutting down trees, including economic ones like orange and mango, leaving land exposed to climate change effects.

Paul Lamin, Director of Natural Resource Governance at Sierra Leone's Environmental Protection Agency, stated that charcoal burning deforests the environment and removes trees that take a long time to regrow, which are crucial for reducing carbon space. He acknowledged the limited alternative energy options available to the population.

Experts and a Food and Agricultural Organization (FAO) report, "The Charcoal Transition," indicate that traditional wood energy, including charcoal, emits 1 to 2.4 gigatons of carbon dioxide equivalent per year, accounting for 2 to 7% of total human-caused greenhouse gas emissions globally. Sub-Saharan Africa contributes one-third of these emissions from wood energy.

Why it mattersThe environmental degradation caused by charcoal burning directly affects the health and livelihoods of Sierra Leoneans, particularly those reliant on forests for resources and vulnerable to climate change impacts on food security.

Sierra Leone Vice Presidency Offers Limited Path to Top Office

The Vice Presidency in Sierra Leone has historically served as a political balancing mechanism rather than a direct route to presidential succession, according to a February 2026.

The office of Vice President in Sierra Leone, despite its constitutional power, has consistently failed to launch its occupants into the presidency, a pattern highlighted by public policy analyst Foday M. Daboh. Since independence, no Vice President has been elected President, leading to a recurring national debate about the office's true utility.

Historically, Vice Presidents have been appointed to balance regional, religious, or ethnic considerations, appease party blocs, neutralize rivals, or reward loyalty. This approach has meant that most Vice Presidents lack a clear succession pathway and an independent political base, making the position more of a cul-de-sac than a corridor to higher leadership.

Notable examples include Solomon Ekuma Berewa, who lost the 2007 presidential election despite his experience, and Samuel Sam-Sumana, who was expelled from his party. These cases illustrate that proximity to power does not guarantee political security or advancement, often exposing Vice Presidents to political liabilities rather than advantages.

Why it mattersThis report directly affects politicians and their supporters by detailing the historical challenges and limited prospects for Vice Presidents to ascend to the presidency in Sierra Leone.

Women miners face sexual violence in Sierra Leone's Kono District

Female artisanal gold miners in Jaiama Nimikor, Kono District, report experiencing rape and sexual harassment while working in the mines.

Women working in artisanal mines in Jaiama Nimikor, Kono District, are encountering significant risks of sexual violence. Janet, now 32, recounted being raped at age 15 in a mine eight miles from Koidu, leading to a pregnancy and community stigmatization. She had turned to mining after her parents' death in the civil war to support herself and her family.

Another miner, Haja, 48, also reported being raped by a man in her village while she was working in the mines. She fled her community due to shame, leaving her children behind, and stated that her report was dismissed by a camp master who suggested the rapist was her boyfriend. The man who raped Haja later left the village.

These accounts highlight the dangers faced by women who rely on mining for their livelihood, often in the absence of other income opportunities. Both women described their experiences as life-changing, resulting in profound personal and social consequences, including pregnancy, stigmatization, and displacement from their homes.

Why it mattersWomen engaged in artisanal mining in Sierra Leone are directly affected by sexual violence, leading to severe personal trauma, social stigmatization, and economic hardship.

Finance Minister Bangura avoids default amidst global economic shocks

Sierra Leone's Finance Minister Sheku Ahmed Fantamadi Bangura maintained IMF program engagement and avoided fiscal freefall despite global economic challenges from 2020 to 2024.

Sierra Leone's Finance Minister Sheku Ahmed Fantamadi Bangura has been credited with disciplined macro-fiscal management, preventing the country from defaulting on its external debt between 2020 and 2024. This period saw other nations like Ghana default and Zambia enter debt restructuring, while Sierra Leone maintained access to concessional financing and avoided fiscal freefall, according to Politico SL.

The country's inflation, which peaked following global food and fuel shocks, subsequently declined due to coordinated fiscal and monetary tightening, as noted in Bank of Sierra Leone Monetary Policy Statements. The IMF Country Reports for Sierra Leone (2023, 2024) also confirm that improved fiscal discipline and reduced central bank financing helped moderate inflationary pressures.

Despite being at high risk of debt distress, Sierra Leone's debt is predominantly concessional, with commercial borrowing restrained and debt management reforms improving transparency, according to the IMF–World Bank Debt Sustainability Analysis. The country has no Eurobond exposure or hidden debt, avoiding unchecked borrowing for subsidies and wage hikes that could have triggered default.

Why it mattersThe story directly affects Sierra Leonean citizens by detailing how the Finance Minister's actions prevented economic default and managed inflation, impacting their daily cost of living and financial stability.

African Economies Maintain Growth Amidst Political Instability and Social Challenges

Africa's economic growth held steady at around 3.8 - 4.1% in 2025, driven by infrastructure investments and market reforms, despite widespread political dissatisfaction and.

African economies demonstrated resilience in 2025, with growth rates maintaining between 3.8 and 4.1%. This performance was attributed to significant infrastructure investments, ongoing market reforms, and recoveries in major economies such as Nigeria, Egypt, and South Africa, according to Abdul Tejan-Cole for Politico SL.

However, this economic progress occurred against a backdrop of deepening political dissatisfaction and escalating conflicts across the continent. Youth-led protests in Kenya, Tanzania, and Morocco demanded better governance amidst economic hardships, while elections in many capitals, including Tanzania and Cameroon, stirred anger and unrest.

The continent also faced persistent social challenges, including disinformation-fueled elections, surging cyber threats, and health crises. Security threats multiplied, with the war in Sudan worsening and displacing more than eleven million people, and armed groups expanding in the Sahel, impacting coastal states.

Why it mattersAfrican citizens are affected by the contrast between economic growth and persistent social and political instability, which influences their daily lives and opportunities.
