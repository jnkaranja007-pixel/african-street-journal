Sánchez Excludes Morocco, Blames Russia and Israel for Ceuta Crisis

Intelligence reports suggest international networks, not Morocco, fueled the Ceuta border crisis, according to Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez.

Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez stated that Moroccan responsibility has been excluded from the Ceuta crisis, with intelligence services identifying Russia, Israel, and far-right international networks as potential contributors to disinformation. This assessment positions Morocco as an essential partner for resolving the situation, a notable shift from previous border issues involving Western Sahara.

During his first interview of the political year on Cadena SER's 'Hoy por Hoy,' Sánchez dedicated significant time to discussing the Ceuta crisis. He referenced reports from the CNI (National Intelligence Centre) and the unexpected scale of the event, while notably omitting Algeria from the discussion despite a recent visit aimed at improving bilateral relations.

The Prime Minister's remarks suggest a reorientation of blame away from regional neighbors towards broader international actors and extremist groups. The exclusion of Morocco and Algeria from direct responsibility for the crisis's escalation indicates a strategic recalibration in Spain's foreign policy approach to regional stability and border management.

Why it mattersThe Spanish government's intelligence assessment excludes Morocco from responsibility for the Ceuta crisis, impacting potential diplomatic and trade relationships.

Western Sahara Leader Attends African Union Summit Amidst Rights Campaign

Brahim Ghali participated in the Extraordinary Summit of the African Union in Luanda while analyst Taleb Alisalem vowed to continue speaking out despite threats.

Western Sahara's presence was noted at the African Union summit in Luanda, with leader Brahim Ghali attending alongside other heads of state. The Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic holds full membership in the AU, a status predating Morocco's return to the organization in 2017. This participation underscores the ongoing institutional role of the Sahrawi cause on the continent, irrespective of diplomatic efforts to diminish its standing within the AU.

Concurrently, Sahrawi analyst Taleb Alisalem responded to a denunciation by Spanish human rights organizations regarding a campaign of threats and harassment against him. The Spanish Pro-Human Rights League and the International Federation for Human Rights-Spain reported that Alisalem has faced direct threats, personal data dissemination, and public targeting. Alisalem stated, "We will not be silenced! We continue," signaling a resolve to maintain public discourse.

The events highlight the dual assertion of the Sahrawi people's presence in both institutional and public spheres. The analysis of the Sahrawi issue extends beyond decolonization to encompass continental debates on peace, security, and political integration. Furthermore, a Spanish publication recalled Spain's historical responsibility, linking the repression under Franco in Western Sahara to Spain's democratic memory.

Why it mattersThe Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic's participation in the African Union summit and the public stance of analyst Taleb Alisalem demonstrate the ongoing institutional and public presence of the Sahrawi cause.

Saharaui Republic Affirms African Union Membership

The Saharaui Republic's full membership in the African Union is an institutional reality not subject to Moroccan decision.

The Saharaui Republic maintains its status as a full member of the African Union, a position established in 1982 when it was admitted to the Organization for African Unity. Morocco, which had rejected the decision, left the organization two years later. The Saharaui Republic remained a member as the OAU transitioned into the African Union. Morocco rejoined the AU in 2017, entering an organization where the Saharaui Republic was already a fully recognized member state.

Morocco has since engaged in diplomatic efforts to diminish the international recognition of the Saharaui Republic and shift the balance of power within Africa. While Morocco has secured political support and promoted its autonomy proposal, the African Union's Constitutive Act does not provide a legal mechanism for expelling a member state based on another's challenge to its legitimacy. The AU's charter does, however, allow for the suspension of governments that come to power through unconstitutional means and permits states to voluntarily withdraw.

The Saharaui Republic's participation in African Union summits, with its flag displayed alongside other member states and its delegation attending meetings with institutional parity, underscores its established presence. This institutional standing is not a political concession or dependent on Moroccan recognition, but rather a continuing legal condition within the continental body.

Why it mattersThe Saharaui Republic's full membership in the African Union grants it institutional rights and a platform for participation in continental affairs, irrespective of Moroccan objections.
