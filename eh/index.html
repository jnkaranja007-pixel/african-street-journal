Sánchez dismisses Morocco in Ceuta crisis, eyes Russia, Israel

Spanish intelligence suggests international networks, not Morocco, fueled the Ceuta crisis, with Western Sahara absent from the analysis.

Spanish Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez has stated that Morocco bears no responsibility for the recent Ceuta crisis, according to government intelligence. Instead, intelligence reports point towards Russia, Israel, and international far-right networks as potential contributors to the disinformation surrounding the event. Morocco is considered an essential partner for resolving the situation, a significant shift from previous border disputes where the Western Sahara conflict was directly implicated.

In a radio interview, Sánchez addressed the unexpected scale of the Ceuta crisis, noting that while his presidency has been long for some, his tenure is eight years compared to the King's over twenty. He questioned the King's visits to Ceuta, drawing a contrast with his own leadership. Notably absent from Sánchez's analysis of the crisis's origins were both Morocco and Algeria, despite a recent diplomatic visit to Algiers aimed at improving bilateral relations.

The Prime Minister's remarks during his first political interview of the season focused heavily on the Ceuta incident, referencing reports from the CNI (National Intelligence Centre). The exclusion of the Western Sahara conflict and Algeria from the intelligence assessment marks a departure from previous analyses of border-related issues in the region, highlighting a new focus on international actors in the current geopolitical landscape.

Why it mattersThe Spanish government's intelligence assessment of the Ceuta crisis, which excludes Morocco and implicates international actors, affects the geopolitical understanding of regional stability and potential foreign interference.

Western Sahara leader attends African Union summit amid threats

Brahim Ghali participated in the African Union Extraordinary Summit in Luanda while analyst Taleb Alisalem vowed to continue speaking out despite harassment.

Western Sahara's presence at the African Union Extraordinary Summit in Luanda, with leader Brahim Ghali in attendance, underscores its status as a full member of the organization. This membership predates Morocco's return to the AU in 2017 and is not dependent on Moroccan approval. The participation highlights that discussions on African political architecture, peace, and security cannot exclude the Western Sahara issue.

Simultaneously, analyst Taleb Alisalem has vowed to continue speaking publicly despite facing a campaign of threats and harassment. Spanish human rights organizations have denounced this campaign, which includes personal data dissemination and public targeting. Alisalem's response, "We will not be silenced! We continue," signals defiance against efforts to suppress his voice and public engagement.

The events also draw attention to Spain's historical responsibility. A recent commentary recalled that Spain's democratic memory must encompass repression that occurred under Spanish administration in Western Sahara. Figures like Basiri and Zemla, victims of Spanish colonialism, are part of this memory, which continues to seek truth and recognition.

Why it mattersThe Western Sahara's participation in the African Union and the defiance of an analyst facing threats highlight the ongoing political struggle for recognition and freedom of expression.

Mohamed Abdelaziz Led Polisario for Four Decades

The former leader of the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic and Polisario Front held his positions until his death in 2016.

Mohamed Abdelaziz assumed leadership of the Polisario Front and the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic in August 1976. He maintained these roles until his passing in 2016, leading the organization for nearly forty years. Abdelaziz hailed from a prominent nomadic family, though not all members shared his political views on the Sahara question.

His tenure began fifty years ago, marking a significant period for the Sahrawi independence movement. The article, originally published in November 2015, reflects on his long leadership. The complexity of the former Spanish Sahara issue is highlighted by his family's diverse perspectives.

Abdelaziz's leadership spanned a critical era for the region. His nearly four-decade command represents a substantial portion of the movement's history. The circumstances of his leadership and family background underscore the intricate nature of the Sahrawi cause.

Why it mattersThe long-term leadership of Mohamed Abdelaziz affected the political direction and stability of the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic and the Polisario Front.

Saharawi Republic solidifies African Union membership despite Morocco's objections

The Saharawi Arab Democratic Republic holds full membership in the African Union, a status established before Morocco's return to the organization.

The Saharawi Arab Democratic Republic (RASD) maintains its status as a full member of the African Union, a position that does not depend on Morocco's approval. The RASD was admitted to the Organization of African Unity in 1982, and despite Morocco's subsequent withdrawal, the RASD remained a member. When Morocco rejoined the continental body in 2017 after a thirty-year absence, it entered an organization where the RASD was already established.

Morocco has pursued diplomatic efforts to diminish international recognition of the RASD and shift the balance of power within Africa, leading some governments to alter their stances and promote autonomy proposals. However, the African Union's Constitutive Act does not provide a legal mechanism for expelling a member state based on another's challenge to its legitimacy, though it does allow for the suspension of governments or voluntary withdrawal.

The presence of the Saharawi president at African Union summits, with the RASD flag displayed alongside other member states and its delegation participating with institutional rights, highlights this ongoing political reality. This institutional standing is not a political concession but a consequence of its established membership, which predates Morocco's re-entry into the continental organization.

Why it mattersThe Saharawi Arab Democratic Republic's full membership in the African Union is a political status that affects its institutional standing and participation in continental affairs.
