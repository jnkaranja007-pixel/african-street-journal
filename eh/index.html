Saharawi women demand equality now, not after independence

Women in Saharawi refugee camps are pushing for immediate action on issues like child marriage and professional barriers, arguing their rights should not be postponed.

Saharawi women have long been central to the organization of refugee camps, contributing to education, healthcare, and political structures. While their access to higher education now rivals that of men, and they hold 42.7% of parliamentary seats, internal discussions highlight persistent inequalities. These include concerns about child marriage, the division of domestic labor, social pressures on young women, and challenges in career development post-education.

These demands are framed within the exceptional context of five decades of war, occupation, and displacement. Historically, positive discrimination and gender quotas have been employed to boost women's institutional representation. However, some women report that raising these issues can be perceived as detrimental to the national cause. They counter that the fight for independence and the pursuit of women's rights are not mutually exclusive and should proceed concurrently.

The debate over women's rights is inseparable from the harsh conditions shaping Saharawi society over the past fifty years, marked by conflict and exile. Acknowledging these inequalities requires understanding the environment in which they exist. The core argument is that the struggle for national liberation should not delay the advancement of women's rights, advocating for both to be addressed simultaneously.

Why it mattersSaharawi women are affected by ongoing debates about their rights and equality, which they argue should not be postponed until after the struggle for independence is resolved.

Mohamed Abdelaziz Led Polisario for Forty Years

The former leader of the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic and the Polisario Front held his positions until his death in 2016.

Mohamed Abdelaziz assumed leadership of the Polisario Front and the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic in August 1976. He maintained this role until his passing in 2016, leading the organization for nearly four decades. Abdelaziz hailed from a prominent nomadic family, though not all members shared his political views on the Sahara question.

His forty-year tenure marks a significant period for the Polisario Front and the Sahrawi cause. The organization's leadership under Abdelaziz navigated complex political landscapes. His leadership began fifty years ago, a milestone noted by Jeune Afrique.

The article revisiting Abdelaziz's leadership was initially published in November 2015, a year before his death. It highlights the enduring complexity of the former Spanish Sahara issue and the long-standing leadership of Mohamed Abdelaziz.

Why it mattersMohamed Abdelaziz's long leadership of the Polisario Front and the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic directly impacts the political future and self-determination efforts for the Sahrawi people.

Morocco Migrant Surge Follows Spanish Sahrawi Law

Tens of thousands crossed into Ceuta after Spain advanced a law granting nationality to Sahrawis born under its former administration.

Tens of thousands of people, estimated between fifty thousand and seventy-two thousand, crossed into Ceuta on July 30, 2026, following a Spanish parliamentary committee's approval of a law granting Spanish nationality to Sahrawis born under Spanish administration. This influx resulted in eighty-two deaths on the Spanish side and fourteen confirmed by Morocco, with most victims remaining unidentified. Security sources cited by El Español suggested Morocco used this migration as diplomatic leverage against Spain's decisions regarding the Sahrawi law and its outreach to Algiers.

The Moroccan Minister of Justice, Abdellatif Ouahbi, later publicly asserted Moroccan sovereignty over Ceuta and Melilla, attributing the surge to Spain's migration policies. The Moroccan National Council for Human Rights also accused the Spanish Civil Guard of mistreating migrants. This pattern, where a border is opened, the action is denied, and the victims are used as a political tool, is considered incompatible with Morocco's border security apparatus, which has been funded by the European Union for two decades.

The seven-day interval between the parliamentary committee's vote on the Sahrawi nationality law and the mass crossing into Ceuta highlights a potential strategic response from Morocco. While no official order from Rabat has been found, independent analyses point to a convergence of factors suggesting a deliberate, albeit unacknowledged, Moroccan strategy to exert pressure on Spain.

Why it mattersMoroccan authorities are accused of using migration as a tool to pressure Spain following its decision to grant nationality to Sahrawis, affecting border control and diplomatic relations.

Congress Considers Spanish Nationality for Sahrawis

A proposed law offers a special path to Spanish citizenship for Sahrawis born before September 1977, regardless of residency.

The Spanish Congress is set to debate a significant bill offering a special pathway to Spanish nationality for Sahrawis. Following approval by the Justice Commission on August 7, the proposal will move to the full Congress after the summer recess. This legislation aims to grant Spanish nationality by naturalization to Sahrawis born in Western Sahara before September 29, 1977, even if they do not currently reside legally in Spain.

To qualify, applicants can present various documents, including old Spanish identity cards, birth certificates, family records, and other Spanish administration documents. Additionally, school certificates, pension records, driving licenses, or medical documentation may be considered collectively to prove eligibility. The proposal also extends to first-degree descendants, who will have a five-year window from their parent's nationality registration to claim their right.

The application period for this special nationality provision is slated to last for three years from the law's enactment. This initiative represents a crucial step in addressing the legal status of Sahrawi individuals with historical ties to Spain, potentially impacting a significant number of people with connections to the region.

Why it mattersSahrawis born in Western Sahara before September 29, 1977, and their first-degree descendants may gain Spanish nationality through an extraordinary process.
