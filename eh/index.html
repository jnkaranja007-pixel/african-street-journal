Morocco Migrant Surge Follows Spanish Sahrawi Law

Thousands crossed into Ceuta after Spain advanced a law granting nationality to Sahrawis, a move Morocco opposed.

Tens of thousands of people crossed into Ceuta in late July, with official figures ranging from 50,000 to 72,000. The influx occurred within hours, following a seven-day period that saw Spain's Congress of Deputies approve a report on a bill granting Spanish nationality to Sahrawis born under Spanish administration. This legislative step, previously stalled, was agreed upon by the PSOE party, with the final vote in the Justice Commission occurring on July 23rd.

Security sources cited by El Español newspaper suggested Morocco used migration pressure as a diplomatic tool to express discontent with Spain's Sahrawi nationality law and its closer ties with Algiers. El Público newspaper described the event as Morocco transferring its displeasure over the same law to Ceuta, through a deliberate lack of border surveillance. Moroccan Minister of Justice Abdellatif Ouahbi later publicly asserted sovereignty over Ceuta and Melilla, attributing the crossing to Spain's migration policy.

The Moroccan National Council for Human Rights accused the Spanish Civil Guard of mistreating migrants. The sequence of events suggests a deliberate opening of borders, followed by denial and the use of migrants as leverage against the receiving country. The scale of the crossing is considered incompatible with the security apparatus Rabat maintains at the border, for which it receives payment from the European Union.

Why it mattersMorocco used migration as a diplomatic tool to protest Spain's law granting nationality to Sahrawis, affecting Spain's border control and diplomatic relations.

Saharawi women demand equality now, not after independence

Despite significant institutional representation, Saharawi women are raising concerns about ongoing inequalities within their society.

Saharawi women have played a vital role for fifty years in refugee camp organization, education, healthcare, and political structures of the Saharawi Republic. Their access to higher education now nears that of men, and they hold 42.7% of seats in the Saharawi Parliament. However, this historical prominence does not preclude internal discussions about persistent inequalities and pending rights transformations.

Voices from ministers, officials, students, and activists highlight issues such as child marriage, the distribution of care responsibilities, social pressures on young women, and professional development challenges after years of training. These debates occur within the context of exceptional conditions of war, occupation, and refuge that have shaped Saharawi society for five decades.

Some women argue that denouncing inequalities should not be seen as detrimental to the national cause, asserting instead that the fight for Western Sahara's independence and the pursuit of women's rights can and should happen concurrently. This discussion is inseparable from the fifty-year reality of war, exile, and limited opportunities that have also influenced social evolution in the camps.

Why it mattersSaharawi women are affected by ongoing inequalities in areas like child marriage and professional development, despite their significant political participation.

Sahrawis' Spanish Nationality Path Advances in Congress

A proposed law offers a special route for Sahrawis born in Western Sahara before September 1977 to gain Spanish nationality.

A legislative proposal concerning Spanish nationality for Sahrawis is nearing a critical stage in the Congress of Deputies following the summer recess. The Justice Commission approved a report on August 7th, which will serve as the foundation for the full congressional debate. This text outlines an exceptional pathway for granting Spanish nationality by naturalization to Sahrawis born in Western Sahara prior to September 29, 1977, regardless of their current legal residency status in Spain.

Documentation such as old Spanish identity cards, birth certificates, family records, and official Spanish administration papers can be used to prove eligibility. Additionally, school certificates, pension records, driving licenses, or medical documentation, when considered collectively, may also serve as evidence. The proposal also extends to first-degree descendants, who will have a five-year window from the date their parent's nationality is registered to exercise their option.

Applications for this special nationality status can be submitted over a three-year period. This legislative move aims to provide a clear process for individuals with historical ties to Spain through their birth in Western Sahara. The process is designed to be accessible through various forms of documentation, acknowledging the complexities of historical records for this population.

Why it mattersSahrawis born in Western Sahara before September 29, 1977, and their first-degree descendants can apply for Spanish nationality through an extraordinary process.
