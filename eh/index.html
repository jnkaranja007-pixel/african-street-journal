Dakar rally to cross occupied Western Sahara

The 2027 race will traverse Smara and Dajla, cities claimed by Morocco.

The 2027 Dakar Rally will include stages in occupied Western Sahara, organizers announced on October 30. The race is scheduled from January 9 to 23, starting in Portugal and ending in Marrakech, Morocco. The route will pass through Smara and extend to the vicinity of Dajla, both cities under Moroccan control. This marks a return to desert racing after the event's absence from Saudi Arabia due to Houthi attacks.

The French organizer, ASO, presents Smara and Dajla as "the southern tip" of Morocco, despite international rulings and the UN considering Western Sahara a non-autonomous territory awaiting decolonization. The Polisario Front has considered the ceasefire broken since November 2020, and Smara experienced projectile impacts in October 2023. The rally's inclusion of these areas follows Morocco's strategy of hosting international events to assert its claim over the territory.

This decision comes as the UN Security Council debated the territory on the same day ASO made the announcement. The rally will spend five days racing on Sahrawi soil, from January 18 to 22. Spain will briefly serve as a logistical corridor for the rally caravan on January 10 before it departs for Nador. Sahrawi people have not consented to these events taking place in their territory.

Why it mattersThe Dakar Rally's route through occupied Western Sahara normalizes Morocco's control over the territory, affecting the Sahrawi people who have not consented to these events.

Morocco commits to Western Sahara ceasefire after UN mission ends

The UN Security Council failed to extend the 35-year-old peacekeeping mission after vetoes from the US and Russia.

Morocco stated on Wednesday that it will uphold the ceasefire in Western Sahara, despite the end of the United Nations peacekeeping mission. The UN Security Council could not agree on an extension for the 35-year-old mission, with both the US and Russia vetoing competing resolutions. King Mohammed VI ordered officials to protect Morocco’s territorial integrity and national security.

The UN Mission for the Referendum in Western Sahara (MINURSO) was deployed in 1988 to monitor a referendum that never occurred. The territory of Western Sahara is claimed by both Morocco and the pro-independence Polisario Front, which is backed by Algeria. Rabat welcomed the mission's end, while Algeria and the Polisario Front expressed regret and concern respectively. UN Secretary General Antonio Guterres warned of an escalation risk.

The UN classifies Western Sahara as a non-self-governing territory. Morocco administers most of the disputed area, except for a small “free zone” east of a Moroccan-built sand wall. An armed conflict between Morocco and the Polisario Front lasted over 15 years after Morocco annexed the territory in 1975, claiming more than 16,000 lives.

Why it mattersMorocco and the Polisario Front, backed by Algeria, both claim the territory of Western Sahara, and the end of the UN peacekeeping mission raises concerns about renewed conflict.

Financial Times warns of Western Sahara escalation risk

UN mission's mandate ends after 35 years, raising fears of military confrontation and uncertainty.

The Financial Times warned on Tuesday, November 3, 2026, that Western Sahara faces unprecedented uncertainty following the end of the UN's MINURSO mission after 35 years. The British newspaper reported that the UN Security Council's failure to renew the mandate, due to US-Russia differences, could lead to a larger military confrontation in a region already experiencing rising tensions and an arms race.

Ricardo Fabiani of the International Crisis Group cautioned that withdrawing UN observers from the buffer zone could trigger an uncontrollable clash from any military miscalculation. Moroccan MP Lahcen Haddad called the mission's end a "gift from heaven," believing Morocco can advance its autonomy plan without UN interference, while Sahrawi presidential advisor Oubbi Bashraya Bashir stated the Polisario Front will continue its struggle for self-determination.

UN Secretary-General António Guterres stressed that the mission's end does not negate the Sahrawi people's right to self-determination but expressed concern over intensified hostilities. The Financial Times cited Fabiani's warning that Morocco might advance into the buffer zone, potentially provoking an escalation with unpredictable consequences.

Why it mattersWestern Sahara faces increased risk of military confrontation and uncertainty following the end of the UN's MINURSO mission.
