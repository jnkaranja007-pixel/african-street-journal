Colonel Rémy Ayayos Ikounga Dikanono named deputy Grand Master of Congo's Masonic Lodge

The appointment follows the dismissal of Jean-Dominique Okemba in April, with a close associate of Edgar Nguesso taking the role.

Colonel Major Rémy Ayayos Ikounga Dikanono has been appointed the new deputy Grand Master of the Grande Loge du Congo. The ceremony took place on October 24-25 in Brazzaville during the organization's general assembly. Ikounga Dikanono, who is close to Edgar Nguesso, replaces Jean-Dominique Okemba, who was dismissed in April.

The new deputy Grand Master also serves as the director of the presidential domain and has gained influence within Congolese Freemasonry. He previously chaired the football club L'Athlétic Club Léopards de Dolisie from 2008 to 2017. His installation was overseen by Claude Dohou, deputy Grand Master of the Grande Loge nationale française.

The Grande Loge du Congo is undergoing a reshuffling, with younger Freemasons advancing. Maixent Raoul Ominga, director-general of the national oil company, was named provincial Grand Master for the North. Lawyer Alexis Vincent Gomes remains pro Grand Master.

Why it mattersColonel Rémy Ayayos Ikounga Dikanono's appointment as deputy Grand Master of the Grande Loge du Congo affects the leadership and internal dynamics of a prominent Masonic obedience in the country.

Congolese civil society seeks say on timber revenue

A new law requires 15% of felled timber to go to the state, but implementation details remain unclear.

Congolese civil society organizations met in Brazzaville from October 29-31, 2026, to scrutinize a new law enacted in October 2024. This law mandates that 15% of all timber felled in the Republic of Congo's forests will directly benefit the state. However, key aspects regarding the management, distribution, and beneficiaries of this state share are yet to be defined in three pending application texts.

The law, stemming from the 2020 Forest Code, transforms the state from a mere tax collector into a co-owner of timber harvests. Forests cover approximately 65% of the Republic of Congo's territory, making timber a significant national resource. Civil society groups, including the Congolese Observatory for Human Rights (OCDH), argue that the law was adopted without sufficient transparency or inclusivity, leaving local communities and indigenous populations uncertain about their share.

Nina Cynthia Kiyindou of OCDH stated that while some representatives participated in the multi-actor text review group (GETMA), broader civil society was not involved. These groups are now preparing recommendations to influence the upcoming application texts, aiming to ensure clarity on how the 15% timber share will be calculated, managed, and utilized for the benefit of Congolese citizens living in forest-dependent communities.

Why it mattersThe implementation of a new law that allocates 15% of felled timber to the state will determine if the revenue directly benefits Congolese citizens living in forest areas.
