Reserve Bank reports Zimbabwe Gold inflation at 2.9% in August

Zimbabwe's annual Zimbabwe Gold (ZiG) inflation rate dropped to 2.9% in August, marking the lowest single-digit inflation recorded since 1980.

The Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe (RBZ) announced that the annual ZiG inflation rate fell to 2.9% in August, a 0.3 percentage-point decline from July's 3.2%. This figure represents the lowest single-digit inflation outturn since 1980, according to the central bank.

Central bank governor John Mushayavanhu stated that the low ZiG inflation demonstrates the effectiveness of prudent policies in achieving price, currency, and exchange rate stability. The RBZ noted that the muted impact of a global oil price shock on Zimbabwe's inflation benefited from anchored inflation expectations, unlike other sub-Saharan African countries that saw increases of two to four percentage points.

The central bank highlighted that prices of domestic goods and services have increased at a similar pace in both ZiG and US dollars since August 2025. This implies businesses should be indifferent to pricing and accepting payments in either currency, supporting predictability for business planning and investment, which is critical for wider ZiG use in the multi-currency environment.

Why it mattersBusinesses and consumers in Zimbabwe are directly affected by the low inflation rate, which aims to provide greater predictability in pricing and payment options between ZiG and US dollars.

RBZ reports 91% surge in capital outflows to US$866 million

Zimbabwe's capital outflows nearly doubled in the first half of 2026, driven by increased external debt repayments and disinvestments, according to the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe.

Zimbabwe experienced a significant increase in capital outflows, with outward remittances surging 91% to US$866 million in the first half of 2026, up from US$453,6 million during the same period last year. This rise was primarily due to companies stepping up external debt repayments, as reported by the Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe (RBZ) in its mid-term monetary policy review.

The RBZ also noted a 162,1% increase in disinvestments, reaching US165,5 million, and a 74,7% rise in foreign investment outflows to US79,6 million. External loan repayments grew by 80% to US$621 million. These figures indicate that companies are moving more capital offshore rather than reinvesting it domestically, at a time when the country aims to attract foreign direct investment.

Total foreign payments processed through banks increased by 44,9% to US7,3 billion in the first six months of 2026, compared to US5 billion in the corresponding period of 2025. Trade-related payments accounted for 81% of this total, reflecting a heavy reliance on imported goods and services, with 37% directed towards raw materials, intermediate goods, and capital goods.

Why it mattersThe surge in capital outflows affects businesses by reducing available funds for domestic investment and potentially impacting the country's economic stability.

IMF reports Zimbabwe's gold incentive spending exceeds budget by US$125 million

Zimbabwe's gold incentive scheme for artisanal miners spent US$141 million by April, nearly nine times its annual budget of US$16 million, according to the International Monetary.

Zimbabwe's gold incentive program for artisanal miners has significantly overshot its budget, reaching US$141 million by the end of April. This figure is US$125 million above the US$16 million allocated for the entire year, as reported by the International Monetary Fund (IMF) in its first review under Zimbabwe’s Staff-Monitored Programme.

The incentives, introduced in 2021 to combat gold smuggling, consumed US$118 million in the first quarter alone. The IMF highlighted this fiscal overrun as a major challenge, alongside other spending pressures like front-loaded pension expenditures and capital spending that included a US$95 million net repayment of unaudited arrears.

This situation presents a policy dilemma for the government: cutting incentives might increase smuggling, while maintaining current spending levels risks further widening the gap between planned and actual expenditures. The IMF noted that protected social spending also fell US$84 million below the agreed program floor.

Why it mattersThe overspending on gold incentives impacts the government's ability to manage its finances and meet other critical obligations, affecting all citizens through fiscal instability.

Chery Zimbabwe expands SUV lineup for diverse motorist needs

Chery Zimbabwe now offers a wider selection of SUVs, including the Tiggo 4 Cross LIT CVT 1.5, Tiggo 7 CSH, Tiggo 7 Pro Max, and the flagship Tiggo 9 2.0T Vanguard AWD.

Chery Zimbabwe has expanded its range of SUVs, aiming to meet varied motorist demands for style, value, comfort, technology, and long-term confidence. The new lineup includes models designed for different lifestyles and budgets, from urban commuters to off-road enthusiasts and executives, according to Chery Zimbabwe.

The Tiggo 4 Cross LIT CVT 1.5 is presented as a practical and economical compact SUV for city driving and daily commutes, while the Tiggo 7 CSH focuses on efficiency and a refined driving experience for business or family travel. These models aim to provide SUV practicality without being oversized, offering a balance of performance and fuel efficiency.

For those seeking more luxury, the Tiggo 7 Pro Max offers premium features and advanced technology. The top-tier Tiggo 9 2.0T Vanguard AWD is positioned as a bold and capable premium SUV for families, executives, and adventure-minded drivers, providing space, power, comfort, and all-wheel-drive confidence.

Why it mattersZimbabwean motorists now have more options when purchasing an SUV, with models catering to different budgets and lifestyle needs.

Afreximbank posts 30% profit growth in first half of 2026

The African Export-Import Bank reported a significant increase in net income, reaching US534,7million, driven by expanded lending and stronger interest income.

Afreximbank announced a 30% rise in net income for the first six months of 2026, reaching US534,7million, up from US412,7 million in the same period last year. This growth was attributed to increased lending activities and higher interest income, despite ongoing inflationary pressures, according to the bank.

The bank's total assets and contingencies grew by 7,8% to US52,3billion from US48,5 billion at the end of 2025, primarily due to expanding lending. Net loans and advances increased by 5,7% to US35,4billion from US33,5 billion. Asset quality improved, with the non-performing loan ratio decreasing to 2,20% from 2,43% at year-end 2025.

Shareholders’ funds increased to US8,5billion from US8,4 billion, supported by internally generated profits and US13,9 million in new equity. Net interest income climbed 22% to US1,0billion from US840 million, while fee and commission income rose 15% to US71,1million from US61,9 million, reflecting higher earnings from guarantees, letters of credit, and advisory services.

Why it mattersThis financial performance indicates the bank's capacity to support trade and economic development, which can impact businesses and economies across Africa and the Caribbean.
