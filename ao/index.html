President Lourenço inaugurates Luanda convention center, urges private hotel investment

President João Lourenço opened a new convention center in Luanda, emphasizing its role in attracting international events and calling for private sector investment in the.

Angola's President João Lourenço inaugurated a new convention center in Luanda, built on land that was ocean three years ago. The facility, which includes an amphitheater with three thousand seats, is intended to host international events previously held in tents and hotels, according to the President.

The new Palace of Conventions features twelve halls, four of which are still unfinished, with one hall offering five hundred seats. President Lourenço stated that the center will elevate the country's image and support business tourism, highlighting Angola's increasing attractiveness for international events.

President Lourenço appealed to private investors to develop the tourism sector, specifically hotel infrastructure, noting that Luanda has few hotels. He stressed that any investment in hospitality would be insufficient given the current needs, emphasizing the government's focus on improving the business environment to attract capital.

Why it mattersThis event affects Angolan businesses and potential investors by creating new infrastructure for international events and directly calling for private sector investment in the hospitality industry.

Angola invites Brazilian firms to invest in local production

Angolan Secretary of State for Industry, Carlos Rodrigues, urged Brazilian businesses to establish production facilities in Angola, aiming to leverage the country as a gateway to.

Carlos Rodrigues, speaking at the Angola-Brazil Business Meeting 2026 in Luanda, encouraged Brazilian companies to consider Angola for investment, production, and as a platform for expanding into the African market. He stated the Angolan executive is ready to collaborate with Brazilian entrepreneurs to strengthen bilateral relations, building on decades of commercial ties.

Rodrigues emphasized Angola's desire for Brazilian companies to shift from solely trading to producing goods within the country. He highlighted the potential for Brazilian firms to process raw materials and form partnerships to develop local capacity, with the goal of reaching other African markets.

Angola is particularly interested in Brazil's assistance in developing agro-industrial complexes, combining agricultural production, research, technology, mechanization, logistics, and industrial processing. Rodrigues noted extensive business opportunities in areas like cereal, fruit, and coffee processing, edible oil production, animal protein transformation, and food industry equipment.

Why it mattersBrazilian businesses are affected by the Angolan government's direct invitation to invest in local production and agro-industrial development.

Angola enrolls unregistered children in schools after 50 years

The Ministry of Education has mandated public and private schools to enroll children without identification documents for the upcoming academic year, addressing a long-standing.

Angola's Ministry of Education has directed all public and private schools, from primary to secondary levels, to accept children for enrollment even if they lack identification documents. This measure, announced in a dispatch, responds to a large influx of school-aged children seeking enrollment for the next academic year without proper identification.

The Ministry's decision aims to prevent the non-presentation of documents from increasing the number of citizens outside the education system. Official data from 2024 indicated that about 60% of children in the country were not registered, highlighting the scale of the issue the new policy addresses.

Schools are now required to identify children without documents by the end of October 2026 and communicate this information to the Ministry of Justice and Human Rights' Identification, Records, and Notary Directorate. This directorate will then visit schools to register the children, while schools also educate families on the importance of civil registration and obtaining identity cards.

Why it mattersThis policy directly affects unregistered children by granting them access to education and their families by requiring them to engage with civil registration processes.

UNITA invite limits political activist's travel in Africa

An independent political activist accepted an invitation from UNITA to fill one of four vacancies on the CNE, which will restrict his political activities across Africa.

A political activist has accepted an invitation from UNITA to serve as an independent member on the CNE, filling one of four available positions. This new role will unfortunately limit his regular political travels and engagements across the continent, particularly in the Sahel region.

The activist has been critical of leaders who sell public assets to private entities, viewing the privatization of public companies as a "market crime" rather than market economy. He argues that countries with histories of slavery and colonialism, lacking developed education systems, should not privatize major public enterprises that serve as beacons for public policies in education, health, and entrepreneurship.

His work has focused on advocating for the transformation of African wealth, specifically rare earths, within each country. The new commitment means he will no longer be able to regularly visit countries like Senegal, Burkina Faso, Mali, Niger, Mozambique, and Malawi to the same extent as before.

Why it mattersThe activist's new role will limit his ability to advocate against the privatization of public companies, which could affect the economic landscape for citizens.

Expo Cabinda Fails to Attract Investment, Creates Temporary Jobs

The Expo Cabinda event, intended to boost development, has instead been criticized for excluding local businesses, wasting public funds, and generating only temporary employment.

The Expo Cabinda, held at the Tafe Multipurpose Pavilion, has been described as a political illusion masking local governance failures and deep disparities. While large companies, many linked to political clientelism, showcased their portfolios, small producers in Cabinda were marginalized or excluded due to prohibitive participation costs, according to Folha 8. The event failed to foster Cabinda's real economy, instead legitimizing the monopoly of existing elites.

Millions of kwanzas were reportedly spent on temporary infrastructure, government advertising, and ceremonial banquets, representing a misdirection of public funds. This spending occurred in a territory where basic medicines are scarce, youth unemployment is high, and water and electricity supply remain critical issues. The Expo Cabinda, lasting only a few days, stands in contrast to the year-round misery in surrounding neighborhoods.

The event has not attracted serious external investment, with critics noting that investors are unlikely to be swayed by decorated pavilions when the region faces impassable roads and stifling bureaucracy. Memoranda of understanding signed at the expo reportedly serve only to generate state press headlines, with no lasting impact. The jobs created were mostly temporary, precarious, and informal, limited to the setup and dismantling of stands or event-focused catering, leaving Cabinda's youth to face unemployment after the expo concludes.

Why it mattersThe people of Cabinda are affected by the Expo Cabinda's failure to create sustainable jobs, attract serious investment, and address critical issues like healthcare, unemployment, and basic services, despite significant public spending on the event.
