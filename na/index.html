Namibia sets N$18 hourly minimum for security guards from January 2027

The final stage of a three-year wage transition will lift the minimum from N$16 and cover more than 30 000 workers nationwide.

From 1 January 2027, more than 30 000 private security guards across Namibia will be paid no less than N$18 an hour, the last step in the sector's national minimum-wage transition. The floor climbed from N$13.50 in January 2025 to N$16 in January 2026.

Namibia Security Labour Forum (NSLF) said the rate applies to every type of security service, and that a 12-hour shift will bring at least N$216 before overtime, night-shift allowances and Sunday or public-holiday premiums. Employers may not take uniforms, boots, radios or patrol transport out of those minimum wages.

Government bodies, state-owned enterprises, businesses and households that buy security services were told to check their budgets and contracts before the change. The forum also wants regulations under the Security Enterprises and Security Officers Act brought in, arguing that tighter oversight would weed out non-compliant firms and raise training standards.

Why it mattersCompanies and public bodies that hire guards must fund at least N$18 an hour from 1 January 2027, and the NSLF has urged them to revise their contracts before then.

Namibia's EU exports rise more than 17% to N$17.6 billion

Exports to the bloc supported an estimated 40 000 direct jobs in 2025, a study presented in Windhoek found, with the EU now taking 19.4% of the country's total exports.

Namibia shipped more than N$17.6 billion of goods to the European Union in 2025, up over 17% from about N$15 billion a year earlier, an independent study presented at a Windhoek trade workshop on Monday found. That made the EU the country's third-largest export market, with 19.4% of total exports.

Working on the figures, EU Ambassador to Namibia Ana Beatriz Martins said the trade sustained an estimated 40 000 direct jobs across fisheries, farming, food processing and other productive sectors. The European market takes over half of Namibia's fish, fruit and vegetable exports and charcoal, and more than 80% of its meat exports.

The EU–Southern African Development Community Economic Partnership Agreement, which turns 10 this year, gives qualifying Namibian goods duty-free, quota-free entry into Europe. Martins said the bloc's new EPA Facility is meant to ease trade constraints and help small and medium-sized firms use the deal. Trade executive director Jerobeam Shaanika urged the country to move from raw materials to higher-value products.

Why it mattersWorkers in fisheries, agriculture and food processing are among the estimated 40 000 direct jobs supported by exports to Europe.

Namibia vehicle sales fall 6.2% to 1,188 in October

Toyota registrations dropped by 106 from a year earlier, ending the market’s 18-month run of annual growth despite gains across other brands.

Namibia sold 1,188 new vehicles in October, down 6.2% from a year earlier and 20.7% from September’s 1,498, according to Simonis Storm Securities. The annual fall ended 18 consecutive months of growth. Sales in the first 10 months of 2026 were still up 10.6% to 13,453.

Toyota registrations fell to 637 from 743 a year earlier, a loss of 106 vehicles that exceeded the market’s total year-on-year decline of 79. All other brands combined rose 5.2% to 551, while Toyota’s market share dropped to 53.6% from 58.6%. Simonis Storm linked much of Toyota’s fall to weaker registrations of the Urban Cruiser, Starlet and Vitz, which together declined to 46 from 111.

Passenger vehicle registrations fell 10.6% to 490 and light commercial vehicles declined 2.7% to 619. Seasonally adjusted sales reached 1,179, their lowest level since February 2026; registrations outside Windhoek were down 2.3% in October, compared with an 8.2% fall in the capital.

Why it mattersSimonis Storm’s October figures show vehicle sellers facing lower monthly volumes, while first-10-month sales remained 10.6% higher at 13,453 units.

Telecom Namibia reports equipment thefts at northern sites

The break-ins damaged infrastructure at sites in Oshakati, Ongwediva and Ondangwa, disrupting voice and data services, the company said.

Telecom Namibia recorded thefts and break-ins at telecommunication sites in Oshakati, Ongwediva and Ondangwa from 13 November and over the weekend, the company said. The incidents damaged infrastructure and equipment at several sites.

Spokesperson Ndapewa Nashila said in a statement released on Tuesday that affected locations included Oshakati Extension 16, Ekuku, Ehenye, Okaku and Efidi, as well as three sites at Evululuko and the Omashaka base station in Ondangwa. She said the disruption affects voice and data services used by emergency responders, businesses, schools, health facilities and residents.

Telecom Namibia urged residents to stay vigilant and report concerns, warning that similar sites could be targeted. The statement did not detail the value of stolen equipment or repairs.

Why it mattersEmergency responders, businesses, schools, health facilities and residents in the three towns face disruption to voice and data services.

Business · 2026-11-18

Gondwana shares begin trading on Namibia’s OTC market at N$9.60

The tourism group’s shares are available through four stockbrokers, but its OTC quotation does not raise new capital and trades can be infrequent.

Gondwana Holdings shares began trading on the Namibia Securities Exchange’s Over-the-Counter Market on 13 November, allowing investors to buy or sell through four participating stockbrokers. The last recorded trade was on 19 October at N$9.60 a share.

The OTC quotation makes existing shares easier to trade through brokers, but Gondwana is not raising new money and its shares trade less often than those on the exchange’s main board. The company says buying N$10,000 of shares costs N$75 and selling the same amount costs N$55; broker fees may differ.

Gondwana reported revenue of N$625.3 million for the six months to August 2026, up 14%, while bed occupancy remained at 50.3%. Management said occupancy was below expectations, citing geopolitical tensions, higher aviation fuel costs and Namibia’s visa regime.

Why it mattersInvestors pay N$75 to buy N$10,000 of shares under Gondwana’s example, while infrequent trading can delay a sale.
