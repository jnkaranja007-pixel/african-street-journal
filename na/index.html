Dangote launches N$41.6 billion pipeline project in Walvis Bay

Nigerian billionaire Aliko Dangote promises cheaper fuel for Namibia with a new N$41.6 billion pipeline project.

Nigerian businessman Aliko Dangote launched a N$41.6 billion regional pipeline project near Walvis Bay yesterday. The project by Dangote Industries Limited aims to create a petroleum storage and distribution facility with pipeline infrastructure to improve fuel access across Namibia and landlocked regional countries.

Dangote told Namibians that the project, which includes a 600 million litre storage facility for diesel and petrol, will reduce fuel supply costs. He assured President Netumbo-Nandi Ndaitwah that petroleum prices would become much cheaper. The development also features truck-loading facilities and an approximately 1,700-kilometre regional pipeline network.

The pipeline is planned to extend from Walvis Bay to Otjiwarongo, Rundu, and Francistown, Botswana, serving Botswana, Zambia, Zimbabwe, Malawi, and parts of the Democratic Republic of Congo. President Ndaitwah revealed she personally intervened to prevent bureaucracy from causing Namibia to lose this strategic investment.

Why it mattersNamibians may see reduced fuel prices due to the new N$41.6 billion pipeline and storage project.

Namibia launches state-owned air ambulance service

The new service aims to reduce travel time for critically ill patients from remote areas to hospitals.

Namibia has launched its first state-owned air ambulance service to transport critically ill and injured patients from remote areas to referral hospitals. Vice president Lucia Witbooi officially launched the service in Windhoek on Wednesday, stating it would address challenges posed by long distances. The service includes a Learjet air ambulance equipped with advanced medical equipment and will collaborate with police and defence forces for helicopter evacuations.

Witbooi said the service would connect patients in remote areas to advanced medical care, reducing gruelling road journeys to minutes or a few hours. Specialised medical personnel will provide critical care during transfers, facilitated by dedicated emergency service centres at referral hospitals. The Government Air Transport Services, along with ministries of works, transport, health, and social services, will provide air emergency units and pilots.

The air ambulance service complements Namibia’s existing ground fleet and strengthens referral pathways between hospitals. Witbooi warned that success depends on sustained funding, aircraft maintenance, training, and safety standards, calling for systems to monitor performance and ensure accountability.

Why it mattersCritically ill and injured patients in remote areas of Namibia will now have faster access to advanced medical care.

Windhoek starts utility disconnections for unpaid bills

Human rights lawyer Norman Tjombe questions the legality of cutting off water and electricity to residents and businesses with outstanding municipal accounts.

The City of Windhoek began disconnecting water and electricity services on Thursday for residents and businesses with outstanding municipal bills. The municipality stated that the disconnections, which affect customers across all suburbs, are part of its credit control policy and municipal laws. Customers are urged to settle their accounts or make payment arrangements to avoid losing essential services.

Human rights lawyer Norman Tjombe challenged the legality of the mass disconnections, warning that cutting off water could violate constitutional rights and expose vulnerable populations to health risks like cholera and typhoid. He noted the disconnections are insensitive given the financial pressures from the foot-and-mouth disease outbreak, which has impacted rural livelihoods and employment in the meat industry.

The municipality's spokesperson, Lydia Amutenya, stated the disconnections were prompted by continued non-payment and growing debt. Namibia's Water Resources Management Act recognizes equitable access to safe drinking water as a basic human right, while local authorities also have powers to disconnect services for non-payment.

Why it mattersResidents and businesses in Windhoek face potential loss of water and electricity due to unpaid municipal bills, with a lawyer raising concerns about human rights and health impacts.

Namibia beef industry faces N$2.4 billion FMD loss

The foot-and-mouth disease outbreak threatens export access and processing investments, impacting jobs and households.

Namibia's foot-and-mouth disease (FMD) outbreak could cost the livestock industry an estimated N$2.4 billion over six months. The Namibia Agricultural Union (NAU) estimates the outbreak is costing the agricultural value chain approximately N$13 million daily, or nearly N$400 million monthly. This disruption affects cattle farms, processing plants, transport operators, and financial institutions.

NAU president Thinus Pretorius stated that the economic consequences extend beyond livestock producers, warning that prolonged market closures threaten jobs and households. The NAU's six-month assessment includes N$1.382 billion in primary production expenses and debt repayments, N$378 million in abattoir fixed costs, and N$331 million in reduced export slaughtering.

Further estimated losses include N$81 million in auction commissions and N$114 million in livestock transport revenue. For commercial producers south of the Veterinary Cordon Fence, N$230 million monthly is needed for operating expenses and debt repayments. The outbreak also threatens Namibia's reputation for meeting international meat safety standards and its access to the European Union market.

Why it mattersThe FMD outbreak is costing Namibia's agricultural sector N$2.4 billion over six months, affecting jobs and households.

Namibia road funding gap narrows to N$2.3 billion

Road Fund Administration adjusts user charges and seeks market borrowing to fund critical projects.

Namibia's road maintenance funding shortfall has decreased from N$3.6 billion to approximately N$2.3 billion. Road Fund Administration CEO Ali Ipinge stated yesterday that this reduction is still insufficient to maintain the nation's roads adequately. The figures were shared at a stakeholder consultation for the RFA's 2027/28–2031/32 Business Plan held at the Windhoek Country Club Resort.

Progress in narrowing the gap stems from a 10% to 20% adjustment on various Road User Charges, with 30 cents of a N$1.60 per litre fuel levy increase now implemented. This 30 cents is specifically allocated to 14 government priority road projects, including rural access and feeder roads connecting communities to essential services. The RFA is also seeking N$1.5 billion in market borrowing against this levy to accelerate these priority works.

The RFA has submitted a request for over N$1 billion in flood damage assistance to the finance ministry, following the January to April 2025 floods that affected local authorities. While a government response is pending, some allocations have been made for flood damage repairs. Challenges persist, including rising maintenance costs, aging infrastructure, and climate-related events, prompting research into alternative funding and resilient road materials.

Why it mattersNamibians face continued challenges with road conditions due to insufficient maintenance funding, impacting travel and access to services.
