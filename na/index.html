FMD costs Namibia's livestock industry N$150 million weekly

Foot-and-mouth disease restrictions disrupt Southern African Development Community trade, threatening farmers' incomes and export markets.

Namibia's livestock industry is losing an estimated N$150 million weekly due to foot-and-mouth disease (FMD), according to Namibia Agricultural Union (NAU) President Thinus Pretorius. The disease and associated movement restrictions are disrupting trade across the Southern African Development Community (SADC), impacting farmers' incomes, employment, and access to export markets. Namibia's weekly losses include N$118 million from cattle, N$26 million from sheep, and N$6 million from goats.

Farmers continue to incur operational expenses for salaries, fodder, and fuel while livestock marketing remains disrupted. Pretorius stated that the regional cattle population of approximately 29 million animals is exposed to the economic consequences of FMD. The Namibian government has allocated N$180 million to combat the disease, an amount Pretorius contrasted with the industry's estimated monthly losses of N$655 million. Approximately 265,000 jobs are at stake.

Restoring export market access could impact economic growth by an estimated 5% to 6%. Namibia's beef exports, approximately 94% of which go to markets like the European Union and Norway requiring FMD-free status without vaccination, are particularly vulnerable. The country's feedlot industry has grown significantly, with 63 feedlots registered with Meatco, supported by favourable export conditions that previously allowed Namibian livestock prices to exceed South African prices by up to 30%.

Why it mattersNamibian farmers face significant financial losses and job insecurity due to FMD restrictions impacting livestock sales and export market access.

Namibian corporate debt rises to N$219.3 billion

State-owned enterprises also saw their debt increase by N$469 million in the first half of 2026.

Namibia's corporate debt stock grew by 10.3% to N$219.3 billion by the end of June 2026, up from N$198.9 billion in December 2025. This increase was primarily driven by higher foreign corporate debt, particularly intercompany loans for direct investment in the mining sector. State-owned enterprises (SOEs) also saw their combined debt rise by N$469 million to N$11.12 billion over the same six-month period.

The Bank of Namibia and NAMFISA's October 2026 Financial Stability Report noted that foreign SOE debt accounted for N$9.37 billion of the total SOE debt. The overall rise in corporate and SOE borrowing was also influenced by increased use of trade credit and higher non-resident deposits with domestic banks. Corporate debt now represents 74.5% of Namibia's nominal GDP, up from 69.8% at the end of 2025.

The report highlighted that a sustained depreciation of the Namibia Dollar could increase the cost of servicing foreign-denominated debt, potentially weakening corporate balance sheets. The financial stability risks for Namibia are tilted to the downside, with climate change, FMD, and cyber threats also identified as key concerns.

Why it mattersNamibian businesses and state-owned enterprises are increasing their debt, with a significant portion owed to foreign creditors, raising concerns about exchange rate risk.

Dangote launches Walvis Bay pipeline project

Namibia's fuel prices rise as the Dangote Namibia Tank Farm and Regional Pipeline Project officially opens.

President Netumbo Nandi-Ndaitwah launched the Dangote Namibia Tank Farm and Regional Pipeline Project near Walvis Bay on Wednesday, November 4. This launch coincides with a fuel price increase, with petrol rising by N$2 per litre and diesel by N$3. Petrol 95 now costs N$28.58 per litre in Walvis Bay, diesel 50ppm is N$30.86, and diesel 10ppm is N$30.96.

The Minister of Industries, Mines and Energy, Modestus Amutse, attributed the price hike to a 40% rise in shipping costs and a 64% increase in vessel waiting costs, compounded by exchange-rate pressures. The new project includes a storage facility for 1.6 million barrels of fuel, dispensing tanks, a truck port, and a 16-kilometre pipeline.

Dangote's engagement with Namibia began on June 19, 2025, exploring investment opportunities, including his Nigerian refinery's capacity. The project aims to create a regional distribution hub, potentially generating revenue from storage, pipeline tariffs, and logistics services, though infrastructure alone does not guarantee lower fuel prices.

Why it mattersNamibian motorists face higher fuel costs due to increased shipping and waiting expenses, despite the launch of a new fuel infrastructure project.

Namibia private sector credit grows by N$5.84 billion

Nominal credit book reaches N$128.2 billion, but real growth is minimal due to inflation.

Namibia's private sector credit increased by N$5.84 billion in the first nine months of 2026, a 39% rise from the same period last year. This growth brought the total credit book to N$128.2 billion by September, with N$1.06 billion added in that month alone. Businesses borrowed N$656.8 million and households N$403.5 million during September.

Almandro Jansen, an economist at Simonis Storm, noted that while nominal credit grew by 5.6% annually in September, real growth after accounting for 5.4% inflation was only 0.2%. He stated that much of the borrowing increase reflects higher prices, not a significant expansion in real activity. Corporate credit growth accelerated to 6.7% in September.

Jansen cautioned that sustained growth is needed to confirm a business investment recovery, especially with drought, foot and mouth disease, and fuel costs impacting agriculture and transport. Household credit growth remained at 4.9% annually, but contracted by 0.5% in real terms for the second consecutive month.

Why it mattersBusinesses and households in Namibia are borrowing more, but inflation is eroding the real value of this credit.

Business · 2026-11-04

Namibia fuel prices jump N$2 to N$3 per litre

Petrol 95 rises by N$2, diesel by N$3 as international market pressures drive up costs.

Namibians will pay more for fuel starting in November after Minister of Mines and Energy Modestus Amutse announced price hikes. Petrol 95 will increase by N$2 per litre. Diesel 50ppm and Diesel 10ppm will each rise by N$3 per litre.

The minister attributed the surge to rising international market pressures. This latest increase comes as a stagnant economy means salaries are unlikely to rise, impacting basic living costs for citizens.

The fuel pump prices in Walvis Bay are affected by this change. The Ministry of Mines and Energy did not immediately state when the new prices would take effect.

Why it mattersNamibians face higher costs for transportation and goods due to increased fuel prices.
