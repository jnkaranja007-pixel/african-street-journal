Namibia state firms owe N$11.12 billion

Taxpayers are exposed to a further N$11.8 billion in state-guaranteed debt, straining the national budget.

Namibia's state-owned enterprises have accumulated N$11.12 billion in debt, with taxpayers facing an additional N$11.8 billion in state-guaranteed liabilities. This debt burden represents about 4% of the country's GDP, creating significant fiscal pressure and a reliance on government bailouts.

Minister of Works and Transport Veikko Nekundi dismissed five Namibia Airports Company (NAC) board members in August 2026 following an audit that found governance and procurement irregularities. These included the sale of 19 company vehicles to managers at reduced prices and procurement issues linked to executive contracts. NAC CEO Bisey /Uirab is challenging the dismissals in the High Court.

The Public Enterprises Governance Act requires financial misconduct to be treated with statutory and criminal liability, but boards are often replaced without personal consequence. This pattern mirrors the collapse of Air Namibia, which was liquidated in 2021 after years of state bailouts.

Why it mattersNamibian taxpayers are directly exposed to billions in debt and guaranteed liabilities from poorly performing state-owned enterprises.

US Democrats' Congress takeover puts Namibia's minerals and AGOA in focus

Democratic control of both US chambers could reshape Washington's trade and investment priorities toward Africa, including Namibia's mineral wealth.

The Democratic Party's takeover of both chambers of the United States Congress in the November 3 midterm elections could change Washington's trade, investment and development priorities toward Africa. The shift also limits President Donald Trump's ability to advance his legislative agenda during the remaining two years of his presidency. For Namibia, the changes could affect its critical minerals, its emerging energy industry and its access to the American market, including the African Growth and Opportunity Act (AGOA).

Opposition Member of Parliament Veraruhe Kandorozu said the Democratic resurgence could open room for renewed American engagement with Africa in trade, development and democratic governance. He said African economies have benefited from American engagement through AGOA, development assistance and trade, and that some of those gains faced setbacks under President Donald Trump and the Republican administration.

US Ambassador to Namibia John Giordano has been encouraging American investors to look at the country's mineral potential, especially uranium and rare earth elements. In April 2026 he told Bloomberg that uranium is the big prize for both countries, linking US interest to rising nuclear power demand, including from artificial intelligence data centres. At an August reception ahead of the Namibia Mining Expo, he said the US wants a full value-chain approach that extends investment beyond extraction into refining, processing, manufacturing and recycling.

Why it mattersNamibian mining and energy firms seeking American capital, and exporters relying on AGOA access, face possible changes in US policy under the new Congress.

Prosecutor seeks 28 years for girlfriend's killer in Windhoek

Tjizake Murangi, who admitted stabbing his former girlfriend Frieda Amadhila, faces a sentence decision on 27 November.

A prosecutor asked the Windhoek High Court for a 28-year effective prison term for Tjizake Murangi, 25, a fitness instructor who admitted murdering his former girlfriend. State advocate Ian Malumani made the suggestion on the charges of housebreaking with intent to murder and murder. Defence lawyer Mbanga Siyomunji had proposed 20 years.

Murangi admitted breaking into the Windhoek flat of Frieda Amadhila, 29, a bank employee, on the night of 7 to 8 April 2024 and killing her. The state alleged she was stabbed and cut at least 15 times. Murangi also admitted assaulting her in January 2024, defrauding her and her sister in a communal land sale, and stealing four head of cattle.

Judge Claudia Claasen postponed sentencing to 27 November after final oral arguments. Murangi testified in mitigation that he realised he could have reacted differently, and said he was not claiming self-defence.

Why it mattersMurangi's sentence will decide how long he is jailed for killing Amadhila, a bank employee, and for the fraud and cattle theft he admitted against her and her sister.

Namibia explores Chad as new market for table grapes

Namibia's High Commissioner to Chad, Walde Ndevashiya, is working to find Chadian buyers for Namibian table grapes and link them with local exporters.

Namibia is exploring whether it can export its table grapes to Chad, as part of a wider push to open new African markets for local farm produce. Walde Ndevashiya, Namibia's High Commissioner to Nigeria, Chad and Cameroon, says the mission is identifying potential buyers and distributors in Chad and connecting them with Namibian grape producers and exporters.

Ndevashiya said Namibia has built a reputation for quality agricultural products, including table grapes, which already have export markets. He said the mission is focusing on credible Chadian importers, wholesalers, supermarket chains and fresh-produce distributors, and will work with the Namibia Investment Promotion and Development Board (NIPDB), farming groups and the private sector. He presented his Letters of Credence to Chadian President Marshal Mahamat Idriss Déby Itno on 19 October 2026.

Several practical steps must be settled before any shipment leaves, including phytosanitary certification, customs procedures, import permits, product standards and tariffs. Transport and cold-chain arrangements also need to be agreed so the grapes arrive in good condition at competitive prices. Ndevashiya said success will be judged by actual orders and shipments, not by the number of meetings held. On 14 October 2026 he also met Gademi Abderrahmane, president of Chad's Chamber of Commerce, Industry, Agriculture, Mines and Crafts, to discuss a possible memorandum of understanding between the two chambers.

Why it mattersNamibian table grape producers and exporters would gain access to Chadian importers, wholesalers and distributors if the trade is established.

Presidency says Dangote terminal launch is not environmental approval

Namibia's Presidency says President Nandi-Ndaitwah's attendance at the N$41-billion fuel terminal launch does not replace the required environmental assessment.

President Netumbo Nandi-Ndaitwah attended the launch of the N$41-billion Aliko Dangote fuel terminal, but the Office of the President says that did not amount to environmental approval. The office told The Namibian on Sunday that the project has yet to go through the required environmental assessment, public consultation and regulatory approval.

Presidential spokesperson Jonas Mbambo said the president's participation at the Farm 58 site in Walvis Bay expressed government support for strategic investment. He said it should not be read as environmental authorisation or an exemption from statutory requirements. The project, which Dangote Group began building last week, is designed to store at least 1.6 million barrels of petrol and diesel.

Mbambo said the project must comply with the Environmental Management Act of 2007 and that government support does not replace those processes or predetermine their outcomes. The Presidency also said Namibia faces pressing unemployment and limited economic opportunities, and that such investments could create jobs and strengthen infrastructure.

Why it mattersThe terminal must still pass environmental assessment and public consultation under the Environmental Management Act of 2007 before it can be approved, and the project is designed to supply several southern African countries with fuel.
