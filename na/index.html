Namibia finance minister to review N$8.5bn development spending

The finance minister will present the 2026/2027 Mid-Year Budget Review, detailing spending, revenue collection, and government liquidity.

Namibia's N$8.5 billion development budget, revenue collection, and government liquidity will be reviewed by the finance minister on Thursday. Deloitte Namibia anticipates updates on budget implementation, revenue performance, and measures to boost government collections. The review will also clarify funding for efforts to contain the foot-and-mouth disease outbreak impacting the livestock sector.

Gerda Brand, director of Namibia tax at Deloitte, stated the review will assess budget implementation against final fiscal indicators for the year ending March 2026. She noted the finance minister previously identified implementation as a significant challenge. Deloitte expects focus on the execution of the N$8.5 billion development budget and the progress of major capital projects.

Tabitha Mugandiwa, manager of Namibia tax at Deloitte, said the minister had estimated total revenue for the 2026/2027 financial year at N$89.8 billion. The review will update on revenue collection against this projection, crucial for assessing if spending plans align with actual collections. Government liquidity, affected by VAT credit refunds, will also be addressed, with potential measures to strengthen revenue mobilization outlined.

Why it mattersInvestors will be affected by updates on government spending, revenue collection, and liquidity, which could impact economic stability and future investment.

UN says over 250 000 displaced in Yemen

The United Nations refugee agency reported a sharp increase in internal displacement since fighting reignited in Yemen in July.

More than 250 000 people have been internally displaced in Yemen since the civil war reignited, the United Nations said. The conflict surged back to life and intensified when the Houthis seized strategic territory along the Red Sea coast last month. Displacement is rising sharply across the country, which was already suffering a vast humanitarian crisis. Matthew Saltmarsh, spokesman for the UN refugee agency, stated that the numbers displaced have reached over 250 000 between August 1 and October 17.

Around half of those newly displaced are being hosted in Taiz, Yemen’s third largest city. Houthi fighters have encircled Taiz since early October, blocking the road that links it to Aden, the interim capital of the Saudi-backed government. The most immediate needs identified are shelter, food, and multi-purpose cash assistance, according to Saltmarsh.

The spokesman also noted that as of Saturday, some 3 836 new arrivals had been recorded in Djibouti, and a further 242 in Somalia, including refugees and returnees. Following a boat capsizing on the night of October 8-9, 139 bodies have been recovered, including 109 identified, while 82 survivors were receiving support.

Why it mattersOver 250 000 Yemenis are displaced and in need of shelter, food, and cash assistance due to renewed fighting.

Police arrest 55 protesters in Lisbon housing camp raid

Portuguese authorities dismantled tent encampments in the capital, detaining demonstrators protesting rising housing costs and eviction policies.

Portuguese police arrested 55 people yesterday during a dawn raid that dismantled tent camps in Lisbon's central Rossio square. Demonstrators had gathered to protest soaring housing costs and government inaction on the housing crisis. Police stated the gatherings were no longer legal and seized 228 camping structures after protesters formed a human chain.

The protesters accuse the government of failing to address the housing crisis and of pushing rental market reforms that facilitate easier evictions. According to demonstrators, approximately 30 people were injured and arrested. Some protesters indicated an intention to re-establish the camp in the coming hours, while a significant police presence remained in the city centre.

In Porto, where the movement originated earlier this month, protesters voluntarily dismantled their camp following threats of police intervention. The protests in Spain, sparked by the eviction of an elderly woman, have led to a snap general election scheduled for November 29.

Why it mattersProtesters are affected by rising housing costs and government policies that make eviction easier.

Namibia orders audits into traditional fishing funds

Minister James Sankwasa announced planned audits into traditional authorities' fishing quota funds due to unexplained withdrawals.

Namibia's Ministry of Urban and Rural Development will audit traditional authorities' fishing quota funds following concerns over unexplained withdrawals by chiefs and their relatives. Minister James Sankwasa announced the audits at the Council of Traditional Leaders meeting, warning that misuse of funds could lead to prosecution. Sankwasa stated that auditors from his ministry and the Ministry of Finance will examine the books to determine who withdrew money and for what purpose.

The minister alleged that some traditional authority councillors were unaware of transactions from accounts controlled by chiefs and their family members, with signatories often including relatives. Sankwasa did not identify specific authorities, disclose amounts, or state when the audits would begin. The announcement follows earlier public concerns about the management of fishing quota proceeds intended for traditional communities.

Fishing quotas are marketed, and proceeds are distributed to traditional authorities, with Oonani Investments Group responsible for marketing and depositing funds into the authorities' accounts. Sankwasa questioned the whereabouts of these proceeds after they reached the accounts, noting that withdrawals lacked clear explanations for specific services. The funds are intended for community needs such as electricity connections, gardens, and medical transport.

Why it mattersTraditional leaders and their communities are affected by potential misuse of fishing quota funds intended for community development.

Namibia government to cap private student funding

Education minister Sanet Steenkamp told parliament that parents must cover tuition shortfalls for private tertiary students.

Private tertiary students in Namibia may still face tuition fees shortfalls despite a government funding model, as parents will be expected to pay the difference between government contributions and actual course costs. Education minister Sanet Steenkamp informed the National Assembly that the government's initial focus was on public universities and colleges. A task force is considering how students at private institutions can benefit, with the government proposing to pay a predetermined amount towards course costs, leaving parents to cover the balance.

The minister's explanation did not confirm if the proposed arrangement had received final approval, stating it was still being presented to the treasury, the President, and Cabinet. This comes after Independent Patriots for Change MP Abed Hishoono raised concerns about private students receiving only partial funding as they prepared for examinations. Steenkamp clarified that public institution funding has specific eligibility criteria, including priority fields of study and minimum entry points.

The proposed capped contribution for private institutions contrasts with the full tuition and registration subsidies available to eligible students at public institutions. The distinction in arrangements between public and private institutions has been communicated to stakeholders, including private institutions represented on the task force.

Why it mattersPrivate tertiary students in Namibia are affected because their parents will have to pay the difference between government contributions and actual course fees.
