Namibia's pension fund has N$5.1 billion ready for projects

The Government Institutions Pension Fund (GIPF) has committed N$28.2 billion to unlisted investments, with N$23.1 billion already invested.

The Government Institutions Pension Fund (GIPF) has N$5.1 billion still available for investment in suitable Namibian projects, even as the country grapples with unemployment, a housing crisis and infrastructure gaps. Board chairperson Penda Ithindi disclosed the figures at the recent GIPF Investment Symposium.

Ithindi said the fund will not compromise its investment principles: projects must be financially viable, properly governed and sufficiently secured. He said the money belongs to thousands of Namibian pension contributors and cannot be spent to satisfy political promises.

The commentary calls for a dedicated national project preparation facility, bringing together the ministry of finance, the National Planning Commission, development finance institutions, local authorities and private-sector experts. It would identify viable projects, prepare business cases and secure approvals, with housing and urban infrastructure named as priorities.

Why it mattersDevelopers who can produce bankable housing, energy or water projects stand to gain access to the pension fund's remaining N$5.1 billion.

Namibia signs N$174m contract for Windhoek hospital site

Elderwood Trading CC and Shangsheng Construction will prepare the Havana site for the capital's first district hospital within 12 months.

The construction of Windhoek's first district hospital has moved forward with a N$174 million contract signed for site preparation. Elderwood Trading CC, in joint venture with Shangsheng Construction, will undertake bulk earthworks and install essential infrastructure services over 12 months at the Havana site. The contract covers access roads, stormwater drainage, water supply, sewerage networks, and electricity services.

Penda Ithindi, executive director for the ministry of health and social services, called the contract signing a historic milestone for Namibia's healthcare sector. He noted that Windhoek's population now exceeds half a million, making new healthcare infrastructure urgent. The new facility aims to ease pressure on Windhoek Central Hospital and Katutura Intermediate Hospital, allowing them to focus on specialised care.

Idi Itope, chief executive officer of the Central Procurement Board of Namibia (CPBN), stated that the N$174 million investment must translate into tangible development. He urged the contractor to adhere to the 12-month implementation period and maintain quality standards. The CPBN oversees the contract, which is the first phase of the hospital development, with hospital building construction to follow.

Why it mattersThe N$174 million infrastructure contract for Windhoek's first district hospital site moves the project forward, aiming to improve healthcare access for the capital's growing population.

Swakopmund wall collapse kills casual worker at rest camp

Stephanus Hamutenya died after a section of a concrete wall fell on him during demolition work at the Municipal Rest Camp in Swakopmund.

Stephanus Hamutenya, a casual worker engaged by the contractor Frampton Investment CC, was fatally injured on Wednesday, 11 November 2026, when a concrete wall collapsed on him at the Municipal Rest Camp in Swakopmund. He was working with other employees on the demolition of existing bungalows at the Fish Bungalows site when the wall fell unexpectedly.

Fellow workers pulled him from the rubble and emergency medical services attended to him before taking him to hospital, where he died of his injuries. The Municipality of Swakopmund confirmed the death in a media statement, extending condolences to his family, friends and colleagues.

All construction and demolition activities at the site have been temporarily suspended while investigations and safety assessments are carried out. The municipality said it is working with the contractor and the relevant authorities to establish the full circumstances of the incident.

Why it mattersWork at the Fish Bungalows site has stopped while investigators and safety assessors examine what caused the wall to fall.

Namibia faces power, regulation hurdles for mineral processing

Former central bank governor Johannes !Gawaxab identified electricity costs and unpredictable rules as key barriers to developing Namibia's mineral sector.

Namibia's mineral beneficiation plans face significant obstacles, including high electricity costs and uncertain regulations, according to former Bank of Namibia Governor Johannes !Gawaxab. Speaking in Johannesburg, !Gawaxab stated that resource-rich African nations struggle to translate mineral wealth into industrial development due to structural barriers. He highlighted electricity, regulatory frameworks, infrastructure, and access to patient capital as the primary constraints.

"Africa has an electrons problem, a rules problem, a competitiveness, and a patient-capital problem," !Gawaxab said, emphasizing that value addition requires solving these issues. He noted that while Namibia has imposed export restrictions on minerals like lithium and cobalt since 2023 to encourage domestic processing, these measures alone are insufficient. "Bans tell capital where it may not go. They don’t tell it where it should go," he explained.

For Namibia's mineral processing ambitions to succeed, electricity must be competitive with international prices, and reliable power is essential for smelting and refining. !Gawaxab also stressed that investors need predictable, long-term rules, not ones that frequently change. He suggested that African institutional investors, such as pension funds, could increase their role in financing mineral processing and infrastructure projects.

Why it mattersInvestors need predictable regulations and competitive electricity prices for Namibia's mineral processing projects to attract long-term capital.

Namibian artists urged to master direct licensing

Nascam handles only certain royalties, leaving artists to exploit other rights such as synchronisation and master use licences on their own.

Many Namibian musicians believe that registering with the collective management organisation Nascam means their copyright administration is taken care of. The source says this is a myth: Nascam issues blanket licences to broadcasters, pubs, shopping malls and live venues, deducts administration costs, and distributes the rest to members.

Rights outside that blanket scheme must be handled by the copyright holder. The most lucrative is the synchronisation licence, which a filmmaker, television producer or advertising agency needs before pairing music with moving images. No collecting society in Namibia administers sync rights on a blanket basis, so the fee depends on market value, length of use, territory and project budget.

Sync licences cover only the song itself, so a company using a studio recording needs a separate master use licence, which artists who fully own their masters can charge for alongside the sync fee. Under the 1994 Copyright and Neighbouring Rights Protection Act, using a track in a corporate video without clearing the master is direct infringement, and anyone wishing to sample a recording must negotiate a clearance fee, usually an upfront buyout or a co-publishing split.

Why it mattersArtists who own their masters can negotiate separate fees for sync and master use licences, since no Namibian collecting society handles these rights on their behalf.
