Namibia private sector credit grows N$5.84 billion in nine months

Nominal borrowing increased by 39% year-on-year, but inflation is eroding real gains, Simonis Storm reports.

Namibia's private sector credit increased by N$5.84 billion in the first nine months of 2026, a 39% rise from the N$4.20 billion recorded during the same period last year. This growth brought the total credit book to N$128.2 billion by September, with N$1.06 billion added in that month alone. Businesses borrowed N$656.8 million, accounting for 62% of the monthly increase, while households added N$403.5 million.

Almandro Jansen, an economist at Simonis Storm, stated that annual private sector credit growth reached 5.6% in September, the fastest in a year. However, he cautioned that with inflation at 5.4%, real private sector credit growth stood at only 0.2%, down from 0.3% in August. This indicates that much of the nominal increase reflects higher prices rather than a significant expansion in real credit activity.

Simonis Storm forecasts credit growth to slow in the fourth quarter of 2026, with monthly flows expected to decline from an average of N$890 million to between N$450 million and N$500 million. This slowdown is anticipated as higher interest rates, including the Bank of Namibia's repo rate increase to 7% on October 28, begin to dampen borrowing demand over the next two to three quarters.

Why it mattersBusinesses and households are borrowing more in nominal terms, but inflation is reducing the actual value of this credit, impacting real economic activity.

Windhoek builds N$4.7 million market for 46 traders

The City of Windhoek is constructing a new market opposite Shoprite, with Debmarine Namibia contributing N$3.2 million towards the project.

Informal traders in Windhoek's city centre will soon have a new N$4.7 million market opposite Shoprite, a project the City of Windhoek is building to accommodate 46 vendors. Debmarine Namibia is contributing N$3.2 million to the development, which is expected to be completed by December 2026. The site has been handed over to the contractor for construction to begin.

Traders who have operated at the site for years, like Ester Andreas, welcome the permanent trading space, hoping it will preserve their businesses and ease their dealings. However, some vendors, including Martha Mukuve, who sells vegetables, are concerned about the allocation of stalls and whether the City will meet the December 2026 completion deadline. They have been informed that traders with proper paperwork will receive priority.

This Shoprite market is one of four informal trading projects the City of Windhoek plans, including a N$8.8 million market and taxi rank near Central Hospital for 50 traders, and a N$15 million market at Truckport. A market for 350 traders at Havana 4-Way is stalled due to the relocation of 53 households. City CEO Moses Matyayi cited rapid urban migration as a strain on service delivery and called for central government support.

Why it mattersInformal traders in Windhoek will gain permanent trading spaces, improving their livelihoods and dignity.

Namibia diesel price passes N$30 per litre

Petrol also rises as higher shipping costs and a weaker currency increase fuel import expenses.

Motorists in Namibia will pay over N$30 per litre for diesel starting tomorrow, November 4, 2026, following a N$3 per litre increase. Petrol 95 will also rise by N$2 per litre to N$28.58. These changes come as Namibian households and businesses face mounting financial pressure from rising fuel, interest rates, and food prices.

Industries, Mines and Energy Minister Modestus Amutse announced that 50ppm diesel will cost N$30.86 and 10ppm diesel N$30.96 per litre in Walvis Bay. The increases are attributed to higher international shipping costs, a weaker Namibian dollar, and global oil market uncertainty. Combined shipping and demurrage costs rose significantly, impacting fuel import expenses.

The National Energy Fund (NEF) faces depletion, with an expected N$384 million in under-recoveries for October. The government aims to protect the fund, which has been under pressure since April and May. Despite these increases, the minister assured the nation of adequate fuel stocks and a stable supply system.

Why it mattersNamibians will face higher costs for transport and goods as fuel prices increase, impacting household budgets and business operations.

Andrada mining contribution rises to N$775.2 million

The company's direct economic impact grew 6% in the 2026 financial year, driven by wages and local procurement.

Andrada Mining's direct economic contribution to Namibia reached N$775.2 million in the 2026 financial year, a 6% increase from the previous year. This growth was fueled by wages, royalties, and procurement as the company expanded its Uis Mine operations. The total contribution exceeded £33.17 million, up from £31.39 million in FY2025.

Local remuneration at Uis Mine totalled N$103.3 million in FY2026, an increase from N$100.38 million in FY2025. Andrada hired 126 new employees and offered internships to 11 graduates from local communities, with two later securing permanent positions. The company engaged 241 suppliers, with 189, or 78%, being Namibian businesses.

Andrada estimated its activities generated an additional N$330.2 million in indirect economic benefits using a World Bank multiplier. Chief executive Anthony Viljoen stated that Uis expansion creates more employment, local procurement, and skills development opportunities. The company is also exploring desalination for long-term water supply solutions with the ministry of agriculture, water and land reform.

Why it mattersNamibian workers and businesses benefited from increased wages, procurement, and employment opportunities due to Andrada Mining's expanded operations.

Namibia financial sector faces rising cyber and climate risks

Cyber vulnerabilities rose 39% and El Niño threatens drought, impacting loan repayments.

Namibia’s financial sector faces growing threats from cyberattacks, climate change, and government debt, according to a report by the Bank of Namibia and NAMFISA. Despite these emerging risks, the country’s financial institutions remained stable in the first half of 2026.

Cyber vulnerabilities increased by 39% to 513,921 in the second quarter of 2026, with reported cyber events up 56% to 109,593. The anticipated strengthening of El Niño raises concerns about drought, which could reduce agricultural production and affect borrowers’ ability to repay loans.

Banks’ holdings of government securities rose to 16.1% of total assets by June 2026, exposing the sector to government finance shocks. Retirement funds’ exposure to government debt also reached 14.8% of total assets.

Why it mattersNamibia's financial sector faces increased pressure from cyber threats, climate-induced drought impacting agriculture, and growing exposure to government debt.
