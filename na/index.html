App Connects Informal Workers to Gigs in Namibia

A new digital platform aims to formalize Namibia's informal labor market by connecting skilled individuals with short-term employment opportunities through a verified rating.

A technology platform called Job is Job has launched in Windhoek, Swakopmund, and Ondangwa to address inefficiencies in Namibia's informal labor market. The app connects electricians, plumbers, domestic workers, and casual laborers with employers seeking short-term help. This initiative aims to move the micro-gig economy from relying on word-of-mouth or street corner waiting to a more structured, digital system.

The platform offers several features designed to improve the hiring process for both workers and employers. It includes geo-located matching for immediate job postings, immutable digital work histories to build verifiable skills and reliability, and a transparent rating system to foster a reputational meritocracy. Additionally, it incorporates scam prevention measures by verifying accounts and eliminating upfront application fees, which often burden job seekers.

Job is Job seeks to drive financial inclusion by enabling informal workers to build trackable financial identities through verified work histories and earnings. This could potentially serve as proof of income for financial institutions. For small and medium enterprises (SMEs), the app promises to reduce recruitment time, thereby improving productivity and enabling faster responses to market demand.

Why it mattersInformal workers in Namibia can now access a digital platform to find work, build verifiable employment records, and potentially gain access to financial services.

NASRIA injects N$25 million into regional development

The Namibia Special Risks Insurance Association has provided N$25 million to support productive initiatives and economic growth across all 14 regions of Namibia.

The Namibia Special Risks Insurance Association (NASRIA) has allocated N$25 million to bolster regional economic development, with funds designated for productive initiatives across all 14 of Namibia's regions. The contribution was formally presented to regional governors in Windhoek on Thursday, in the presence of Prime Minister Elijah Ngurare and Finance Minister Ericah Shafudah. Prime Minister Ngurare emphasized that the funds must yield tangible economic benefits, including business growth, job creation, and infrastructure improvements.

This injection of capital aligns with the government's strategy to cultivate a more diversified and resilient economy, responding to the unique economic potential and needs of each region. Initiatives include a fruit and beverage agro-processing plant in the Zambezi region, a tomato processing plant and feedlot project in Omusati, and an agro-processing plant for peanut butter and cooking oil in the Omaheke region, all aimed at adding value to local produce and reducing post-harvest losses.

Prime Minister Ngurare urged regional governors to ensure the funds are directed towards practical, implementable initiatives that create lasting economic value and visible improvements in people's lives. He highlighted the necessity of partnerships with financial institutions like NASRIA to support national development priorities beyond their commercial operations, given limited public resources.

Why it mattersFarmers will benefit from new processing plants that add value to their produce, while investors can capitalize on new infrastructure and business opportunities across multiple regions.

Namibia's Industrial Policy Lacks Cohesion, Lags Behind Goals

Despite decades of discussion on beneficiation and value addition, Namibia's industrial policy has failed to integrate resources, finance, energy, trade, and skills.

Namibia's long-standing aspirations for industrialization, including beneficiation and value addition, remain largely unfulfilled, according to analysis of the nation's economic strategy. For over thirty years, these concepts have been recurring themes in official discourse, yet the country remains heavily reliant on the export of raw materials like uranium, diamonds, copper, zinc, gold, lithium, manganese, and rare earth elements.

The government's own vision for a dynamic, sustainable industrial economy that derives value from natural resources for all citizens has not materialized. While manufacturing, processing, and other productive enterprises exist, they have not been consolidated into the envisioned industrial economy. This persistent gap between ambition and reality raises questions about the effectiveness of past and present industrial policies.

Namibia's Vision 2030, adopted in 2004, aimed for industrialization by that year. Now four years from the deadline, the language has shifted from becoming an industrialised country to focusing on industrialization and economic transformation. This shift, without a clear explanation for the failure to meet original objectives, suggests a potential recalibration of national economic goals.

Why it mattersNamibians are affected by the lack of a cohesive industrial policy that hinders the creation of a diversified economy and limits the benefits derived from the country's natural resources.

Police Officer Shoots Suspect Who Stole Phone, Suspect Dies

A 26-year-old man died in hospital after being shot by a police officer in Oshakati, following an incident where the suspect allegedly stole a police officer's phone.

A 26-year-old man has died in hospital after being shot by a police officer in Oshakati. The incident occurred last Friday when the suspect, identified as Titus Titus, allegedly stole a police officer's phone. Police commander for the Oshana region, Andreas Shilelo, confirmed the event, stating that officers and civilians were attempting to apprehend Titus after he allegedly stole a phone from a shop complex.

According to Shilelo, the suspect had initially asked the officer for his phone to send an E-wallet PIN. After taking the phone and sending the money, Titus attempted to flee. While being pursued by civilians and the officer, Titus allegedly produced a knife and threatened the officer. The officer then shot Titus, who sustained serious injuries and was taken to hospital, where he later died.

Shilelo stated that the deceased was a known criminal in the area, involved in theft and fraud. The investigation into the shooting is ongoing, and the case will be handed over to the court for a decision. The police commander emphasized the importance of public safety and the protection of property, noting that residents have been increasingly concerned about theft and other criminal activities.

Why it mattersThe death of a suspect shot by police affects the community by raising concerns about public safety and police conduct during arrests.

Namibian Farmers Face Unsubsidized Competition

Despite agriculture's essential role, Namibian farmers lack the public support common elsewhere, hindering their ability to compete internationally.

Namibian agriculture, vital to the national economy, operates under a significant disadvantage due to insufficient government support. Farmers contend with a harsh environment characterized by dry conditions, variable rainfall, and high costs for water and electricity, all while facing competition from countries with robust agricultural subsidies.

The sector, dominated by livestock farming but also including crops like maize and horticultural products, contributes an average of 8.3% to GDP. Approximately 70% of Namibians rely on its value chains for their livelihoods, with livestock, particularly beef and sheep, forming the backbone of commercial agriculture and enjoying international markets.

This export-oriented model, achieved despite challenging environmental conditions, places the burden of high operational costs on individual farmers. While the government already provides some support, its scale and structure are deemed insufficient to make Namibian agriculture competitive against internationally subsidized producers.

Why it mattersNamibian farmers are affected by the lack of adequate national subsidies, which prevents them from competing on a level playing field with farmers in other countries.
