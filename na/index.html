Bank of Namibia raises repo rate to 7%

The central bank increased borrowing costs by 25 basis points to protect the Namibia Dollar's peg to the South African Rand.

The Bank of Namibia increased its repo rate by 25 basis points to 7.00% on Wednesday, raising the prime lending rate to 10.50%. This move by the Monetary Policy Committee aims to curb inflation and prevent capital outflows. Borrowers with variable-rate loans will likely see higher monthly repayments.

Governor Ebson Uanguta stated the decision was necessary to maintain the Namibia Dollar's one-to-one peg with the South African Rand and manage inflationary pressures from rising fuel prices. He noted the need to reduce the interest rate gap with South Africa to safeguard international reserves. The committee unanimously agreed on the policy tightening.

Namibia's annual inflation reached 5.4% in September 2026, up from June, driven by transport costs. The bank now forecasts 4.2% average inflation for 2026, up from 3.5% in 2025, due to persistent elevated global energy prices. Brent crude oil recently hit US$116 per barrel amid Middle East tensions.

Why it mattersHouseholds and businesses face higher borrowing costs due to the central bank's decision to increase the repo rate.

San community accuses Tsumkwe chief of land betrayal

Residents claim Chief Arnold has ignored their welfare, secretly signing away ancestral lands since 2013.

The !Kung San community in Tsumkwe West, Namibia, has accused their leader, Chief Ilony Arnold, of abandoning her duties and betraying their ancestral lands. Community members, speaking through spokesperson Nandu Ndumba, stated they are discriminated against and stripped of natural resources, with the unresolved crisis building since 2013.

Ndumba alleged that Chief Arnold and her councillors do not represent the people, have abandoned customary law, and are managing land for private benefit, threatening the community's indigenous way of life and culture. They also claim wealthy farmers guard illegal camps with firearms, posing a threat to their lives.

The community also reported that commercial farmers' cattle put immense pressure on their water infrastructure, breaking boreholes installed by the Directorate of Rural Water Supply. Chief Arnold has allegedly failed to hold community meetings for nearly 11 years, appointing relatives and councillors who act as land agents accepting bribes.

Why it mattersThe !Kung San community in Tsumkwe West faces discrimination, loss of ancestral lands, and denial of access to natural resources vital for their income and health.

Namibia Business School mourns Nigerian academic Simon Akpo

Simon Akpo, a lecturer at the Namibia Business School, died last Thursday at age 70.

Tributes are being paid to Nigerian academic Simon Akpo, who died last Thursday at the age of 70. Akpo was a lecturer at the University of Namibia, specifically at the Namibia Business School, where he made significant contributions and impacted students' lives.

The Namibia Business School described Akpo as more than a lecturer, noting his great contribution and the impact on his students. Former student Nkrumah Bankong-Obi called Akpo a pathfinder and anvil for many, while Joy Jakes recalled his fatherly help in settling into Namibia, from finding an apartment to buying household items.

Another former student, Ndeshiuva Taapopi, described Akpo as a legend who organized free extra evening classes for students struggling with business statistics. Details about memorial services and the funeral will be announced by the Namibia Business School when available.

Why it mattersThe death of Nigerian academic Simon Akpo affects students and staff at the Namibia Business School and the wider community in Namibia.

Namibia ranks 14th for African investment

The RMB report highlights stable democracy and low corruption as strengths, but import dependence remains a constraint.

Namibia secured the 14th position in the 2026/27 RMB Where to Invest in Africa report, with its personal freedom score ranking second among 31 African markets assessed. The report cited stable democratic institutions, low perceived corruption, and strong personal freedom as factors contributing to Namibia’s investment resilience.

However, the report identified import dependence and deep income inequality as significant constraints on Namibia’s investment prospects. Isaah Mhlanga, chief economist at RMB, noted that while strong institutions provide a foundation, Namibia remains exposed through concentrated exports and reliance on imports.

Namibia’s investment future hinges on its ability to commercialise offshore hydrocarbon discoveries in the Orange Basin. These discoveries offer potential for new investment and economic activity, but their successful development depends on moving beyond diamonds and uranium to diversify the production base and broaden government revenue.

Why it mattersNamibia's investment prospects are directly affected by its ranking and the report's analysis of its economic strengths and weaknesses.

Namibia government rejects electricity grid privatisation

Minister Modestus Amutse stated that public ownership is key to regulating tariffs and protecting national security.

Namibia’s government will not privatise the nation’s electricity transmission and distribution infrastructure, Minister of Mines and Energy Modestus Amutse announced in October 2026. The Ministry of Mines and Energy argues that public ownership of the grid is essential for controlling connection prices and routes, safeguarding a strategic national asset, and aligning grid capacity with electricity generation.

Minister Amutse told the National Assembly that retaining public control is necessary to regulate tariffs and prevent prices from becoming “uncontrollable” if the network were privately owned. He described electricity as a national security asset, emphasizing that public ownership ensures transmission capacity matches generation needs. This approach is compatible with private investment in new generation and regulatory work by the Electricity Control Board.

The government plans to expand low-cost renewable generation, storage, and regional interconnection on the publicly controlled grid. NamPower’s current projects and the Namibia–Angola Baynes Hydropower Project exemplify this strategy. Electricity is a fundamental input for all economic activities, including mining, fishing, agriculture, manufacturing, and the digital economy.

Why it mattersNamibians will be affected by how the government owns and governs the electricity wires, shaping the cost of living and doing business.
