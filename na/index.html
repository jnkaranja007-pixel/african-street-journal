Namibia's FMD-free suspension halts N$5.5 billion beef exports

Namibia's FMD-free status was suspended after a September 2026 biosecurity breach in //Kharas, stopping beef exports worth N$5.5 billion a year.

Namibia lost its Foot-and-Mouth Disease free status overnight after a biosecurity breach in the //Kharas region in September 2026, halting an annual beef export pipeline worth N$5.5 billion. The suspension puts more than 52,000 rural jobs at immediate risk, according to Dr. Tuhafeni DaSilva Hishitelwa, who writes in The Brief that the status is suspended rather than lost.

Hishitelwa points to the 1961 epidemic, which began on the farm Esperance in the eastern Windhoek district and was suspected to have been carried by wild buffaloes from Bechuanaland, now Botswana. Dr. Vorster, then Secretary of the Department of Agriculture-Technical Services, deployed 40 veterinarians from South Africa, more than 1,300 officials and volunteers, and over 1,700 workers to build fencing and enforce quarantines. The disease was eradicated by 1964.

The 1961 response relied on a Stamping Out policy, game-proof fencing, movement bans and farm-by-farm testing, a model Hishitelwa says Namibia should follow to regain its trade standing.

Why it mattersBeef exporters and the rural workers who depend on them face the loss of N$5.5 billion in annual sales and more than 52,000 jobs until the status is restored.

Meatco suspends slaughter operations amid FMD crisis

Namibian beef exporter Meatco faces severe financial pressure and potential job cuts due to the suspension of international exports.

The Meat Corporation of Namibia (Meatco) has suspended slaughter operations because the ongoing Foot and Mouth Disease (FMD) outbreak has stopped international beef exports, which historically accounted for over 85% of its earnings. Interim Chief Executive Albertus Aochamub stated that local sales cannot replace the lost income, placing the company under severe fiscal pressure.

Meatco's monthly wage bill of N$15 million is now unsustainable, prompting management to consult with the Namibia Food and Allied Workers Union (Nafau) on cost-containment measures. These include possible temporary salary reductions, shorter working hours, and voluntary separation packages, subject to funding availability.

The company is also exploring a payment holiday with the Development Bank of Namibia and other financial relief. Should export restrictions persist, Meatco may consider organisational restructuring, workforce reductions, or temporarily placing operations under care and maintenance, while aiming to preserve the company as a going concern and protect jobs.

Why it mattersNearly 800 Meatco employees face potential salary reductions, shorter working hours, and retrenchments due to the FMD crisis impacting the company's export revenue.

Walvis Bay handles N$13.2 billion in September trade

Namibia's trade deficit widened to N$4.7 billion as imports outpaced exports, with the port handling the bulk of goods by value.

In September, the port of Walvis Bay handled a combined N$13.2 billion in imports and exports, according to the latest bulletin from the Namibia Statistics Agency. The country's trade deficit reached N$4.7 billion for the month, as imports of N$16.9 billion outstripped exports of N$12.2 billion.

Walvis Bay moved N$6.3 billion of outbound cargo and received N$6.9 billion in imports, making it the largest point of trade by value on both sides. Sea transport carried 53.5% of export value, shipping N$6.5 billion in goods, mainly uranium, nickel ores and base metal ores. Road transport moved the greatest physical volume of exports, led by petroleum oils, sulphur and fish.

China absorbed 36.2% of Namibia's export revenue, driven largely by maritime uranium shipments, while South Africa supplied 30.7% of the import bill. The agency's data show strong mineral exports and steady demand for fuel and industrial inputs shaped the month's trade.

Why it mattersNamibian importers and exporters move goods through Walvis Bay and the border posts, so the N$4.7 billion deficit reflects costs and volumes they handle directly.

Namibia faces water supply strain

Central coast uses double its aquifer capacity, relying on desalination.

Namibia's water supply is under strain due to rising demand, aging infrastructure, and limited resources, with the central coast heavily dependent on desalinated seawater. The 3rd Namibia Regional Water Symposium in Swakopmund on Tuesday heard that NamWater's coastal aquifers are supplying nearly double their permitted capacity. Adelino Batista, NamWater's head of coastal business unit, stated that the Omdel and Kuiseb aquifers have a supply limit of 14 million cubic metres annually, but demand reaches 29 million cubic metres.

The shortfall is met by the Orano desalination plant, which produced a record 18 million cubic metres last financial year. Water demand on the coastal system has risen by 10% and a further 6% over two years, with NamWater selling 28.4 million cubic metres of treated water, primarily to mines. Batista highlighted the need for new water sources to support existing and emerging industries.

The N$2.1 billion Erongo Sunam desalination project, a joint venture between NamWater and Swakop Uranium, is slated for mid-2028 operations. This project aims to produce around 20 million cubic metres annually, with Swakop Uranium consuming at least half. NamWater is also undertaking N$170 million pipeline replacements for the Omdel and Swakopmund lines, with work beginning in July and expected completion in the next financial year.

Why it mattersMining companies are being asked to share the risks and costs associated with new water infrastructure due to the long lifespan of the infrastructure compared to mine operations.

Family collects cans for N$100 a bag

Nicklas Ortman and Margeret Garies collect and crush discarded cans to earn money for food and school.

Nicklas Ortman, 49, and Margeret Garies, 49, collect discarded cans around Windhoek to sell for income. Their sons, Immanuel (16) and Joshua Gariseb (12), sometimes help their father search for cans near Grove Mall. The family spends most of their day collecting recyclable material, returning home around 19:00.

A bag of crushed cans, weighing up to 30 to 40 cans, can fetch up to N$100. Ortman stated that taxi costs sometimes prevent them from travelling to collect cans. The earnings are used for basic needs like food and school expenses for their children. Immanuel, in Grade 7, expressed pride in his father's efforts to provide for the family.

The family has faced dangers while collecting cans, including threats and robberies. Ortman described one incident where robbers threatened their lives. Despite the hardships, the family continues to collect cans to survive and support their children's education.

Why it mattersThe family's income depends on collecting and selling crushed cans, which they use for food and their children's schooling.
