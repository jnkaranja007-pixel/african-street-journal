Uganda begins Somalia troop withdrawal

Uganda's military has started pulling out troops deployed under bilateral agreements, with a full withdrawal by end of 2027.

Uganda's military has begun withdrawing its troops from Somalia under bilateral agreements with the Somali federal government, UPDF spokesman Chris Magezi announced Monday. The first group of soldiers has already returned to Uganda, and the complete withdrawal of these bilateral forces is expected within days. The UPDF plans to remove all its forces in Somalia, including those currently deployed under the African Union Support and Stabilization Mission in Somalia (AUSSOM), by the close of 2027.

Magezi stated that this phased operation will permit the Somali Armed Forces to assume full responsibility for security in the country. This move marks the end of Uganda's 20-year peace and stabilization support operations in Somalia. The UPDF's withdrawal is effective immediately.

The UPDF will gradually withdraw all its forces from Somalia.

Why it mattersUganda's military withdrawal will lead to the Somali Armed Forces assuming full responsibility for security in the country.

One in 10 Somali women journalists feel safe

A new report by the National Union of Somali Journalists reveals widespread harassment and pay issues.

A new report by the National Union of Somali Journalists (NUSOJ) found that only one in 10 women journalists in Somalia feel safe at work. The report, "Unsafe, Underpaid and Unheard: The Workplace Crisis Facing Women Journalists in Somalia," surveyed women in Mogadishu and six federal member states.

More than half of the surveyed journalists reported feeling unsafe or very unsafe, with many experiencing harassment in newsrooms, in the field, and online, including AI-generated deepfakes. NUSOJ Secretary-General Omar Faruk Osman stated that women journalists face greater risks and lack protection, demanding written contracts, timely wages, and consequences for perpetrators.

The report also highlights that 46.5% of women journalists have been denied promotions due to their gender, and 63.8% received no workplace training. These findings were released on the International Day to End Impunity for Crimes against Journalists, with support from the UNDP and funding from the European Union.

Why it mattersWomen journalists in Somalia face significant safety, pay, and career advancement challenges, impacting their ability to work and report freely.

Hawadle militias quit front lines in Hiran region

About 2,000 fighters have seized government offices in Beledweyne, seeking an administration independent of Mogadishu.

Hawadle clan militias, known as Macawisley, have abandoned front-line positions in Somalia’s central Hiran region, including in Mataban and Buulo Barde districts. The fighters seized control of the regional capital, Beledweyne, over the weekend, taking over the provincial headquarters and other government offices. About 2,000 heavily armed clan fighters are now in Beledweyne as part of a plan by Hawadle political figures to create an authority separate from Mogadishu and Hirshabelle.

Fears exist that Hawadle soldiers serving in the federal army in Hiran could also defect, potentially exposing remaining government-held areas to Al Shabaab. The Macawisley were formed in 2022 to fight Al Shabaab and had received support from the federal government, Ethiopia, and the United States. A former head of the National Intelligence and Security Agency stated that they planned to set up an administration to confront the government in Hiran.

Hawadle politicians claim President Hassan Sheikh Mohamud promised their clan the Hirshabelle presidency but later reneged. They accuse the federal government of backing incumbent regional president Ali Gudlawe in a Mogadishu-controlled re-election vote. It remains unclear if Mogadishu will send reinforcements to the Hiran region.

Why it mattersThe withdrawal of militias and seizure of government offices creates a security vacuum that could allow Al Shabaab to regain territory.

Somali women journalists face 96 reported abuses

SOMWA documented arrests, physical attacks, and digital harassment against media workers, particularly women.

The Somali Women in Media (SOMWA) organization reported 96 incidents affecting journalists, including arrests, torture, equipment confiscation, and online attacks. SOMWA presented this report in Mogadishu on November 2, 2026, coinciding with the International Day to End Impunity for Crimes against Journalists.

SOMWA director Maryam Seylac urged the government to reveal the whereabouts of any arrested journalist within 24 hours and to cease illegal detentions and media equipment seizures. The report noted that while not exclusive to women, the documented abuses show variations requiring clarification, with some data spanning November 2025 to September 2026 and other parts including October.

SOMWA also recorded 79 cases of gender-based harassment, online abuse, or defamation against women journalists since 2023, covering a longer period than the current report. The organization highlighted that female journalists reporting sensitive stories often face personal and reputational attacks that can extend to their families, potentially hindering their return to work.

Why it mattersFemale journalists in Somalia face harassment, defamation, and physical attacks, impacting their ability to report on sensitive issues and their personal lives.
