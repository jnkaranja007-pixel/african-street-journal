High Court review delays Danny Jordaan fraud trial until November 2026

The fraud case against South African Football Association (SAFA) president Danny Jordaan and co-accused has been postponed to November 27, 2026, pending a High Court decision.

The Specialised Commercial Crimes Court in Palm Ridge postponed the fraud trial involving SAFA president Danny Jordaan and his co-accused to allow the High Court to rule on a review application. This application is linked to an earlier Section 342A application, which permits a court to investigate delays in criminal proceedings. The National Prosecuting Authority (NPA) stated its readiness to proceed with the case, according to communications officer Magaboke Mohlatlole.

Jordaan, former SAFA chief financial officer Grown Hluyo, former journalist and Grit Communications director Trevor Neethling, Grit Communications, and former acting SAFA CEO Russell Paul face multiple charges. These include fraud involving more than R1 million, theft, and conspiracy to commit fraud and theft. The state alleges Jordaan unlawfully signed and backdated a service level agreement with Grit Communications in December 2017, bypassing SAFA's approval processes to benefit his personal image.

Prosecutors also accuse Jordaan of procuring personal security services under the guise of SAFA business operations. Hluyo is alleged to have authorized payments to Grit Communications and a security company despite knowing the agreements were unauthorized by SAFA. Paul is accused of colluding with the others in connection with an allegedly unlawful 2018 service level agreement between SAFA and Grit Communications.

Why it mattersFootball fans and stakeholders in South African football are directly affected by the ongoing legal proceedings against the SAFA president, as it impacts the leadership and integrity of the sport's governing body.

Floods in Nepal-Tibet region leave four South Africans missing

Four South African nationals are among hundreds of tourists and foreign nationals reported missing after flash floods swept through Nepal’s Rasuwa district near the Tibet border.

Devastating flash floods in Nepal’s Rasuwa district have left at least 95 people dead and more than 403 travelers unaccounted for, including four South African nationals. The disaster struck the mountainous region, a popular route for tourists traveling between Nepal and Tibet, sweeping away a bridge and destroying buildings.

The Nepal Tourism Board confirmed the missing South Africans as part of the 291 foreign nationals among the 384 people still missing. Rescue operations by Nepal’s armed forces and paramilitary units are ongoing, but damaged roads, destroyed bridges, and disrupted communications are hindering efforts to reach affected areas.

The exact cause of the floods remains under investigation, though preliminary reports from Nepal’s Foreign Minister Shishir Khanal suggest an earthquake may have triggered an ice-rock avalanche. The Lhende Khola river, which flows through the affected region, saw a massive surge of water, causing destruction on both the Nepali and Tibetan sides of the border.

Why it mattersSouth African families and friends of the missing tourists are directly affected by the uncertainty surrounding their loved ones' whereabouts and safety.

Scientists investigate Gqeberha fish deaths, warn of industry impact

Thousands of dead fish, predominantly sardines, have washed ashore on Gqeberha's beaches, prompting scientists to investigate potential causes and issue warnings about touching.

Thousands of fish, mostly sardines, have washed up dead along Gqeberha's beaches, leading authorities to investigate the cause. Scientists are examining whether pilchard herpesvirus, an algal bloom, or sudden ocean temperature changes are responsible. Nelson Mandela Bay University Professor Nadine Strydom suspects the mortality is linked to the pilchard herpesvirus (PHV) found in the Western Cape earlier this month.

Professor Strydom noted that the number of dead fish visible on beaches is small compared to what lies on the ocean floor, indicating a larger die-off. While other species have been affected, sardines make up most of the deaths. Authorities have advised against collecting or eating the dead fish, though commercially canned sardines are confirmed safe.

The sardine deaths could have significant consequences for the small-scale fishing industry and marine life, according to Professor Strydom. She warned that penguins and other marine animals could face starvation. She also suggested that the government might need to adjust pilchard harvesting quotas to protect the industry, drawing parallels to similar PHV-related mortality events in Australia in the 1990s.

Why it mattersThe fish die-off directly affects the small-scale fishing industry through potential harvest reductions and consumers who are warned against eating the dead fish.

Salif Keïta cancels South Africa concerts over 'xenophobic violence'

Malian musician Salif Keïta has canceled all future South African performances, citing recent waves of xenophobic violence across the country.

World-famous Malian musician Salif Keïta has canceled his upcoming South African concerts, including a festival performance next month, due to what he described as "xenophobic violence." Keïta stated that the xenophobia did not "match the South Africa I know," according to BBC Africa.

This decision follows a wave of protests in South Africa demanding that undocumented migrants leave the country, with an unofficial deadline set for September 30. Tens of thousands of Africans have voluntarily repatriated in recent months, with some reporting intimidation and attacks.

While the South African government has been cracking down on illegal immigration, it has also stated that individuals who have taken the law into their own hands have been arrested. South Africa, as Africa's wealthiest nation, has historically attracted migrants seeking better economic opportunities, but illegal immigration has become a contentious political issue, with protesters accusing migrants of straining public services and involvement in crime.

Why it mattersThe cancellation of concerts by a prominent artist affects South African cultural event organizers and fans who planned to attend the performances.

South Africa shifts education to meet job market demands

South Africa is transitioning from pre-2009 occupational qualifications to a new system that links skills directly to employment, self-employment, entrepreneurship, and lifelong.

South Africa is moving to update its education system to better prepare its workforce for current and future economic needs, according to Dr. Makhapa Makhafola, chairperson of the Ministerial Technical Task Team and COO for the South African Qualifications Authority. This shift aims to address the country's unemployment crisis by focusing on occupational qualifications instead of outdated credentials.

The change comes as South Africa's unemployment figures continue to rise. The official unemployment rate increased from 32.7% in the first quarter to 33.6% in the second quarter, with 345,000 more people becoming unemployed, bringing the total to 8.5 million, according to Stats SA's Quarterly Labour Force Survey (QLFS).

Youth unemployment is particularly high, increasing by 1.5 percentage points to 47.4%, with five million young people now unemployed. This represents an increase of 264,000 young people without jobs, while the number of employed young people decreased by 40,000 to 5.6 million in the last quarter.

Why it mattersThis shift directly affects young people and graduates struggling to enter the labor market by aiming to provide them with more relevant skills for available jobs.
