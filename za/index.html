SAHRC flags R777m tanker bill amid Gauteng water crisis

The South African Human Rights Commission found infrastructure failure, not water shortage, causes outages.

The South African Human Rights Commission (SAHRC) has raised concerns over millions spent on water tankers in Gauteng while residents face prolonged outages. The commission's findings, released on Monday, November 2, followed an inquiry into water and sanitation services.

SAHRC commissioner Dr. Henk Boshoff questioned why municipalities spent hundreds of millions on emergency water instead of repairing infrastructure, calling water tankering a form of corruption and financial mismanagement. The City of Tshwane spent R777 million on water tankers in the 2024/25 financial year, an increase from R47 million in 2021/22.

The SAHRC inquiry, conducted from May 19-21, identified ageing infrastructure, poor maintenance, water losses, inadequate storage, and electricity interruptions as key contributors to Gauteng's water problems. The commission made eight recommendations, including repairing wastewater plants and improving maintenance, and will monitor their implementation.

Why it mattersGauteng residents are affected by prolonged water outages due to failing infrastructure and mismanagement of funds meant for repairs.

US to suspend South Africa's duty-free trade access

Washington cites trade barriers and other practices inconsistent with the AGOA agreement.

The United States intends to suspend South Africa's duty-free access to the US market under the African Growth and Opportunity Act (AGOA). US Trade Representative Jamieson Greer announced on Monday that Washington found South Africa engaging in practices inconsistent with AGOA's eligibility criteria.

Greer stated that these practices include barriers undermining American trade and investment, and actions threatening US national security and foreign policy interests in the region. South Africa has benefited from AGOA for years.

The US administration has previously expressed concerns about trade barriers, rule of law, and human rights in South Africa. The South African government disputes claims of persecution of white people, calling them falsehoods spread by fringe groups.

Two arrested for election offences in Limpopo

Suspects detained for tearing down posters and assault ahead of local government elections.

South African Police Service officers in Limpopo arrested two men on November 3, 2026, for election-related offences during the special voting period. One suspect was detained in Mankweng for allegedly tearing down official election posters, an act captured on video. The other arrest occurred in Sekhukhune District following a confrontation where a T-shirt was forcibly removed and burned.

The Mankweng suspect faces charges under Section 92 of the Electoral Act 73 of 1998, with his case remanded to November 30, 2026. In Sekhukhune, police registered a case of assault and intimidation after a rival party's supporters allegedly confronted and attacked a complainant. Limpopo Provincial Police Commissioner Lieutenant General Jan Petrus Scheepers stated that SAPS will act swiftly against intimidation and the destruction of election material.

Scheepers urged all parties to exercise restraint and resolve differences peacefully. The 2026 Local Government Elections are scheduled for November 4, 2026, with voting stations open from 07:00 to 21:00. Police advise voters to remain vigilant and follow safety tips, such as avoiding valuables and not lingering at voting stations.

Why it mattersTwo men were arrested for election-related offences, including tearing down posters and assault, impacting the special voting period for the 2026 Local Government Elections.

South Africa's power outlook relies on uncertain assumptions

A new 10-year electricity plan faces doubts after a failed gas tender and ambitious Eskom targets.

South Africa's electricity supply may remain stable for the next decade, according to the National Transmission Company South Africa's (NTCSA) Medium-Term System Adequacy Outlook 2027-2036. This forecast depends on Eskom improving its plant performance, new generation capacity arriving on schedule, and the transmission grid expanding as planned.

However, the report's optimistic scenario, published on October 30, 2026, is already challenged by the recent failure of the government's first gas-to-power procurement round. The NTCSA's reference case assumes significant new renewable energy, storage, transmission upgrades, and new gas generation from 2032, alongside Eskom's energy availability factor rising from 66% to 70%.

Even with these assumptions, the outlook projects 13.42 GWh and 377 hours of unserved energy by 2036, indicating potential demand shortfalls. The report's modelling represents a significant advance, but its outcomes are conditional and not certain.

Why it mattersSouth African businesses and households face continued uncertainty regarding electricity supply due to the conditional nature of the new 10-year outlook.

South Africa launches new insecticide for potato moths

Syngenta’s SILVALIS®, powered by PLINAZOLIN® technology, offers a new mode of action for crop protection.

Syngenta South Africa has launched SILVALIS®, the nation's first registered IRAC Group 30 insecticide, to combat the destructive potato tuber moth. This new product, powered by PLINAZOLIN® technology, introduces a novel mode of action to South African agriculture, providing potato growers with a new tool against a challenging pest.

The insecticide is registered for controlling the potato tuber moth (Phthorimaea operculella), which can cause up to 100% crop loss by damaging leaves, stems, and tubers in fields and storage. Sarike le Roux, Syngenta’s Head of Marketing, stated the product reflects the company's aim to turn science into practical breakthroughs for farmers.

SILVALIS® contains the active ingredient isocycloseram and was developed over the past 10 years. Chris Steyn, Syngenta’s Portfolio Manager Insect Control, noted that grower feedback has exceeded predictions and trials, offering new chemistry for integrated pest management programmes.

Why it mattersSouth African potato farmers gain a new tool to protect their crops from severe losses caused by the potato tuber moth.
