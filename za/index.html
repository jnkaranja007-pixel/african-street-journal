Sharpeville survivors challenge apartheid-era law blocking compensation

Legal action seeks to overturn legislation preventing claims for damages from the 1960 massacre, aiming for government dialogue on reparations.

Lawyers representing survivors and relatives of those killed in the 1960 Sharpeville Massacre have initiated legal proceedings against the South African government. The action targets a law enacted shortly after the event that has barred compensation claims for over sixty years. The Sharpeville Massacre, where police fired on protesters, resulted in 69 deaths and at least 180 injuries according to official records, becoming a stark symbol of apartheid's brutality.

The legal challenge, spearheaded by Lawyers for Human Rights, aims to open a dialogue with the government regarding reparations for victims and their families. Survivors like 87-year-old Abram Mofokeng, who was shot in the foot and back, and Mpai Chabane, whose grandmother was killed, express deep emotional and financial hardship. They seek justice through compensation and the preservation of the township's history, which is slowly falling into disrepair.

The Sharpeville Massacre occurred on March 21, 1960, as protesters demonstrated against pass laws that restricted the movement of Black South Africans. While official figures state 69 killed and 180 wounded, subsequent research cited by lawyers suggests at least 91 people died and over 238 were injured. The day of the massacre is now observed as Human Rights Day, a public holiday, following the end of apartheid.

Why it mattersSurvivors and families of those killed in the Sharpeville Massacre are seeking compensation and reparations due to an apartheid-era law that has prevented them from claiming damages for over six decades.

Zungu Challenges Incumbent Jordaan for Safa Presidency

AmaZulu owner Sandile Zungu faces Danny Jordaan, who is seeking a fourth term as Safa president despite ongoing fraud charges.

Businessman Sandile Zungu is challenging Danny Jordaan for the South African Football Association (Safa) presidency at the upcoming elective congress on September 12. Jordaan, who has led Safa since 2013, is seeking a fourth term despite facing ongoing fraud charges. Zungu, owner of the AmaZulu football club, has received the backing of AmaZulu King Misuzulu kaZwelithini, who urged all 11 KwaZulu-Natal football regions to support his bid.

Jordaan, who turned 75 on September 3, has been president of Safa since replacing Kirsten Nematandani. He has faced challengers before and is currently serving his third term. His arrest in November 2024 for fraud, along with Safa's chief of finance and former CEO, has led to calls for his resignation, but Safa's constitution does not mandate this. The fraud case remains in the pre-trial phase.

Despite the legal proceedings, Jordaan remains confident in his re-election, attributing the accusations to a coordinated attack by critics within Safa. He believes such campaigns are intended to deter sponsors, noting that football is uniquely targeted compared to rugby and cricket. Zungu's candidacy presents a significant challenge to Jordaan's long tenure at the helm of South African football.

Why it mattersThe leadership battle for the South African Football Association presidency affects business interests through potential sponsor attraction and the business operations of affiliated clubs.

Diesel Prices Jump 62% Amid Strait of Hormuz Conflict

South African diesel double-cab owners face significantly higher refueling costs due to a 62% price increase since January.

Diesel prices for 50 ppm fuel have surged by 62% since January 2026, with the latest increase on September 2 pushing the coast price to R28.79 per litre and inland to R30.05 per litre. This escalation, driven by events in the Strait of Hormuz, has added R11.03 per litre coast and R11.53 per litre inland. The conflict, triggered by US-Israeli strikes on Iran on February 28, led Iran to close the Strait of Hormuz, a vital oil transit route.

Filling an 80-litre tank now costs R2,303.20 on the coast and R2,404.00 inland, an increase of R882.40 and R922.40 respectively compared to January. This means motorists may spend nearly R900 more per fill-up, with many refilling weekly. The Department of Mineral and Petroleum Resources and National Treasury have not implemented fuel levy reductions or other safeguards to mitigate these costs.

Analysts do not expect Middle East oil production to normalize before early 2027, suggesting continued high fuel costs. The relative strength of the rand has partially offset the price hikes, preventing even more severe increases. Owners of diesel double-cab bakkies are being advised to consider alternatives like petrol or new-energy vehicles.

Why it mattersDiesel vehicle owners are affected by significantly higher refueling costs, making it more expensive to operate their vehicles.

Activists Protect Cape Town Farmland from Mining and Development

Over 100 participants at Vegkop Polyculture Farm discussed food and water security, highlighting the Philippi Horticultural Area's role in offsetting climate change.

Over 100 climate activists and participants gathered at Vegkop Polyculture Farm during Cape Town Climate Week to discuss preserving the Philippi Horticultural Area (PHA) wetland and the Cape Flats Aquifer from mining and development. The event focused on food and water security, including a water mapping exercise to determine household water usage.

PHA Food and Farming Campaign chairperson Nazeer Sonday addressed the gathering, detailing their efforts to combat development in the PHA, which is a vital recharge zone for the Cape Flats Aquifer. Previous reports indicated that mining posed a significant threat to the environment, the aquifer, and surrounding farmlands, potentially impacting Cape Town's food security.

The PHA is described as drought-proof farmland, increasingly valued for its role in mitigating climate change impacts. The campaign has previously warned that proposed urban development could lead to an irreplaceable loss of fertile agricultural land. Sonday also noted that organic farming can reduce greenhouse gas emissions from pesticides and fertilizers, contrasting with the two billion hectares of unfarmable land globally due to degradation.

Why it mattersFarmers are directly affected by the threat of mining and development to the drought-proof farmland of the Philippi Horticultural Area, which is crucial for food security.

Illegal mining damages Johannesburg infrastructure

Residents and officials warn that illicit gold and mineral trade is undermining transport, power networks, homes, and buildings across the economic hub.

Johannesburg's infrastructure faces increasing risks from illegal mining operations, according to local officials and residents. Whole sections of roads have collapsed, and cracks and sinkholes have appeared due to underground blasting by miners, known locally as zama zamas. These round-the-clock activities are damaging the city's transport and power networks, as well as its homes and buildings.

The zama zamas, who are often heavily armed, descend into abandoned mine shafts or dig new ones to extract gold and other minerals. This illicit trade is not only physically damaging the city's foundations but also introducing crime linked to competition between gangs, creating a dangerous environment for residents.

Voneen Trompeter, a 45-year-old resident, described the escalating situation, stating, "At first it was the blasting and the tremors from underground, and we would feel our flat shake. Now there are shootouts in the area. It is scary." Thousands of impoverished men from surrounding countries are drawn to this dangerous work, spending days underground.

Why it mattersIllegal mining activities are directly damaging Johannesburg's transport, power, and residential infrastructure, posing risks to property and safety.
