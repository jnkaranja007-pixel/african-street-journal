Mauritius police arrest man over TikTok video

A 25-year-old man faces a provisional charge of child ill-treatment after a video circulated online.

Police in Mauritius arrested a 25-year-old man from Quatre-Bornes on Friday, October 30, 2026, after a controversial TikTok video circulated online. The video reportedly shows the man making sexual remarks during an interaction with a minor.

The Cybercrime Unit questioned the suspect, whose TikTok account has been banned and the video removed by authorities. He was transferred to Quatre-Bornes police and detained pending a court appearance.

The man was scheduled to appear before the Weekend Court on Saturday, October 31, 2026, facing a provisional charge of child ill-treatment. The police investigation into the matter is ongoing.

Menlo Park demands $19.55 million from Bank of Mauritius

Company claims former central bank governor Rama Sithanen sought commission on financing deal before his appointment.

Menlo Park Ltd served a legal demand on the Bank of Mauritius on October 30, seeking $19.55 million in damages. The company claims $9.25 million for loss of share value, $5.3 million for lost investment, and $5 million for reputational damage. The bank has one month to comply or face legal action.

The demand alleges that Rama Sithanen, his son Tevin Sithanen, and Ziyad Bundhun discussed a financing request with Menlo Park between October 17 and 24, 2024. According to the claim, Rama Sithanen suggested he could unblock $6.3 million from the MIC if a commission was paid to him or his son. Menlo Park states it refused this arrangement.

After Rama Sithanen became governor on November 16, 2024, he publicly called the MIC investment a fraud on December 7, 2024, freezing Menlo Park's account. The company views this as retribution. A WhatsApp call recording from November 12, 2024, involving Tevin Sithanen, has also become public, with claims of his father's influence over the MIC and threats of arrest.

Mauritius cabinet considers lowering age of criminal responsibility

Government examines prosecuting 12-year-olds after recent riots, but legal experts and child rights groups raise concerns.

The Mauritian government is considering lowering the age of criminal responsibility to 12 years old, a move prompted by recent riots where minors were arrested. The current law, established by the Children’s Act 2020, sets the minimum age at 14. Cabinet discussed the proposal on October 14, but decided on further consultation and a parliamentary Select Committee before any decision is made.

Police reported that 16 out of 151 people arrested during the unrest were minors, approximately one in nine. However, legal experts point out that a retroactive change would not apply to these arrests due to constitutional protections. The proposed reform would only affect future cases, and concerns remain about the justice system's capacity to handle cases involving younger children, given existing delays highlighted in past Privy Council rulings.

Child-rights organisations and the UN Committee on the Rights of the Child advise against lowering the age, citing potential harm to children and a lack of evidence that prosecuting younger children deters violence. The current system, designed for those aged 14 and above, would require significant changes to accommodate 12- and 13-year-olds, including staff training and facility adjustments. The Select Committee will also examine parental responsibility and the role of adults inciting minors to commit crimes.

Why it mattersThe government's consideration of lowering the age of criminal responsibility affects children aged 12 and 13, their families, and the broader justice system.

Mauritians honour ancestors with cemetery rituals

Families across the island spent November 1 and 2 cleaning and decorating graves for All Saints' Day and All Souls' Day.

Thousands of Mauritian families gathered at cemeteries across the island, including Saint-Jean in Quatre-Bornes and Salines in Port-Louis, on November 1 and 2. They cleaned, renovated, and decorated the graves of their departed loved ones for All Saints' Day and All Souls' Day.

Fabrice, a 45-year-old resident of Curepipe, explained his annual visit to his grandparents' graves at Saint-Jean cemetery. He stated, "I repaint their tombs, not just to update them and keep them clean, but it's a way to tell my grandparents: 'Yes… I love you and I haven't forgotten you.'" Families used water, brushes, paint, and flowers like lilies, carnations, and tropical bouquets to adorn the tombs.

Despite rising costs for imported and local flowers, most families are committed to maintaining this tradition. Fabrice added, "Whatever the cost of flowers, paint, or renovation… we do it out of love and respect for our elders." The annual observance highlights the strength of family ties and cultural continuity in Mauritius.

Why it mattersMauritian families uphold traditions of remembrance and respect for their ancestors through cemetery visits and grave decorations.
