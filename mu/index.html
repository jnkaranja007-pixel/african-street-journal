Opposition Leader Fails to Press Health Minister on Surgery Details

Chetan Baboolall did not fully exploit the opportunity to question the health minister's private doctor's role in a public surgery.

Opposition leader Chetan Baboolall used only half of his allotted time during his first Private Notice Question, failing to press for crucial details regarding the health minister's recent surgery. The question centered on whether the minister of Health, Anil Bachoo, had a private doctor who participated in his operation, a role the minister had initially described as purely observational. The Prime Minister acknowledged that no formal protocol governs such situations, a point the opposition leader could have further explored.

Several key questions remained unanswered, including who authorized Dr. Sookha's intervention and when, and whether written records of this decision exist. The Prime Minister stated that no formal protocol exists, but the specific authorization for Dr. Sookha's change from observer to active participant was not clarified. The exact timing of this role change, crucial for determining patient consent, was also left unaddressed.

The opposition leader's questioning did not delve into whether Anil Bachoo explicitly consented to Dr. Sookha's participation in the surgery, nor the form or timing of any such consent. The Prime Minister had emphasized patient consent as a prerequisite for private doctors' involvement in public hospitals, a point that was not fully pursued by the opposition.

Why it mattersThe public's ability to understand the oversight and accountability mechanisms within the public health system is limited by the lack of detailed answers.

Fuel Pellets Found on Pointe-d’Esny Beach

Environmental and police officers observed scattered fuel pellets and patches on the shore, with no visible oil slick in the lagoon.

Scattered fuel pellets and patches were discovered on Pointe-d’Esny beach on Wednesday, September 2. Officials from the Ministry of Environment and environmental police confirmed the findings but noted no visible oil film or slick in the lagoon. The scattered hydrocarbon pellets on the beach are believed to originate from the discharge of contaminated bilge or ballast water from ships transiting the maritime highway offshore.

The action of waves gradually solidifies these hydrocarbon residues, which then deposit on the shore as pellets or patches. This phenomenon has been observed in the area previously. In accordance with the National Oil Spill Contingency Plan (NOSCP), the Special Mobile Force is tasked with cleaning the coastline in such incidents. A Special Mobile Force team was dispatched to collect the fuel pellets.

The collected waste will be transported to the hazardous waste storage center in La Chaumière for secure disposal. The relevant authorities are monitoring the situation. This event highlights ongoing challenges with maritime pollution impacting coastal areas.

Why it mattersThe presence of fuel pellets on Pointe-d’Esny beach affects the local environment and requires cleanup operations by the Special Mobile Force.

Mauritius Rethinks Drug War Metrics

The nation's new drug master plan shifts focus from arrests and seizures to reducing drug-related harm.

Mauritius is questioning the effectiveness of traditional drug enforcement tactics, moving towards a policy that measures reductions in drug-related harm rather than just increased arrests and seizures. The National Drug Control Master Plan 2026–2030 outlines four pillars: supply reduction, demand reduction, health and wellbeing, and a coordinated response, including a framework for impact and evaluation.

This policy shift acknowledges that drug issues are multifaceted, encompassing criminal justice, public health, social, economic, and governance challenges. The Master Plan emphasizes a coordinated national approach aimed at not only limiting drug availability but also decreasing demand, improving public health, and enhancing inter-institutional cooperation.

While Mauritius recorded 4,373 drug-related infractions and 2,520 arrests in 2024, these figures are seen as indicators of enforcement activity, not necessarily a shrinking drug market or improved community safety. The focus is now on more complex questions like declining drug dependency, delayed initiation among youth, dismantled trafficking networks, and reduced drug-related hospitalizations and deaths.

Why it mattersThe new drug policy in Mauritius affects citizens by aiming to reduce drug dependency, prevent young people from starting drug use, and decrease drug-related harm and deaths.

MP Beechook Flags Disappearing Rs 8 Baguette

The regulated 200g baguette, priced at Rs 8 and subsidized by taxpayers, is reportedly unavailable in some shops.

Member of Parliament Raviraj Beechook raised concerns in parliament that the regulated 200g baguette, sold at Rs 8, has vanished from store shelves. He noted that only more expensive "premium" versions, costing Rs 10 or Rs 15, are now available. This subsidized bread is intended to ensure affordability for families, and its disappearance alongside a reported decline in quality is a matter Beechook stated he will monitor closely.

This issue of bread quality and availability is not new. In July 2026, local media reported that the Mauritian bread, particularly the "dipin mezon," had become less crispy and flavorful, with a noticeable decrease in overall quality. Online comments also highlighted inconsistencies and a worrying degradation of the product.

The current situation, where the regulated bread is difficult to find and special versions are prioritized, raises questions about access to affordable food. While special breads can coexist, their availability should not come at the expense of the regulated product meant for wider public consumption.

Why it mattersFamilies are affected because the affordable, taxpayer-subsidized baguette is disappearing from shops, replaced by more expensive options.
