Mauritius minister defends decision on cancelled exams

Education Minister Mahend Gungapersad responded to criticism over using assessed marks for SC and HSC exams.

Mauritius's School Certificate (SC) and Higher School Certificate (HSC) exams, cancelled on October 13 due to social unrest and security issues, will not be re-organized. Cambridge confirmed the use of assessed marks for grading. Education Minister Mahend Gungapersad defended this decision yesterday at an event in Phoenix, addressing criticism from former minister Leela Devi Dookun-Luchoomun.

Dookun-Luchoomun had previously called for Gungapersad's resignation on October 26, citing "poor management" and "failure of leadership." She questioned the risk assessment and the timing of informing Cambridge, arguing that assessed grades penalize students. Gungapersad dismissed these criticisms as inconsistent, recalling a similar proposal when he was in opposition in 2021.

The minister stated that the current situation should serve as a lesson. He plans to review the agreement with Cambridge to establish specific mechanisms for exceptional circumstances. This review aims to prevent similar issues in the future and ensure better preparedness for unforeseen events.

Why it mattersStudents in Mauritius will receive grades based on assessed marks instead of taking the cancelled SC and HSC exams.

Mauritius PM meets NGOs after unrest

Prime Minister Navin Ramgoolam initiated dialogue with youth and vulnerable family support groups on October 29.

Prime Minister Navin Ramgoolam led a three-hour meeting with non-governmental organizations on Thursday, October 29, following recent national unrest. The consultation aimed to identify factors contributing to marginalization and exclusion and to address public concerns. Deputy Prime Minister Arianne Navarre-Marie, Minister of Social Integration Ashok Subron, Minister Delegate for Tourism Annabelle Savabaddy, and MP Rubna Daureeawo attended the session.

The government chose to start discussions with NGOs due to their close connection to ground realities and their ability to assess gaps in existing support systems. Topics covered included education, employment, poverty, mental health, drug addiction, domestic and sexual violence, discrimination, and the marginalization of artists. This initial meeting is the first step in a broader consultation process.

Future consultations will involve interfaith committees, government ministries, parastatal bodies, and other key national stakeholders. The government's stated goal is to create a roadmap with a clear timeline, deadlines, and identified responsibilities to ensure verifiable results and provide tangible prospects for young people.

Why it mattersPrime Minister Navin Ramgoolam is meeting with NGOs to address issues affecting vulnerable youth and families after recent unrest.

Mauritius weighs lowering age for criminal responsibility

Government considers prosecuting 12-year-olds after recent riots, but experts cite concerns about system capacity and child welfare.

The Mauritian government is considering lowering the age of criminal responsibility to 12 years old, a move prompted by recent riots where minors were among those arrested. Currently, under the Children’s Act 2020, individuals under 14 cannot be prosecuted. The Cabinet discussed the proposal on October 14, deciding to establish a parliamentary Select Committee for further consultation.

Police reported that 16 minors were among the 151 people arrested during the unrest. However, legal experts note that any change would only apply to future cases due to constitutional protections against retroactive punishment. Past court rulings, including cases from 2010 and 2013, have highlighted significant delays within the Mauritian justice system, raising concerns about the impact of prolonged legal processes on young offenders.

Child-rights bodies and experts advise against lowering the age, with the UN Committee on the Rights of the Child recommending a minimum age of 14. They warn that early contact with the justice system can be detrimental to developing children. The proposed reform would also necessitate changes to the existing Children's Court system, which is designed for individuals aged 14 and above, requiring staff training and facility adjustments.

Why it mattersThe government's consideration of prosecuting 12-year-olds could lead to more children entering a justice system already struggling with delays and potentially harm their development.
