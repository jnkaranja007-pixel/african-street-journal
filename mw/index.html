Malawi firms can now import their own diesel

The Malawi Energy Regulatory Authority (MERA) has opened applications for companies to import diesel directly for their operations.

Applications opened on November 17, 2026, letting major companies in Malawi bring in their own diesel from the Malawi Energy Regulatory Authority (MERA). Firms running large projects can now secure fuel directly for their operations, widening who takes part in fuel importing.

Dad Chinthambi, MERA's chief executive, said in a statement dated November 16, 2026, that the scheme targets companies that use large volumes of diesel daily and already have approved storage or agreements with authorised facilities. The Ministry of Energy backs the scheme, and the Ministry of Finance has agreed exemptions from customs and excise duty to support it.

Applicants must meet all legal and regulatory requirements before they can be licensed, MERA said. Applications close on January 31, 2027, although MERA warned the window could change depending on circumstances.

Why it mattersCompanies that use large volumes of diesel can now apply to import their own fuel, with applications open until January 31, 2027.

Castel Malawi pauses plan to cut 100 jobs after union talks

The brewer agreed with its workers union to suspend the layoffs for three weeks while the union takes its case to government ministries.

Castel Malawi, the country's second-largest taxpayer, has put on hold a plan to retrench 100 employees, which had been due to take effect in the second week of November. Management cited a challenging business environment that it says is affecting the company's operations and sustainability. The two sides agreed to suspend the decision for three weeks.

Camawu President Israel Mughogho said the Castel Malawi Workers Union wants to meet the Ministry of Labour, the Malawi Revenue Authority, and the ministries of trade and finance. He said the market is flooded with smuggled and locally produced illicit beer and spirits, which evade tax and pose health risks, and he called on the government to urgently review beer and spirits taxation. Managing Director Thomas Reynaud confirmed the agreement, saying the union asked for time to persuade the authorities to fix the problems.

The Employers Consultative Association of Malawi's executive director, George Khaki, said high inflation, though easing in recent months, is eroding buyers' purchasing power and reducing demand, which triggers retrenchments. He also cited a persistent shortage of foreign currency that stops employers importing raw materials and forces them to run below normal capacity.

Why it mattersAbout 100 Castel Malawi workers keep their jobs for now, and the three-week pause is the window the union has to win tax and labour changes from government.

Malawi's Chief Justice sends Speaker row to constitutional panel

Mkoka's challenge to 12 proposed parliamentary committees will now be heard by a panel of at least three High Court judges.

Chief Justice Rizine Mzikamanda has certified a case involving Speaker of Parliament Sameer Suleman and citizen Austin Mkoka as a constitutional matter. A panel of at least three High Court judges will now hear it sitting as a Constitutional Court. The decision followed an application by Attorney General Frank Mbeta, who joined the case as an interested party.

Mkoka is challenging the planned formation of 12 parliamentary committees to investigate the operations of some statutory companies, which he says is unconstitutional. Mbeta said the case raises fundamental questions that need judicial interpretation to clarify the powers of Parliament and the Executive. Judiciary spokesperson Ruth Mputeni said the court is waiting to empanel the judges and would do so soon.

The case was due to proceed on November 5, 2026, but was halted by Mbeta's application. High Court Judge Allan Hans Muhome had earlier, on November 3, 2026, referred the matter to the Chief Justice, suspending proceedings until its constitutional status was decided. Proceedings will remain suspended until the panel hears and determines the constitutional questions.

Why it mattersParliament's plan to set up 12 investigative committees cannot move ahead while the court decides whether it is lawful.

Business · 2026-11-17

Lilongwe City Council signs deal for 150 electric buses

The Memorandum of Understanding with Indian firm Mercury EV-Tech Limited also covers an electric vehicle assembly plant in the capital.

Lilongwe City Council has signed a Memorandum of Understanding with Mercury EV-Tech Limited, an Indian electric mobility company, covering the proposed deployment of 150 electric buses in the capital. The first buses are expected to arrive between February and March 2027.

Council Chief Executive Officer Clement Stambuli confirmed the expected arrival period and said Mercury EV-Tech had already registered its operations in Malawi. He described the partnership as an investment opportunity that could bring modern transport technology and open doors for local vehicle assembly and job creation.

The proposed assembly plant is still subject to feasibility assessments, further discussions and regulatory approvals, and commercial terms and an implementation schedule are yet to be finalised. In a disclosure to India's stock exchanges on November 16, 2026, Mercury EV-Tech said the agreement also covers technology transfer, technical support, skills development and capacity building.

Why it mattersResidents of Lilongwe would gain a new public transport option if the first buses arrive between February and March 2027 as expected.

Education · 2026-11-17

Malawi school group defends exam re-marking

The Private Schools Association of Malawi says grade changes after re-marking are a standard procedure, not a conspiracy.

Questions have arisen over changes to Malawi School Certificate of Education (MSCE) results after a candidate's score shifted from 10 points to eight, making them the year's top scorer. The adjustment has led some to suspect manipulation of the examination process.

Ernest Kaonga, president of the Private Schools Association of Malawi (PRISAM), stated that re-marking is a right under MANEB regulations and that grade changes do not automatically indicate wrongdoing. He argued that the continued selection of private school learners into top universities contradicts claims of deliberate targeting.

Kaonga urged critics to provide evidence for their allegations, stating PRISAM would not act on social media claims. He described the re-marking process as an accountability measure and asked for politics to be kept out of education, warning against using learners as tools in result disputes.

Why it mattersThe Private Schools Association of Malawi defended the re-marking of national examination results, a process that affects students seeking university admission.
