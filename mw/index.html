Malawi allows private power producers to feed grid

New net-metering rules let individuals and companies sell surplus renewable energy to the national grid.

Malawi's Energy Minister Jean Mathanga launched new net-metering rules in Lilongwe on Wednesday, allowing private entities to feed surplus electricity from renewable sources into the ESCOM national grid. The country currently generates 564 megawatts, which falls short of rising demand. Mathanga stated that new approaches are needed to increase electricity generation and improve access to reliable power.

The pilot phase of the net-metering initiative identified 34 customers eligible to connect to the national grid, with nine already connected. Minister Mathanga also introduced policies to encourage electric vehicle adoption, which could help ease fuel shortages and lessen dependence on imported fuel. She urged Malawians to protect electricity infrastructure.

Managing Director for Sky Energy Schizzo Thomson said public awareness of electric vehicles could help cut transport costs. The Human Rights Defenders Coalition (HRDC) has threatened demonstrations over the fuel supply crisis, demanding the resignation of Minister Mathanga, who identified a shortage of foreign exchange as the root cause.

Malawi state firms lose K640 billion

World Bank report highlights weak cash flows and declining dividends from government-owned enterprises.

Malawi's state-owned enterprises (SOEs) are financially weak and rely heavily on government support, according to the World Bank's 23rd Malawi Economic Monitor. These parastatals face weak cash flows, struggle to service debts, and contribute declining dividends to the government. The World Bank's analysis, presented in Lilongwe last week, found that SOEs routinely provide goods and services below cost-recovery levels, leading to debt accumulation and reduced service quality.

The hidden costs from these quasi-fiscal activities amount to K640 billion, equivalent to two percent of Malawi's gross domestic product (GDP). Between 2022 and 2025, commercial SOEs received K292.17 billion in government grants alone. Minister of Finance Joseph Mwanamvekha acknowledged the financial weakness and inefficiency but noted that some SOEs perform essential social functions.

Despite a significant expansion in the SOE sector, with total assets rising from 14 percent of GDP in 2019 to 26 percent in 2024, performance has not improved. The World Bank identified technical losses, overstaffing, weak revenue collection, and governance flaws as the true drivers of this decay. Regulatory parastatals, funded by mandatory fees, remain the only consistent performers.

Why it mattersMalawian government-owned companies are losing billions of kwacha, impacting the national economy.

Malawi mourns poet Jack Mapanje

The writer, known for exploring freedom and power, died in the United Kingdom.

Distinguished Malawian poet, linguist, and academic Jack Mapanje has died in the United Kingdom. Mapanje, born on March 25, 1944, built an international reputation for his metaphorical and subtly subversive poetry. His work often examined themes of freedom, power, and belonging, drawing attention during Malawi's one-party era.

The Malawi Writers Union of Academic and Non-fiction Authors paid tribute to Mapanje, hailing his significant contributions to scholarship and literature. His acclaimed collections include "Of Chameleons and Gods" and "The Chattering Wagtails of Mikuyu Prison." His memoir, "And Crocodiles Are Hungry at Night," detailed his experiences of imprisonment, exile, and resilience.

Mapanje's legacy will endure through his body of work and his example as a writer who used language for artistic expression and quiet defiance. For many readers, students, and writers, his influence is profound.

Why it mattersMalawi has lost one of its most influential literary voices, Jack Mapanje, whose writings explored themes of freedom and power.

Malawi mines absent from Commonwealth investment plan

The Commonwealth Investment Platform lists 115 projects worth US$52.9bn but omits mining infrastructure.

Malawi's mining projects are missing from the Commonwealth's US$52.9bn investment pipeline, despite the nation's mines entering production and reforms gaining traction.

The Commonwealth Investment Platform, launched in Antigua, lists 115 projects from 28 developing countries, but includes no mining operations or related infrastructure like power and rail.

Malawi's own submissions to the platform focus on tourism resorts, reflecting the government's growth strategy, while the absence of mining packages highlights a structural issue in how national submissions are compiled.

Why it mattersMalawi's mining sector, poised for production, is not being supported by the Commonwealth's new investment diplomacy.

Malawi farmers need irrigation equipment

Nsanje District farmers can only cultivate half of their land due to a lack of water pumps.

Farmers in Traditional Authority Malemia, Nsanje District, are appealing for irrigation equipment to increase their crop yields. The scheme's chairperson, Mzinga Mulaphandevu, stated on Tuesday that insufficient materials prevent them from using 30 hectares of land, with only 15 hectares currently cultivated. Farmers expressed a desire for more solar-powered water pumps to support irrigation efforts.

Mulaphandevu highlighted that irrigation offers a reliable harvest compared to unpredictable rainfall. Gloria Genti, a farmer from Chipwembwe Village, reported harvesting 12 to 16 bags of maize, 10 bags of rice, and five bags of beans from her plot. However, Mercy Goliati from Lazao Village noted that the current mobile solar-powered pump system requires significant manual labour.

Collins Walasi, an agriculture development officer for Zunde Extension Planning Area, confirmed that farmers are eager to cultivate the entire scheme but lack the necessary equipment. He mentioned that farmers previously used watering cans before Concern Worldwide recently provided a mobile solar-powered pump system.

Why it mattersFarmers in Nsanje District are unable to fully utilize their land for cultivation due to a shortage of irrigation equipment.
