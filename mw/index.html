Meteorology Department Ignored VVIP Flight, Official Testifies

The head of Malawi's weather services confirmed no forecaster was assigned to a fatal flight due to lack of prior notification.

Malawi's Department of Climate Change and Meteorological Services was unaware of the June 10, 2024 VVIP flight to Mzuzu, its director Lucy Mtilatila told a parliamentary committee. This lack of knowledge meant no weather forecaster was deployed to support ground operations for the flight carrying Vice-President Saulos Chilima and eight others. Mtilatila stated that standard procedure requires a dedicated aviation forecaster at both origin and destination airports for such flights to brief the crew and aid operations.

Mtilatila explained that the forecaster at Kamuzu International Airport did not provide weather information to the flight crew because the crew never requested it. She emphasized that if her department had been informed in advance, a forecaster would have been dispatched from Lilongwe to Mzuzu. The department typically receives flight information from the Department of Civil Aviation, but no established protocols were in place for this specific flight, leaving uncertainty about who was responsible for notification.

The parliamentary committee is investigating the circumstances surrounding the fatal crash. Mtilatila recommended mandatory notification for VVIP flights, including clear written protocols on responsibility, notice periods, and consequences for non-compliance. She also proposed a pre-departure checklist that makes weather briefings a condition for flight clearance. The committee noted that while records covered Lilongwe and Mzuzu, German investigators indicated a lack of en-route weather information was provided.

Why it mattersThe lack of weather information and forecaster deployment directly affected the safety of the VVIP flight, leading to the crash.

Court Reinstates Blantyre Water CEO Yeremiah Chihana

The High Court of Malawi has ordered the immediate reinstatement of Yeremiah Chihana as Blantyre Water Board CEO, days after his suspension pending an inquiry.

The High Court of Malawi has reinstated Yeremiah Chihana as chief executive officer of Blantyre Water Board (BWB) following his suspension. Judge Jack N’riva granted Chihana permission to seek judicial review of the BWB board of directors’ decision to suspend him. The court ordered that the suspension and related measures be pended until the determination of the substantive judicial review or further order, and that Chihana be restored to his office, functions, and responsibilities.

Chihana was suspended by the BWB board to allow for a disciplinary inquiry into allegations of misrepresentation, unauthorized appointments, and a K500 million overdraft secured without board approval. Chihana’s lawyer argued that his client was not provided with necessary documentation or an opportunity to address the allegations during the emergency board meeting. This marks the second suspension for Chihana in five months, with a previous court order reinstating him after an earlier suspension.

The BWB board chairperson, Stanely Chirwa, stated he had not yet received the court order. The strained relationship between Chihana and the board has previously prompted the BWB Employees Trade Union to request intervention from the Comptroller of Statutory Corporations due to concerns that the standoff threatens operations. The latest suspension followed allegations from April, including bypassing the board in facilitating arrests of acting directors.

Why it mattersBlantyre Water Board CEO Yeremiah Chihana's reinstatement impacts the stability and leadership of a key public utility, affecting its operational capacity.

US Embassy Clarifies Specimen Sharing for Health Cooperation

Malawi will share biological specimens with Washington during public health emergencies to aid in developing diagnostics, treatments, and vaccines.

The US Embassy in Lilongwe has clarified that biological specimens from Malawi will only be shared with Washington during public health emergencies and disease outbreaks. This sharing is part of a five-year, $936 million health cooperation agreement where the US will provide up to $792 million to support Malawi's efforts against HIV and Aids, malaria, and other infectious diseases. The agreement, signed on February 11, 2026, also aims to strengthen disease surveillance and outbreak response, and enhance global health security.

Embassy spokesperson Amy Petersen stated that these agreements codify existing practices and that rapid specimen sharing enables a coordinated response to public health emergencies and accelerates the development of medical countermeasures. She emphasized that no personally identifiable information will be shared with the US government or private American companies. Safeguards are in place, with the Malawi government requiring authorization for any specimen sharing in accordance with laws and protocols.

Under the agreement, Malawi commits to sharing requested specimens within seven days of a US request. In return, the US pledges priority access to any resulting diagnostics, vaccines, or therapeutics after meeting its own domestic needs. The Society of Medical Doctors president noted that the agreement offers opportunities for early detection, faster responses, innovation, and capacity building with external partners.

Why it mattersMalawians will benefit from faster development of diagnostics, treatments, and vaccines for infectious diseases through the sharing of biological specimens.

Forgotten crops gain traction amid climate change

Malawian farmers are rediscovering sorghum and cowpeas, drought-resistant indigenous crops, as rainfall becomes less reliable.

In Malawi, indigenous crops like sorghum and cowpeas are re-emerging as vital agricultural assets due to increasingly unreliable rainfall patterns linked to climate change. A recent seed fair in Chikwawa District showcased products made from these traditional grains, highlighting their versatility and nutritional value. These crops, once staples, had been sidelined by market preferences for maize and government support programs that favored hybrid seeds and fertilizers.

The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), in collaboration with the Ministry of Agriculture, is actively promoting these drought-resilient varieties. Initiatives like the Awsame project are training researchers and extension officers in data collection and water management techniques to better support these crops. Government experts are evaluating various cowpea and sorghum varieties under different conditions to identify those best suited for dry environments.

The revival of sorghum and cowpeas is seen as a strategy to adapt to water scarcity, enhance biodiversity, and create new income opportunities. Historically, these crops were crucial for food security and soil health in Malawi, particularly in the Shire Valley. Their decline accelerated after independence as agricultural focus shifted, but their inherent resilience is now driving renewed interest and research.

Why it mattersFarmers in disaster-prone areas are returning to sorghum and cowpeas to adapt to climate change and improve household nutrition.

Teen Mothers Gain Farming Skills for Backyard Gardens

Native Youth Animators for Development has equipped 20 teen mothers in Nsanje District with skills to manage backyard gardens for economic resilience.

Native Youth Animators for Development has equipped 20 teen mothers in Nsanje District with farming skills to manage backyard gardens, aiming to build their resilience against economic hardships. The day-long orientation covered nutritious meal preparation, the importance of diverse food groups for children, and permaculture farming approaches. The organization also taught participants how to produce compost manure from kitchen waste and crop residues, funded by the UK-based Hilden Charitable Fund.

Jonas January, executive director of Native Youth Animators for Development, stated that this is a new project targeting adolescent and teen mothers aged 15 to 19 who are mentees at Jeke Village. The goal is to empower them economically and improve their nutrition and well-being through backyard gardens, with the hope that they can recover from the effects of past disasters. Agriculture extension development officer Flyton Flyton highlighted that vegetable production is a lucrative business that can economically empower the girls.

Participants like Yana Kalonga, 19, and Steria James, 18, expressed that the training was informative. They learned about food security, nutrition, site selection, soil preparation, seed selection, planting techniques, and compost manure production. The teen mothers see the backyard gardens as a vital source for feeding their children nutritious food and as a future income stream from selling vegetables and fruits.

Why it mattersTwenty teen mothers in Nsanje District are gaining skills in backyard gardening to improve their nutrition and create income sources, thereby building economic resilience.
