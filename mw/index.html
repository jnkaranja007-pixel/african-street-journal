Mutharika directive plunges Malawi into constitutional crisis

President Peter Mutharika's order to postpone Parliament is being challenged by commentators and government insiders.

Malawi President Peter Mutharika has been criticized for his directive to postpone Parliament, with commentators stating he is plunging the nation into a constitutional crisis. They argue the President cannot unilaterally order the Speaker to delay an ordinary parliamentary meeting. The directive, dated October 23, 2026, instructs the Speaker to postpone the fourth meeting of the 52nd session until the next Budget Meeting, citing Section 59(1) of the Constitution.

Cabinet sources revealed the directive caused shockwaves within the administration and the ruling Democratic Progressive Party (DPP), with one senior official stating, "Malawi is not at war, nor in a state of emergency. This needs to be challenged." Opposition members allege the postponement aims to shield Mutharika from Parliament due to his alleged ill health, though his medical condition remains unverified. The core constitutional issue revolves around Mutharika's use of "I direct," as Section 59(1) assigns the commencement of ordinary meetings to the Speaker in consultation with the President.

Some Cabinet sources questioned the origin of the instruction, suggesting a minister acting as de facto president might be behind it, though no evidence was supplied. While Section 59(3) requires the President to open a parliamentary session, the postponement of a meeting within an existing session does not inherently constitute avoiding a constitutionally required opening appearance.

Why it mattersPresident Mutharika's directive to postpone Parliament has created a constitutional crisis in Malawi.

WFP cuts Malawi food aid by 33.3%

200,000 people risk hunger as the World Food Programme faces a $142.3 million funding shortfall.

The World Food Programme (WFP) has reduced its planned humanitarian assistance for Malawi's lean season by 33.3 percent, cutting the target from 600,000 to 400,000 people. This reduction means 200,000 individuals face potential hunger between October 2026 and March 2027 due to funding constraints. WFP operations face a $142.3 million shortfall, forcing a narrowed geographical focus to only Chikwawa, Nsanje, and Phalombe districts.

The funding gap threatens essential programs including emergency food aid, nutrition support, school feeding, and refugee assistance. WFP stated that vulnerable households will lack support during a period of high food insecurity, likely leading them to eat fewer meals and adopt negative coping strategies. Centre for Agricultural Research and Development Director Innocent Pangapanga Phiri warned this could worsen food insecurity and increase reliance on climate shock coping mechanisms.

Centre for Humanitarian Assistance and Social Protection Executive Director Kenneth Sakala recommended the government reallocate resources and expand the Social Cash Transfer Programme. He also advised pre-positioning maize and cash before January price peaks and funding last-mile transport. Phiri urged strengthening food price monitoring and market stabilisation, including maize price monitoring and addressing supply bottlenecks.

Why it matters200,000 Malawians face hunger due to reduced food aid from the World Food Programme.

Malawi health unions warn of strike over staffing crisis

10 unions demand government address vacancies and unpaid allowances within 14 days or halt services.

A coalition of 10 health worker unions in Malawi has issued a 14-day ultimatum to the government to resolve critical staffing shortages, unpaid allowances, and poor hospital conditions. The Human Resource for Health Coalition warned of a nationwide strike that would disrupt healthcare services if their demands are not met.

Coalition chairperson Solomon David Chomba stated that the group petitioned President Peter Mutharika about the worsening health sector crisis. The coalition reported severe staff shortages, with nursing and midwifery vacancies at 54% despite 6,000 qualified nurses being unemployed. Clinician vacancies exceed 75%, with 5,500 trained clinicians also jobless.

The unions also highlighted a 60% vacancy rate in pharmacy services, noting 118 pharmacists, 413 pharmacy technicians, and 1,250 pharmacy assistants have not been recruited. Medical doctors, radiographers, sonographers, and laboratory professionals are also among the unemployed trained health workers.

Why it mattersMalawians face disruptions to healthcare services due to a potential strike by health workers over staffing and pay issues.

Reserve Bank of Malawi withdraws K80.6 billion

Central bank tightens monetary policy to combat inflation despite recent price decreases.

The Reserve Bank of Malawi (RBM) removed K80.6 billion from the financial market last week through open market operations. This action is part of the central bank's ongoing tight monetary policy aimed at controlling inflation. The withdrawals included K68.5 billion via overnight operations, K10 billion through 62-day agreements, and K2.1 billion via 60-day agreements.

RBM figures show that inflation eased to 19.4 percent in September 2026, falling below 20 percent for the first time since May 2022. Despite this decrease, the central bank maintained its policy rate at 24 percent. Malawi Economic Justice Network Executive Director Bertha Phiri stated that the withdrawal reduces aggregate demand and money supply growth, complementing the policy rate.

Economist Marvin Banda suggested the withdrawal should be viewed alongside fiscal challenges, noting government borrowing's contribution to inflationary pressures. The government forecasts inflation to reach 15 percent by the end of 2026, while the IMF projects it to average 21.9 percent in 2026 and rise to 25 percent in 2027.

Why it mattersThe Reserve Bank of Malawi's withdrawal of K80.6 billion from the market could increase borrowing costs for businesses.

Malawi justice minister vows tougher cybercrime laws

Charles Mhango announced new legislation to trace bank accounts and identify online fraudsters.

Malawi's Justice Minister Charles Mhango announced plans for tougher laws to combat online scammers. The proposed legislation aims to equip authorities with the ability to trace suspicious bank accounts and identify cybercriminals. Mhango made the vow on Tuesday at the National Cyber Drill and Cybersecurity Conference 2026 in Lilongwe.

The minister stated that cybercrime poses a serious and growing threat to Malawians, necessitating urgent action to bolster the nation's digital defenses. He emphasized that new laws must provide investigators with tools to follow money trails and apprehend those behind fraudulent transactions. Mhango called for enhanced cooperation among banks, mobile money operators, MACRA, and law enforcement agencies to defeat cybercrime.

The conference, themed “Strengthening National Cyber Resilience Through Collaboration and Preparedness,” gathered cybersecurity stakeholders including MACRA, banks, mobile money operators, and law enforcement. Mhango specifically highlighted the need to protect vulnerable groups like women, girls, and rural residents from online exploitation.

Why it mattersMalawians face new laws designed to track and prosecute online scammers.
