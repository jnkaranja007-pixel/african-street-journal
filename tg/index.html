ECOWAS court presses Togo on constitutional reform

The regional court sent a reminder to Togo's Justice Minister about its January 29, 2026 ruling.

The Court of Justice of the Economic Community of West African States (ECOWAS) has sent a reminder to Togo’s Minister of Justice regarding its January 29, 2026 ruling. The regional court had previously determined that the constitutional reform passed in March 2024 constituted an unconstitutional change of government.

The ECOWAS court's letter follows a conference in Lomé discussing the power of regional courts. This action by the court questions the Togolese government's response to the January ruling.

The ECOWAS court is seeking an update on the actions taken by Togo following its judgment on the constitutional reform.

Why it mattersTogo's government is being questioned by a regional court over a constitutional reform it passed.

Burkina Faso reinstates death penalty for economic crimes

The new penal code also imposes life imprisonment and triple fines for public fund embezzlement.

Burkina Faso officially reinstated the death penalty on October 20, 2026, with the unanimous adoption of a new penal code by its transitional legislative assembly. The 71 deputies approved capital punishment for individuals involved in terrorism, espionage, or treason, according to Justice Minister Edasso Rodrigue Bayala.

The revised code also targets economic and financial crimes, making perpetrators or accomplices of importing expired or prohibited products that lead to death liable for the death penalty. For public fund embezzlement, offenders face life imprisonment and fines triple the amount stolen, alongside significantly increased fines for all offenses.

This move by Burkina Faso, initiated by military leader Captain Ibrahim Traoré in December 2025, has drawn condemnation from human rights groups like Amnesty International and Human Rights Watch, who fear it could be used to suppress dissent. The strict application of such a law in Togo, a neighbor with a history of economic crimes without accountability since 2005, raises questions about its potential impact.

Why it mattersTogo's proximity to Burkina Faso and its own history of unpunished economic crimes raise questions about the potential application of similar strict laws.

UDS-TOGO calls for national dialogue amid political deadlock

The party seeks constitutional reforms and an amnesty law to foster national reconciliation and restore order.

The Union of Socialist Democrats of Togo (UDS-TOGO) called for an inclusive national consultation on October 24, 2026, in Lomé to address the country's political impasse. The party advocates for institutional and electoral reforms, socioeconomic measures, and an amnesty law to promote national reconciliation.

This call follows a ruling by the ECOWAS Court of Justice on January 29, 2026, which deemed Togo's March 25, 2024, constitutional revision an unconstitutional change of government. UDS-TOGO stated this ruling politically and morally damages the current administration and its legitimacy.

The party believes the court's decision provides international legal backing for Togolese citizens and democratic forces seeking to restore constitutional order and popular sovereignty. UDS-TOGO insists that the current constitution, not born from national consensus, will continue to be seen as an imposed diktat.

Why it mattersThe political deadlock and calls for reform directly affect the governance and constitutional framework of Togo.

Lomé university hosts legal debate on judicial sovereignty

Jurists from eight countries discuss limits of regional court power over national laws.

The University of Lomé hosted a two-day conference starting October 29, 2026, bringing together legal scholars and political scientists from eight countries. The event focused on the extent to which regional judges can exercise authority without infringing upon the sovereignty of member states, a question heightened by a recent ECOWAS Court ruling concerning Togo.

Organized by the university's Public Law Centre, the conference examined regional human rights courts' role in protecting the rule of law, overseeing constitutional changes, and respecting state sovereignty. University President Pr Kossivi Hounaké stated the goal was to analyze the court's decisions without blind celebration or outright rejection based on closed sovereignty, emphasizing that a ruling is only fully effective when understood and applied.

Discussions included panels on regional oversight of constitutional revisions, the authority of national constitutional courts versus the ECOWAS Court, and the legal force of international protocols. A roundtable on Friday, October 30, addressed community protection of human rights, democracy, and the rule of law, including judicial dialogue and citizen access to regional courts.

Why it mattersTogolese legal and political experts are debating the limits of regional court power over national sovereignty following a recent ECOWAS Court decision affecting Togo.
