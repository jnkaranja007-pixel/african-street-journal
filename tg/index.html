Wycliffe-Togo Mobilizes Resources for Local Bible Translations

The organization faces challenges in translating the Bible into maternal languages, requiring significant human and financial resources.

Wycliffe-Togo held its ordinary general assembly on August 29, 2026, in Lomé, focusing on the significant challenges of translating the Bible into local languages. The organization highlighted the need for linguistic, cultural, and technological expertise, alongside substantial human and financial investment. Sépényon Komla Egou, President of the Board, described Bible translation as a demanding, long-term endeavor requiring time, energy, and diverse resources.

The importance of using local languages for Bible translation was emphasized by both Egou and Samuel Tinnidjotobe Kpagheri, Wycliffe-Togo's Executive Director. They stated that using maternal languages facilitates a deeper and more natural understanding of biblical messages, making them more accessible. Projects are currently underway for Lama, Kabyè, Ifè, and Moba languages, with the New Testament in Akébou taking seventeen years to complete.

Facing declining external funding, Wycliffe-Togo is calling for increased involvement from local churches, missionary organizations, and individual Christians. Missiologist Pr Nouwodé Kwassi Adjé stressed that the church's mission is integral to its purpose and future, warning that neglecting this mission leads to a loss of vocation. The organization urges the Christian community to unite in sustained support for this missionary work.

Why it mattersThe translation of the Bible into local languages is crucial for ensuring that religious texts are more directly and deeply understood by Togolese citizens in their daily lives.

Ex-Togo Minister Detained Nearly One Year Without Trial

Former Army Minister Marguerite Gnakadé faces charges of inciting uprising and remains in custody since September 2025.

Essossimna Marguerite Gnakadé, Togo's former Army Minister, has been detained for almost a year. She was arrested on September 17, 2025, at her Lomé residence following public statements against the government. Four days later, she was formally charged and placed under arrest.

The justice system accuses Gnakadé of "incitation to uprising against the authority of the state." Her trial date has not yet been set, leaving her in prolonged pre-trial detention. The specific conditions of her confinement are not detailed in the available information.

Gnakadé's arrest followed several public stances she took that were critical of the current administration. She previously served as the Army Minister under President Faure Essozimna Gnassingbé before her detention.

Why it mattersFormer government minister Marguerite Gnakadé is affected by prolonged pre-trial detention and charges of inciting uprising.

Togolese Repatriated From South Africa Amid Xenophobic Violence

Around twenty Togolese citizens returned to Lomé on Sunday, August 30, under a voluntary repatriation program supported by the Togolese state.

The Togolese government facilitated the return of approximately twenty nationals from South Africa on Sunday, August 30. This voluntary repatriation operation saw the individuals arrive back in Lomé, the capital city. The return occurs against a backdrop of rising xenophobic violence in South Africa, which has led to difficulties for Togolese citizens in obtaining or renewing their residency permits.

The United Nations Committee for the Elimination of Racial Discrimination (CERD) issued an alert on August 27 detailing the severity of the situation. The committee reported at least four migrants killed and fifty thousand people forcibly displaced since January, alongside incidents of kidnapping and looting targeting foreigners. Other African nations, including Nigeria, Ghana, Malawi, Zimbabwe, and Mozambique, have also conducted similar repatriation efforts.

The Togolese government has not provided details regarding potential future returns for its citizens still in South Africa. The current operation addresses immediate concerns for those who have returned, but the broader issue of xenophobic violence and its impact on foreign nationals in South Africa remains a significant concern highlighted by international bodies.

Why it mattersTogolese citizens in South Africa are directly affected by xenophobic violence, leading to forced displacement and difficulties with legal status.

Belarusian Businessman Expands Influence with Togo President

Alexander Zingman, a Belarusian businessman with a US passport, is reportedly strengthening his ties with Togolese President Faure Gnassingbé.

Alexander Zingman, a 59-year-old businessman holding a US passport, is consolidating his influence with Togolese President Faure Gnassingbé, according to Africa Intelligence. Zingman, who heads Dubai-based Aftrade DMCC, has been active on the continent for about a decade, previously focusing on the Democratic Republic of Congo and Zimbabwe. He is now strategically repositioning towards French-speaking Africa, from Lomé to Antananarivo.

Since 2024, Zingman's presence alongside President Gnassingbé has reportedly prepared the logistical groundwork for the port of Lomé to become a new corridor for Russian arms in the Sahel in 2026. Zingman is said to be currently organizing a trip for President Gnassingbé to Minsk.

In parallel, Zingman is facilitating the upcoming opening of the Togolese embassy in Moscow. His business activities have previously been identified with the DRC and Zimbabwe, but he is now focusing on French-speaking African nations.

Why it mattersThe businessman's growing influence may affect Togo's international relations and trade partnerships, particularly concerning Russian arms and diplomatic ties with Moscow.

Togo President Attends Shanghai Cooperation Organisation Summit

President Faure Gnassingbé joined world leaders in Kyrgyzstan to discuss global governance, security, and economic development.

Togo's President, Faure Essozimna Gnassingbé, is attending the 26th summit of the Shanghai Cooperation Organisation (SCO) in Bishkek, Kyrgyzstan. The summit, themed "25 Years of the SCO: Together for Lasting Peace, Development, and Prosperity," began on August 31, 2026. President Gnassingbé is participating alongside other leaders, including Egypt's Abdel Fattah al-Sissi, to represent Africa and contribute to discussions on global governance challenges.

The summit agenda includes mechanisms to combat terrorism, violent extremism, and cybercrime, alongside policies for infrastructure development, connectivity, and the creation of an independent financial architecture. President Gnassingbé is also set to address the security and humanitarian situation in West Africa and the broader continent, highlighting Togo's contributions to finding sustainable solutions.

The SCO, established in 2001, comprises about ten countries aiming to strengthen regional peace, security, and stability, and to foster a democratic, just, and rational international political and economic order. The organization is also seeking to establish a partnership with the African Union based on dialogue and shared interests between Asian and African nations.

Why it mattersTogo's participation in the SCO summit allows its leader to engage in discussions on global economic and security issues that could impact the nation's development and international standing.
