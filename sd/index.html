Sudanese refugees walk 20km for aid in eastern Chad

Dozens of refugees trekked through heavy rain to demand food and cash assistance after arriving five to eight months ago.

Dozens of Sudanese refugees walked approximately 20 kilometers from Marra refugee camp in eastern Chad to Amliouna to demand humanitarian assistance. Those who left Mile refugee camp arrived five to eight months ago and have not received food or cash aid for about four months. They are now heading to the National Commission for Refugee Reception and Registration to complain and seek solutions. The journey occurred during heavy rain and strong winds on Monday evening.

The refugees are calling for regular delivery of food or cash assistance. This action follows a similar protest last year by refugees from Kaga camp who walked 55 kilometers to Farchana demanding better conditions. The current group seeks to ensure authorities address their ongoing needs for essential supplies and financial support.

Separately, heavy rain and strong winds on Monday afternoon damaged shelters at Mile refugee camp near Guéréda, injuring some residents. Refugee Ishaq Adam reported that many tents were destroyed, with contents swept away, leaving numerous refugees, including children and the elderly, without shelter. He appealed for urgent aid, including food, water, medicine, and shelter materials.

Why it mattersSudanese refugees in eastern Chad are affected by the lack of food and cash assistance and damaged shelters due to heavy rains, leaving them without basic necessities.

Sudan Farmers Split Over New Associations Amid Drought Fears

El Gezira farmers are divided over new agricultural groups while North Darfur faces food insecurity due to delayed rains.

Farmers in Sudan’s El Gezira Scheme are split over the formation of new agricultural and livestock associations. The El Gezira Scheme General Assembly reported broad farmer participation in establishing these groups, aiming to address scheme challenges like irrigation, financing, and marketing. However, the El Gezira and El Managil Farmers’ Alliance rejected the new associations, accusing the General Assembly of bloc-building and regulatory violations, despite objections from some members. The process involved establishing 1,982 grassroots associations and 21 specialized organizations.

Meanwhile, communities in El Sarif locality, North Darfur, are warning of severe threats to their farming season, livestock, and food security due to delayed rains. Bashir Ibrahim Haroun described the situation as “very dangerous,” noting that nine of his cows have already died and families are moving cattle in search of pasture and water. The outlook is particularly grim in El Sarif and surrounding areas like Saraf Umra and Kutum.

Residents of El Sarif are urging humanitarian organizations, UN agencies, and the international community to provide urgent food and livestock assistance. They warn that continued rainfall shortages could deepen food insecurity and increase famine risks across North Darfur. The delayed rains have become a direct threat to livelihoods, impacting areas including Ambro, Karnoi, El Tina, and Kabkabiya.

Why it mattersFarmers in El Gezira are divided over new organizational structures, while North Darfur faces potential famine due to drought impacting crops and livestock.

Sudanese Syndicate Demands Answers on Five Missing Reporters

The Sudanese Journalists’ Syndicate is demanding immediate disclosure of the fate and whereabouts of five members who have been forcibly disappeared.

The Sudanese Journalists’ Syndicate has stated that five of its members are victims of enforced disappearance, with their current status and locations unknown. The syndicate described these disappearances as severe violations of fundamental rights, international law, and the journalists' right to life and personal safety. They are calling for an immediate revelation of the journalists' fates and locations, alongside a thorough, independent inquiry into the circumstances surrounding their disappearances.

The syndicate expressed deep concern that these cases have persisted for months without any reliable information regarding the journalists' well-being, leaving them vulnerable to significant physical and psychological harm. They warned that enforced disappearances during wartime, characterized by widespread violence, hate speech, and misinformation, heighten the risks for those missing, fostering an environment where impunity and further abuses can thrive. The syndicate emphasized that practicing journalism or expressing opinions does not justify arrest, disappearance, or retaliation.

The syndicate further urged the United Nations and international human rights organizations to act urgently to ascertain the fate of the missing journalists. They called for pressure on conflict parties to ensure the safety and release of the reporters and to hold accountable those responsible for abuses. The syndicate stressed that journalists are not combatants and that targeting them harms individuals and undermines the practice of journalism.

Why it mattersFive Sudanese journalists are victims of enforced disappearance, raising concerns about their safety and the rule of law.

South Sudanese Media Establish Independent Ethics Body

Journalists in South Sudan are forming their own ethics body to improve professionalism and self-regulation, bypassing government control.

South Sudanese media professionals are initiating the creation of an independent ethics body to enhance journalistic standards and professionalism. The Association for Media Development in South Sudan (AMDISS) announced the move, stating the body will be managed by media practitioners themselves, not the government. This initiative aims to address concerns about competence and ethics within the profession.

Ayaa Irene, Executive Director of AMDISS, explained that the media sector is in the early stages of establishing this self-regulatory mechanism. Consultations with journalists and stakeholders are planned to determine its structure and operations. The goal is to create clearer professional conduct standards and a system for addressing complaints before they can be used to justify media restrictions.

However, Irene emphasized that strengthening media professionalism must be coupled with reforms to media regulatory institutions. She criticized government appointments to boards of key media bodies, arguing it undermines their independence and effectiveness. Irene cited an instance where 20 journalists were expelled without board consultation, highlighting the challenges faced when management bypasses oversight.

Why it mattersSouth Sudanese journalists are creating an independent ethics body to self-regulate, aiming to improve professionalism and avoid government interference in media operations.
