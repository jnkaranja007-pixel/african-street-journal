400 inmates escape East Darfur prison after drone attack

Officials blamed the Sudanese army for drone strikes on Al-Jalabi Prison, leading to the escape of hundreds of inmates.

Approximately 400 inmates escaped from Al-Jalabi Prison in East Darfur on Monday, October 19, following drone attacks. Local officials, including Ahmed Issa Abou, a legal adviser to the RSF-aligned East Darfur state government, attributed the strikes to the Sudanese army. Abou stated that the attack on the civilian facility, which held over 400 inmates convicted of or awaiting trial for criminal offenses like murder and theft, caused the mass escape. He denied the presence of prisoners of war or political detainees.

The East Darfur state health ministry reported that 34 people were killed or injured in the drone attacks, which involved four drones hitting the prison. The director general, Al-Azraq Hassan Himida, described the prison as a civilian facility and accused the army of violating inmates' rights. He called for investigations into attacks on civilian infrastructure, including prisons and hospitals, by international organizations.

The prison strike reportedly followed an attack on a restaurant. Journalist Ali Al-Rafiq condemned the strike on the prison as a grave crime and expressed concern over attacks on civilian infrastructure. The Sudanese army has been at war with the Rapid Support Forces since April 2023, with front lines contested across several regions, leading to widespread displacement and infrastructure damage.

Why it mattersInmates convicted of or awaiting trial for criminal offenses escaped from prison following drone attacks.

Sudan talks resume in Geneva amid deepening crisis

Representatives from 21 countries and five international institutions met to discuss ceasefires, security, and political solutions to the ongoing conflict.

Talks aimed at halting the fighting in Sudan and easing the humanitarian crisis resumed in Geneva on Wednesday. The two-day meeting brought together international and regional partners to coordinate peace initiatives and explore a path towards a ceasefire and a peaceful political transition. The conflict, which began in April 2023 between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces, has led to deadly drone attacks targeting civilians and infrastructure.

Pekka Haavisto, the United Nations secretary-general’s personal envoy for Sudan, urged delegates to identify practical measures and use their channels of engagement to build confidence and create conditions for a comprehensive ceasefire. Switzerland’s state secretary, Alexandre Fasel, highlighted that civilians are suffering and dying today, and protections under international law, including the Geneva Conventions, must be upheld. He noted that famine-like conditions persist in Darfur and Kordofan states, driven by the war.

Nearly 34 million people across Sudan need humanitarian assistance, according to the United Nations Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs. UNICEF warned that eight million Sudanese children are out of school, a situation described as a violation of the right to education and a risk factor for a stable future. Conflict-related sexual violence has re-emerged as a tactic to terrorize the civilian population.

Why it mattersNearly 34 million Sudanese people need humanitarian assistance, and eight million children are out of school due to the ongoing conflict.

UNICEF condemns killing of 12 children in Sudan attacks

The agency warns that displacement camps and residential areas offer no safety for children amid escalating conflict.

UNICEF has condemned the reported killing of at least 12 children in separate attacks in Sudan’s West Kordofan and Khartoum states. Nine children aged between five and 10 were reportedly killed on Saturday, October 17, in an attack on a camp for displaced families east of Sanakit in West Kordofan. Two days later, an armed drone struck a residential area in Khartoum, reportedly killing three children aged between two and six, along with their father.

The agency stated that families cannot protect their children by fleeing their homes or returning to neighbourhoods, as neither location offers reliable protection. Angela Kearney, UNICEF’s acting representative in Sudan, noted that children were killed in a safety camp in West Kordofan and in their own Khartoum neighbourhood. She emphasized that every party to the conflict must protect children, wherever they are.

UNICEF called on all parties to the conflict to meet their obligations under international humanitarian law and protect civilians and civilian infrastructure. The deaths occur amid an escalation in drone attacks across Sudan, where the war has devastated communities and forced millions from their homes, with children facing deadly violence after more than three years of conflict.

Why it mattersChildren in Sudan are directly affected by the conflict, with at least 12 killed in attacks on displacement camps and residential areas.

Drones strike Khartoum airport, disrupt flights

Repeated drone attacks attributed to the RSF have targeted areas around the capital's airport, causing civilian casualties and raising safety concerns.

Khartoum International Airport has been the target of five successive waves of drone attacks, disrupting flights and raising passenger safety concerns. The attacks, attributed to the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), have struck seven locations across the city, killing a man and four children. While air defences reportedly intercepted some threats, a drone fell in an unoccupied area east of the runway. Aviation experts are calling for a temporary suspension of commercial operations pending an independent risk assessment.

The disruptions have left passengers uncertain about their travel plans, with some arriving at the airport only to find flights cancelled without prior notification. One passenger expressed frustration over the lack of official communication, noting that her cancelled flight still appeared as scheduled in the booking system while another departed on time. An airport official, however, maintained that flights were operating normally and that air traffic had not been affected by the bombardment.

Sudan's national carrier, Sudan Airways, continued operations, with one flight from Cairo landing and later departing for Port Sudan. An adviser to the head of Sudan’s Sovereignty Council stated that the airport was operating normally and accused unnamed parties of spreading rumours of closure to hinder the return of normal life. Despite the ongoing attacks, authorities appear determined to maintain airport operations.

Why it mattersPassengers are affected by flight disruptions and uncertainty due to drone attacks near Khartoum airport.

Sudanese refugees face deportation from Egypt

Hundreds of thousands of Sudanese seeking safety in Egypt are now at risk of arrest and forced return amid tighter immigration controls.

Sudanese refugees and asylum seekers in Egypt face growing fear of arrest and deportation, despite having initially found safety in the country. Egyptian authorities have conducted a campaign of arbitrary arrests and detentions targeting Sudanese nationals, with hundreds of thousands having fled to Egypt since the war began. This situation extends beyond Egypt, with restrictions also emerging along regional migration routes, complicating efforts for those fleeing the conflict to find secure havens.

Human Rights Watch reported in July that Egyptian authorities arrested individuals with expired residency permits, including some holding valid UNHCR documentation, leading to deportations. Reuters documented over 5,500 deportations since November 2025 and reported the deaths of three Sudanese refugees in Egyptian prisons this year. Amnesty International previously recorded incidents of Egyptian authorities deporting around 800 Sudanese nationals without allowing them to claim asylum.

The crackdown creates a precarious reality for Sudanese in Egypt, where community solidarity exists alongside constant anxiety about immigration enforcement. For many, the challenge is no longer just finding refuge but securing a place where they can remain legally, access essential services like healthcare and education, and reunite with family, rather than being forcibly returned to an active warzone.

Why it mattersSudanese refugees are affected by Egyptian authorities' arbitrary arrests, detentions, and deportations, which threaten their safety and legal status.
