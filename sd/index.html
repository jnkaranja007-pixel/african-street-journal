Aid group supplies water to Sudan camps as displacement grows

Médecins Sans Frontières has provided over one million litres of water to thousands displaced by conflict in South Kordofan.

Thousands of people displaced by Sudan’s war are living in temporary shelters in South Kordofan State with limited access to basic services, particularly water. The International Organization for Migration estimates 57,880 displaced people are in Abu Jubayhah locality, with local authorities suggesting the number is higher. Conditions in camps near Abu Jubayhah town centre show the growing needs of displaced families, many of whom live in small tents or straw structures.

Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF) began its response in June, initially trucking water to the Al Safa camps before drilling a borehole and installing a solar-powered pump. The organization has supplied over one million litres of water, reducing the distance women and children need to travel. Previously, residents walked at least three kilometres daily for water, with some families going a week without it. MSF also repaired latrines and provided cleaning materials.

Beyond water, MSF is addressing other health needs, including treating children for severe diarrhoea and malaria. The organization has established a clinic at a reception centre for new arrivals, screening for malaria, malnutrition, and maternal health issues. MSF also supports a Ministry of Health vaccination campaign for measles, rubella, polio, and yellow fever.

Why it mattersDisplaced families in South Kordofan are affected by the lack of access to clean water and essential healthcare services.

Sudan Farmers Divided Over New Associations Amid Drought Fears

El Gezira Scheme farmers are split on new agricultural bodies, while North Darfur faces a food security crisis due to delayed rains and livestock deaths.

Farmers in Sudan’s El Gezira Scheme are divided over the formation of new agricultural and livestock associations. The El Gezira and El Managil Farmers’ Alliance rejected the new bodies, stating they do not represent farmers' interests and accusing organizers of exclusion. Despite objections and calls for postponement, the General Assembly proceeded with elections, establishing 1,982 grassroots, 123 qualitative, and 21 specialised organisations. Mustafa El Tarifi, a member of the General Assembly, stated the new organisation aims to address challenges in irrigation, financing, and marketing to boost production and technology adoption.

Meanwhile, communities in El Sarif locality, North Darfur, are warning of a severe threat to the farming season, livestock, and food security due to delayed rainfall. Bashir Ibrahim Haroun reported that nine of his cows have died and families are moving cattle in search of pasture and water. The situation is described as "very dangerous," with fears of deepening food insecurity and famine risks if rainfall shortages continue.

Residents of El Sarif and surrounding areas, including Saraf Umra, Kutum, Ambro, Karnoi, El Tina, and Kabkabiya, are facing a dire outlook. Haroun urged humanitarian organizations, UN agencies, and the international community to provide urgent food and livestock assistance. The delayed rains have become a direct threat to food security and livelihoods across North Darfur, impacting families and their herds.

Sudan's Emergency Response Rooms' Coordination Council Faces Imbalance

A founding document grants disproportionate influence to Khartoum-based groups within the grassroots humanitarian network.

Sudan's Emergency Response Rooms (ERRs), lauded for their grassroots humanitarian efforts, face internal challenges regarding representation. The Localisation Coordination Council (LCC), established in November 2023 to manage coordination and engage with international partners, has a founding charter that allocates seven seats to Khartoum's localities while granting only one seat to every other state, irrespective of population or humanitarian need. This structure, born from the crisis's scale and donor demands, was intended to create a recognizable body for funding.

This imbalance means that ERRs based in Khartoum hold disproportionate sway over agenda-setting, donor engagement, and funding priorities. Volunteers from states outside the capital have reported feeling that their concerns are not adequately represented in LCC discussions, and that decisions often reflect Khartoum's perspective rather than the movement's collective needs. The situation is particularly acute for Women's Response Rooms, which focus on the specific needs of women and girls.

The ERRs have been instrumental in sustaining communal kitchens, health centers, water systems, and evacuation networks across thirteen states since April 2023, reaching millions in areas inaccessible to international aid organizations. Their efforts have garnered international recognition, including a Nobel Peace Prize nomination. However, the internal debate over who speaks for the movement and how representatives are chosen highlights a structural issue within their coordination body.

Why it mattersKhartoum-based ERRs have disproportionate influence over agenda-setting, donor engagement, and funding priority decisions, affecting how aid is distributed across different states.

Sudanese Groups Reject African Union Dialogue Initiative

The civilian coalition 'Somoud' and the 'Tassiss and Somoud' alliance criticized the AU's welcoming of a government-led dialogue, deeming it a 'farce' that ignores the ongoing war.

The African Union Commission welcomed a Sudanese-led dialogue initiative, stating its aim to foster national unity and establish a democratic state. The commission emphasized the need for inclusivity, involving all civilian, political, and societal actors, to achieve a unified, sovereign, and peaceful Sudan governed by legitimate civilian institutions. This initiative is seen as reinforcing the AU's roadmap and the Quintet Mechanism strategy, driven by Sudanese stakeholders.

However, the civilian coalition 'Somoud' and the 'Tassiss and Somoud' alliance strongly condemned the AU's statement, calling it a 'farce' that disregards the ongoing conflict and humanitarian crisis. They argued that the dialogue, initiated by the current regime, lacks credibility, threatens national unity, and aims to legitimize an illegitimate military authority. The groups asserted that their priority is to stop the war and initiate a serious peace process.

Both 'Somoud' and 'Tassiss and Somoud' reiterated their commitment to ending the war and achieving a just peace that addresses the root causes of the crisis. They expressed openness to any serious initiative prioritizing the cessation of hostilities and the alleviation of civilian suffering. The groups vowed to resist any attempts to undermine Sudan's unity or prolong the conflict and oppression.

Why it mattersThe Sudanese civilian groups 'Somoud' and 'Tassiss and Somoud' are affected by the African Union's endorsement of a dialogue initiative they view as a 'farce' that legitimizes the current military rule and ignores the ongoing war and humanitarian crisis.
