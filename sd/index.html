Australia sanctions four Sudanese figures over rights abuses

Travel bans and financial restrictions target SAF and RSF leaders, plus an Islamist paramilitary group.

Australia placed financial sanctions and travel bans on four Sudanese individuals, including SAF Commander-in-Chief General Abdel Fattah Abdelrahman al-Burhan and RSF commander Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo Mousa. West Darfur governor Al-Tijani Al-Tahir Karshoum and RSF field commander Hussein Barsham also face sanctions. The Al-Baraa Bin Malik Brigade, an Islamist paramilitary group aligned with the SAF, is also subject to financial sanctions.

The Australian government stated that both the SAF and RSF committed serious human rights violations and abuses, such as attacks on civilians, torture, sexual violence, and extrajudicial killings. "Those responsible for serious human rights violations and abuses must be held to account," the government said in a statement. The sanctions were enacted under Australia’s Autonomous Sanctions Regulations 2011.

The four individuals are now subject to travel bans preventing entry to Australia, and their assets are frozen. These measures build on previous sanctions against 13 individuals under the UN Security Council's framework. The conflict, which began on April 15, 2023, has killed hundreds of thousands and displaced over 14 million people, with nearly 34 million needing humanitarian aid.

Why it mattersFour Sudanese figures and one paramilitary group face Australian sanctions for alleged human rights abuses linked to the ongoing conflict.

Syrian community leaves Khartoum as conflict erupts

The Syrian community, once central to Sudan's economy, departed Khartoum in April 2023 amid escalating conflict.

The Syrian community, whose presence in Sudan dates back to the 1870s, left Khartoum in April 2023 as fighting intensified between the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) and the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF). The conflict, which began on April 15, 2023, has led to what the UNHCR describes as the world's most devastating humanitarian crisis.

Syrian migrants, including merchants and professionals from Greater Syria, established a significant economic presence in Sudan by the 1950s. Early settlers traded goods like elephant tusks and Arabic gum, while later arrivals, including physicians, contributed to the nation's development. Their community was a central social, cultural, and religious hub until their departure.

The community's history in Sudan began with merchants from Aleppo in the 1870s, followed by a new wave in the 1900s. By the 1950s, businessmen like Nicola Malouf played a central role in the Sudanese economy. The Melkite Catholic Church on Jumhuriya Street served as a key gathering place before the community's exodus.

Why it mattersThe Syrian community, integral to Sudan's economic and social fabric for generations, was forced to depart their homes due to the escalating conflict.

Sudan war drives fuel prices over SDG 30,000 per litre in El Obeid

Residents face soaring costs for water, bread, and milk as transport grinds to a halt.

El Obeid, North Kordofan’s capital, is grappling with severe economic hardship due to the ongoing war. Petrol now costs over SDG 30,000 per litre and diesel exceeds SDG 35,000, crippling transport and forcing many to walk. Attacks on fuel stations and infrastructure have worsened the crisis, making it difficult for residents to afford basic necessities.

Hassan Ibrahim Hassan told Radio Dabanga that fuel shortages are acute, with a gallon of petrol costing over SDG 120,000 and diesel over SDG 140,000. Milk prices have also surged to more than SDG 12,000 per litre. Drinking water supplies are failing, forcing residents to buy poor-quality water at over SDG 500 per litre.

The price of two small loaves of bread has reached SDG 1,000, consuming a large part of government salaries that do not exceed SDG 150,000. Millet costs over SDG 160,000 per keila, and meat is over SDG 50,000 per kilogramme. These high prices, combined with insufficient rainfall, raise fears of a severe humanitarian catastrophe.

Drone attacks kill 14 civilians in Sudan

Power infrastructure damaged, disrupting water and electricity supplies across multiple regions.

Drone attacks across Sudan killed at least 14 civilians and injured several others between Monday evening and Tuesday. Eight people died in Um Dibo, West Kordofan, on Monday. On Tuesday, four died and over 10 were injured when a drone struck a fuel station in El Abbasiya Tagali market, South Kordofan. In Ed Damazin, Blue Nile region, a woman and a young man were killed and others wounded in strikes on Tuesday evening.

The attacks on civilian areas and infrastructure prompted warnings from the United Nations and the Arab League. The Blue Nile Civil Society Initiative blamed the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces and its ally for the Ed Damazin strikes, calling for an end to attacks on civilians. Blue Nile governor Ahmed El Omda condemned the strikes as an assault on civilians and property.

The strikes on power infrastructure have triggered widespread power cuts and disrupted clean water supplies. The Arab League condemned repeated attacks it attributed to the RSF on energy facilities in Khartoum, El Gezira, Sennar, El Gedaref, River Nile, and Red Sea states. The UN warned that continued drone attacks on critical energy infrastructure were disrupting essential services.

Why it mattersCivilians across Sudan are directly affected by drone attacks that kill people, damage property, and disrupt essential services like electricity and water.

Business · 2026-11-06

US proposes mineral trade act for Sudan

The Sudan IMPACT Act would offer American equipment and expertise for access to Sudanese resources, pending progress on civilian safety.

US Representative Jim Baird introduced the Sudan Investing in Minerals, Partnership, Agriculture, Commerce, and Trade (IMPACT) Act on November 4, 2026. This proposed law would provide Sudan with American equipment, technology, and technical expertise in exchange for access to its natural resources, covering both agriculture and mining sectors.

The initiative aims to aid Sudan's economic recovery, but its implementation depends on the US secretary of state certifying substantial progress in halting violence against civilians and managing national resources responsibly. Republican Representative Baird stated that economic cooperation could foster long-term stability if civilian safety remains the priority.

However, legal counsel Mutasim Ali from the Raoul Wallenberg Center for Human Rights expressed skepticism, calling it "too early to speak about investments and trade" while atrocities continue. Sudanese analyst Dallia Abdelmoniem noted the gap between the bill's ambitions and the political realities, questioning if conditions could be met amidst ongoing war and a lack of unified civilian leadership.

Why it mattersSudan's war-torn economy faces potential foreign investment in its mineral and agricultural sectors, contingent on peace and responsible resource management.
