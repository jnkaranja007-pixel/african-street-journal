Water runs short at Kosti displacement camp as fuel dries up

Thousands of displaced families at the Goz Al-Salam camp are going without steady drinking water as power and diesel supplies falter.

Drinking water has run short at the Goz Al-Salam Displacement Camp in Kosti, White Nile State, where power cuts and diesel shortages have left water supplies irregular for thousands of displaced families. The camp accommodates more than 5,000 families, most of whom fled conflict in Kordofan and Darfur, and many depend on water tankers for their daily needs.

Khalid Idris, the camp's chairman, said that deliveries have become increasingly irregular because of the fuel shortages. He pleaded with the authorities to allocate diesel on a regular basis and to add the camp's drinking water needs to the state's emergency response plan. The area's petroleum director has approved a diesel allocation, but Idris said the amount falls short of the camp's daily needs.

The electricity shortage follows the destruction of the Um Dabaker Power Station, which supplies power to Kosti. Jamila Mustafa, a camp resident with diabetes and severe kidney pain, said the water shortage weighs most heavily on schoolchildren and people with chronic illnesses, and that clinics in the market lack the medicines she needs.

Why it mattersSchoolchildren and people with chronic illnesses bear the heaviest load, with water costs rising as tanker deliveries grow erratic.

Drone strikes on Sudan power stations leave children without water and care

UNICEF says up to 15 power stations were hit in October, and the planting window for the 2026–27 cereal harvest has largely closed.

Armed drones struck Sudan’s power stations almost every day in the final week of October, with up to 15 hit during the month. James Elder, UNICEF’s Senior Adviser and Spokesperson in Geneva, told journalists the attacks, combined with drought, have cut children off from water, schooling and essential healthcare.

Elder, who had just returned from Sudan, said that when a power station is struck, water pumps and water treatment stop, and families turn to costly, unsafe alternatives. Cold storage needed to keep vaccines and medicines usable is also at risk. He said two in three child casualties in Sudan this year were caused by armed drones.

He estimated that about 825,000 children under five would suffer severe acute malnutrition in 2026, a form of the condition that kills. Elder said the rains could arrive late but would not undo the damage already done to the season.

Why it mattersFamilies whose power and water supply has been cut must pay for costly, unsafe alternatives, and an estimated 825,000 children under five face severe acute malnutrition this year.

RSF arrests Rizeigat members after deadly tribal clashes in East Darfur

Seven people died and six were injured after a Rizeigat attack on Um Dirab, an area inhabited by the Habbaniya tribe.

The Rapid Support Forces (RSF) have arrested several members of the Rizeigat tribe suspected of attacking a Habbaniya village in East Darfur State, in an effort to contain renewed intercommunal violence. The clashes followed the discovery of a Rizeigat tribesman's body in a Habbaniya village, after which a group of Rizeigat men attacked Um Dirab last Friday. Seven people were killed and six injured on both sides.

Brigadier General Mohamed Salman, deputy RSF commander in El Daein, told Radio Tamazuj that the violence did not reflect the character of either community and blamed criminal elements seeking looting and theft. He said suspects would be apprehended through tribal chiefs and elders, and urged both communities to be careful and accurate when filing complaints. Several Rizeigat suspects have been arrested, and the RSF is still pursuing Habbaniya suspects.

In September, armed clashes between the two groups caused about 12 killings and injuries before the RSF and local mediators intervened. Gen. Salman commended the traditional administrations of both communities for their work to resolve the crisis since the incident on 2 November.

Why it mattersTraders and families in the border areas where the two communities meet now face the same risk of attack that led to seven deaths, with several suspects already in custody.

Sudan's war economy keeps running despite sanctions, research finds

Targeted sanctions from Australia, the UK and the UN have raised costs for Sudan's warring sides but have not cut their access to money and partners.

Australia has imposed targeted sanctions and travel bans on three leaders in the Rapid Support Forces (RSF), on army chief Abdel-Fattah al-Burhan and on the allied Islamist militia, the Bara-bin Malik Brigade. A week earlier, the United Kingdom sanctioned 15 senior commanders from the RSF and the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF). Both moves follow a UN Security Council resolution passed unanimously on October 8 that extended Sudan sanctions for two months, including travel bans and asset freezes.

Research by the Sudan Transparency and Policy Tracker (STPT) finds that sanctions have disrupted some established financial and procurement channels in the war, which began on April 15, 2023. Designated businesses can move activities between jurisdictions, work through unsanctioned subsidiaries and form new commercial ties that make their transactions harder to trace.

The Red Rock Mining Company, which the STPT describes as SAF-aligned, faces sanctions from the UK, the EU and Switzerland, yet by late 2025 it still listed operations in Tanzania, Ethiopia and the United Arab Emirates. It also kept a European presence through a Rotterdam-based sister company, Red Diamond Industries. The firm Zadna, also military-affiliated, relies on Egypt-based partnerships and state backing in SAF-controlled areas to keep winning contracts and financing.

Why it mattersCompanies tied to the warring sides still hold contracts and financing abroad, so the sanctions meant to cut funding for the fighting are not stopping it.

Sudanese refugees urge ICC to speed up justice

Refugees in Chad told an International Criminal Court delegation that slow proceedings encourage further violations in Sudan.

Sudanese refugees in eastern Chad's Gaga camp met a visiting International Criminal Court (ICC) delegation on Tuesday, urging the court to accelerate its proceedings. They told the delegation that the court's failure to arrest accused individuals for crimes in Sudan was encouraging perpetrators to commit more violations.

Refugees described the court's proceedings as extremely slow, noting that only one defendant who surrendered himself had been tried so far. The ICC delegation stated that the court has no army to arrest suspects and relies on states for such actions. In July, ICC Deputy Prosecutor Nuzhat Shameem Khan reported progress in investigations into crimes in El Geneina and El Fasher.

Khan informed the UN Security Council that witness testimonies confirmed war crimes and crimes against humanity in El Geneina and El Fasher, reinforcing the preliminary assessment of the scale of these crimes. The ICC opened an investigation into alleged war crimes and crimes against humanity in Darfur on July 13, 2023, following violence between the Sudanese Armed Forces and the RSF.

Why it mattersSudanese refugees in eastern Chad are calling for faster International Criminal Court proceedings to deter ongoing violations in their country.
