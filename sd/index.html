UN Condemns Drone Strikes Killing 48 Civilians in Sudan

The United Nations decried drone attacks in Blue Nile and West Kordofan that killed at least 48 civilians, including women and children, and called for accountability.

The United Nations has condemned drone attacks that killed at least 48 civilians in Sudan's Blue Nile and West Kordofan states. In Blue Nile's Wad El Mahi, 31 civilians, mostly women and children, died in a strike near a displacement site, with dozens more injured. These victims had fled fighting in Geissan and Kurmuk localities. The UN resident and humanitarian coordinator in Sudan, Denise Brown, called for accountability for the "flagrant disregard for international humanitarian law."

In West Kordofan, the UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) reported two drone strikes on October 9. In El Nuhud, 12 civilians, including women and children, were killed and eight injured when a drone hit a building sheltering displaced families. A second strike in Ghubeish killed five civilians, including three children, in a neighborhood hosting displaced families. OCHA warned that increasing drone strikes expose civilians to further death and suffering.

Separately, a drone strike on a livestock market in Nyala, South Darfur State, killed 39 people and injured 43. Health officials stated the attack, attributed to the Sudanese Armed Forces, was intended to maximize casualties. The South Darfur Chamber of Commerce condemned the attack on the market, a vital economic center, as a violation of international humanitarian law.

Why it mattersDisplaced civilians and market shoppers in Blue Nile, West Kordofan, and South Darfur are directly harmed by drone strikes, facing death and injury.

Drone strike kills over 150 in Abyei market

A drone strike hit a busy market in the disputed Abyei region, causing a large fire and killing at least 153 people.

A drone strike on Monday at the Amiet Market in the disputed Abyei region killed at least 153 people, according to local officials. The attack, which occurred around midday, also caused a large fire when it hit a fuel depot, with some bodies burned beyond recognition. Around 8,000 people have been displaced by the bombing, local authorities reported.

South Sudanese officials and the caretaker information minister in Abyei, Yohana Akol Ngor, blamed Sudan's military for the attack. Ngor stated that the Sudanese army was responsible but provided no evidence. The UN mission in Abyei had previously warned that the presence of the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) militia in the region increased danger for residents and risked intensifying the territorial dispute between Sudan and South Sudan.

Residents, civil society groups, and local chiefs held protests on Tuesday to condemn the aerial attacks. They urged armed groups to respect Abyei Special Administrative Area as an arm-free zone and called on the international community, the African Union, IGAD, and the South Sudanese government to consider the plight of the Abyei people.

Why it mattersThe drone strike directly killed and displaced over 150 people in the Abyei market.

Sudan Firms Evade Sanctions Through Obfuscation

Companies linked to Sudan's war economy are circumventing international sanctions by obscuring their affiliations and manipulating business fronts.

International sanctions imposed on individuals and companies tied to Sudan's civil war have proven ineffective in curbing war profiteering, according to a new report. Entities designated by the US, UK, EU, and UN have adapted by obfuscating their connections to parent companies and operating through shell corporations. For instance, the Export Development Group, a subsidiary of the sanctioned Defense Industries Systems, presented itself at a London business forum for Sudan's reconstruction while downplaying its affiliation with the military conglomerate.

The Sudan Transparency and Policy Tracker (SPTP) report highlights that sanctions have been narrowly drawn and loosely enforced, allowing targeted entities to adapt quickly. Zadna International, a DIS subsidiary sanctioned by multiple governments, has reportedly become a primary beneficiary of contracts in army-controlled areas. The company was awarded a contract to resurface a road in Gezira state for approximately $7 million, a cost ten times higher than a previous estimate for similar work.

While some sanctions have had limited impact, such as preventing arms sales from Pakistan and Turkey, many sanctioned entities continue to operate and profit from the conflict. The ease with which these companies maneuver around restrictions underscores the challenges in effectively implementing policy responses to Sudan's ongoing crisis, where humanitarian needs are escalating.

Why it mattersInvestors are affected as companies linked to Sudan's war economy are evading sanctions, potentially impacting reconstruction efforts and financial transparency.

South Sudanese women decry loss of political representation

Activists warn that removing women from decision-making roles undermines progress made under affirmative action quotas ahead of upcoming elections.

Women activists in South Sudan are accusing the caretaker government of reversing gains in political representation, citing the removal of women from decision-making positions. This trend threatens progress made under the 35% affirmative action quota, with some states reportedly having no women in leadership roles. Jackline Nasiwa, Executive Director of the Centre for Inclusive Governance, Peace and Justice, stated that only two of the 13 caretaker ministers are women, and representation has also declined at the state level.

The decline is seen as frustrating for women who had secured leadership positions under previous administrations, raising concerns about the government's commitment to affirmative action and women's empowerment. Dr. Angelina Mathijo Bazugba, a women, peace and security analyst, noted that while South Sudan has progressive legal frameworks on gender equality, implementation and enforcement remain significant challenges. She highlighted that the minimum 35% quota has not been achieved across all decision-making positions.

Women's underrepresentation could weaken their influence over legislation, peace negotiations, gender-responsive budgeting, and policies affecting education, healthcare, and economic empowerment. Professor Pauline Riak, a national peacebuilding expert, urged the government to implement existing commitments on women's and youth representation, stating that adhering to quotas would strengthen the nation.

Why it mattersWomen activists are concerned that the underrepresentation of women in government will weaken their influence over crucial legislation and policies.

West Darfur Launches Mosquito Control Amid Disease Surge

Health authorities in West Darfur State initiated a three-day campaign targeting disease-carrying vectors, including displaced persons' shelters, due to rising mosquito.

Health authorities in Sudan’s West Darfur State have launched a three-day campaign to combat disease-carrying vectors, targeting several areas, including shelters for displaced people. The initiative comes amid growing concerns over the spread of mosquitoes and an increase in patients seeking care at health facilities. The World Health Organization and its partners are supporting these efforts, emphasizing vector control as crucial for disease prevention, especially given the humanitarian challenges in the region.

The El Geneina locality executive director stated that this campaign is the locality’s first of its kind and is vital for reducing mosquito-borne diseases. Local hospitals and health centers are seeing a large influx of patients, and officials hope the campaign will lower disease incidence. Continuous spraying is deemed necessary due to the increasing prevalence of mosquitoes.

The West Darfur Ministry of Health confirmed the three-day campaign will include displaced persons' shelters, with efforts focused on household inspections, eliminating stagnant water, and space spraying. However, a shortage of insecticides limits the campaign to only three of the state’s eight localities. The campaign's delayed start is attributed to difficult circumstances, and securing necessary supplies for wider intervention is stressed.

Why it mattersDisplaced people and residents in West Darfur are at increased risk of mosquito-borne diseases due to rising mosquito numbers and a limited insecticide supply for control efforts.
