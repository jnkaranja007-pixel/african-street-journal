Australia sanctions Sudan's El Burhan and Hemedti

Canberra imposes travel bans and financial sanctions on four Sudanese individuals over war and rights abuses.

Australia has imposed targeted sanctions and travel bans on four Sudanese individuals, including SAF Commander Lt Gen Abdelfattah El Burhan and RSF commander Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo, known as ‘Hemedti’. The Australian Foreign Minister Penny Wong announced on Thursday that financial sanctions were also placed on the El Bara Bin Malik Brigade. Al-Tijani Al-Tahir Karshoum, the de facto governor of West Darfur, and RSF field commander Hussein Barsham also face financial sanctions and travel bans.

Wong stated that the RSF and the SAF have committed serious human rights violations and abuses, including attacks on civilians, torture, sexual and gender-based violence, and extrajudicial killings. The sanctions build on Australia’s previous measures against 13 individuals under the UN Security Council’s Sudan sanctions framework. Dealing with the assets of listed individuals or the El Baraa Bin Malik Brigade without ministerial authorisation is now a criminal offence, and the four individuals are barred from entering or remaining in Australia.

Australia reported that hundreds of thousands have been killed and over 14 million displaced since the war began on April 15, 2023. Almost 34 million people, more than 70 per cent of Sudan’s population, need humanitarian assistance. Canberra has provided more than $67 million in humanitarian aid to Sudan, and Wong issued a humanitarian permit to ensure sanctions do not obstruct relief efforts.

Sudan formalised militias into RSF in 2013

The Rapid Support Forces were created from Darfurian Arab militias and attached to intelligence services.

Sudan officially formed the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) in 2013, attaching them to the National Intelligence and Security Service (NISS). These militias, including fighters like Mohammad Hamdan Dagalo ‘Hemedti’, were initially mobilised in Darfur in 2003 to counter rebel groups. The government admired this counterinsurgency model despite international criticism, including an ICC arrest warrant for President Al-Basheer in March 2009 for war crimes.

The RSF's formalisation was partly a response to internal security concerns, including a foiled coup in November 2012 involving the NISS chief. The RSF's growth and official identity were seen by some as a tool to prevent coups against the regime. Hemedti later stated that 'Sudan has no army but us,' highlighting the group's perceived power.

The decision to formalise the RSF occurred between 2013 and 2014, a period when Sudan faced economic challenges exacerbated by sanctions, as noted by Al-Basheer in his January 2014 'National Leap Speech'. Concurrently, Europe was dealing with a 'Migration Crisis', leading to the establishment of the EU-Horn of Africa Migration Route Initiative, known as the Khartoum Process, in November 2014.

Why it mattersThe creation of the RSF from informal militias and their attachment to intelligence services directly impacts Sudan's internal security and political stability.

Syrian community leaves Khartoum as conflict erupts

Syrian families who settled in Sudan from the 1870s departed their homes in April 2023.

Syrian families who settled in Sudan from the 1870s departed their homes in April 2023. The community, whose members arrived from Greater Syria, had established significant commercial and agricultural enterprises by the 1950s. Their departure followed the outbreak of fighting between the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) and the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) on April 15, 2023.

Architect and photographer Arwa Ahmed observed 10 trucks carrying men in civilian dress on March 18, 2023, less than a month before the conflict began. The UNHCR has described the resulting humanitarian crisis as the world's most devastating. The Melkite Catholic Church on Jumhuriya Street, once a social and cultural hub for the community, was central until their departure.

The earliest Syrian settlers in Sudan, arriving in the 1870s, were merchants from Aleppo involved in interregional trade. A later wave in the 1900s included professionals recruited from the Syrian Protestant College. By the 1950s, businessmen like Nicola Malouf, who also developed a large-scale agricultural enterprise, played a central role in the Sudanese economy.

Why it mattersSyrian families who had lived in Sudan for generations were forced to leave their homes and businesses due to the conflict.

UN struggles to shelter millions fleeing Sudan war

UN agencies can only reach 10% of 15.4 million people needing emergency shelter due to funding shortages.

Millions of people fleeing Sudan's devastating conflict face a lack of shelter, the United Nations warned Friday. The UN estimates 15.4 million people need emergency shelter and essential household items after more than three years of civil war. However, UN agencies have only been able to assist 10% of those in need due to funding shortages.

Mohamed Refaat, the International Organization for Migration's chief of mission in Sudan, stated that they could only reach 900,000 people. The war, which began in April 2023 between the Rapid Support Forces and the regular army, has caused over 200,000 deaths and triggered massive hunger and displacement crises. Around 8.6 million people remain displaced within Sudan.

Refaat reported that the UN agency had distributed over 40,000 tents since the start of the year. "But our warehouses are completely empty now," he told reporters in Geneva, speaking from Khartoum. Millions have fled across borders, and many who returned found their homes and essential services damaged or destroyed.

Why it mattersMillions of Sudanese civilians displaced by war lack shelter and essential household items because aid agencies cannot reach them due to funding shortages.
