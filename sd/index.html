Drone attacks kill 14 civilians in Sudan

Power infrastructure damaged by strikes disrupts water supplies and causes widespread blackouts.

Drone attacks across Sudan killed at least 14 civilians and injured several others between Monday and Tuesday. Eight people died in Um Dibo, West Kordofan, on Monday evening. On Tuesday, four people were killed and more than 10 injured when a drone struck a fuel station in El Abbasiya Tagali market, South Kordofan, also damaging vehicles.

In Ed Damazin, Blue Nile region, drone strikes on Tuesday evening killed a woman and a young man, and wounded others. The Blue Nile Civil Society Initiative identified the woman as Ilham Ali Shikan and blamed the Rapid Support Forces for the attack. The Arab League condemned repeated attacks it attributed to the RSF on energy facilities in Khartoum, El Gezira, and other states.

The strikes have severely damaged power facilities, cutting electricity to large areas and worsening conditions for civilians. The United Nations warned that continued drone attacks on critical energy infrastructure are disrupting essential services like clean water supplies. The UN Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA) expressed deep concern over the attacks.

Why it mattersCivilians across Sudan are suffering from civilian deaths, injuries, and disruption to essential services like power and water due to drone attacks.

Sudan war drives fuel prices over SDG 30,000 per litre in El Obeid

Residents struggle with soaring costs for bread, milk, and water as conflict disrupts supply lines and infrastructure.

Residents of El Obeid, North Kordofan, face severe economic hardship due to the ongoing war, which has crippled fuel supplies and driven up prices of essential goods. Petrol now costs over SDG 30,000 per litre, disrupting transport and forcing many to walk. The conflict has also damaged fuel stations and infrastructure, worsening the crisis.

Hassan Ibrahim Hassan told Radio Dabanga that fuel shortages are extreme, with diesel exceeding SDG 35,000 per litre and a gallon of petrol costing over SDG 120,000. Water stations are inefficient, forcing people to buy salty, unusable water for up to SDG 500 per litre. Basic food items like milk now cost over SDG 12,000 per litre, and two small loaves of bread are SDG 1,000.

Government salaries, capped at SDG 150,000 monthly, are insufficient as a keila of millet costs over SDG 160,000 and meat exceeds SDG 50,000 per kilogramme. These conditions, combined with poor rainfall, raise fears of a severe humanitarian catastrophe in El Obeid.

Why it mattersResidents of El Obeid are struggling to afford basic necessities like fuel, water, bread, milk, and meat due to war-driven price hikes.

Rebel coalition captures Sali town in Blue Nile

Sudan Founding Alliance forces advanced from two directions to seize the strategic military base.

The Rapid Support Forces (RSF) and allied Sudan People’s Liberation Movement-North (SPLM-N) fighters captured Sali in southern Blue Nile State on Tuesday morning. The fighters advanced from Kurmuk to the south and Sirkum to the west, according to local accounts. Sudan War Monitor geolocated a video showing RSF fighters outside Sali Health Centre, confirming their entry into the town.

The Sudan Founding Alliance (Ta’sis) coalition announced the capture of the strategic Sali military base, claiming significant army personnel were killed or captured and that weapons and military equipment were seized. The coalition stated its forces are committed to protecting civilians and their property in liberated areas.

The Ta’sis coalition described the victories as a significant shift in the fighting and a strategic step towards ending the dominance of extremist groups. They reaffirmed their commitment to advancing until remaining strongholds are liberated to build a Sudanese state based on justice and equality.

Why it mattersThe capture of Sali by rebel forces affects civilians in Blue Nile State who are now under the coalition's control.

US proposes Sudan resource deal amid conflict

The Sudan IMPACT Act offers equipment and expertise for access to minerals and agriculture.

United States Representative Jim Baird introduced the Sudan Investing in Minerals, Partnership, Agriculture, Commerce, and Trade (IMPACT) Act, proposing a bilateral partnership. This act would provide Sudan with American equipment, technology, and technical expertise in exchange for access to its natural resources, including minerals like gold. The initiative aims to support Sudan's economic recovery by covering agriculture and mining sectors.

The proposed US economic partnership with Sudan has sparked debate over whether it will aid the war-torn nation's rebuilding or empower competing interests vying for control of its resources. The bill's effectiveness hinges on the US secretary of state certifying substantial progress in halting civilian violence and responsible resource management. Republican Representative Baird stated that economic cooperation could foster long-term stability if civilian safety remains the priority.

However, legal counsel Mutasim Ali from the Raoul Wallenberg Center for Human Rights stated it is "too early to speak about investments and trade." Political analyst Dallia Abdelmoniem noted the gap between the bill's ambitions and Sudan's current political realities, questioning if conditions could be met during the ongoing war without a unified civilian leadership.

Why it mattersSudanese citizens face uncertainty as proposed US investment could reinforce existing power balances during the ongoing war, rather than help establish peace.

Sudan demolishes over 100 homes in Al-Jazira village

Residents protest demolitions, citing discrimination and lack of shelter as winter approaches.

Authorities in Sudan’s Al-Jazira state demolished over 100 homes in Taiba Al-Sheikh Abdul Baqi village on Tuesday. The Violations Unit of the state Ministry of Urban Planning carried out the operation to remove illegal structures from government land.

Wael Al-Amin, a land inspector, stated the demolitions were part of the mandate to protect state land. However, Adam Dahab, a traditional leader of the Mararit tribe, condemned the action as appalling and called for an assessment of the damage and documentation of the destruction.

A female resident described families enduring difficult conditions for years and questioned the authorities' failure to protect them, leaving them exposed as winter begins. The Central Committee of the Kanabi Conference rejected the demolitions as routine, calling for an investigation, suspension of further demolitions, and compensation for losses.

Why it mattersOver 100 families in Al-Jazira state are left homeless with winter approaching after their homes were demolished.
