Sudan army regrets South Sudanese mercenaries in war

Army chief al-Atta accused the RSF of recruiting tens of thousands of South Sudanese fighters as mercenaries.

Sudan’s Army Chief of Staff, Lt. Gen. Yassir al-Atta, expressed regret on Sunday in Khartoum over the alleged recruitment of tens of thousands of South Sudanese nationals by the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) to fight in Sudan’s war. Al-Atta described South Sudan as a “sisterly country” but accused the RSF of recruiting thousands of its nationals as mercenaries, alongside fighters from Chad, Libya, Niger, Ethiopia, Somalia, other West African countries, and Colombia.

A UN Panel of Experts report from April 2024 stated that South Sudanese nationals fought on both sides of Sudan’s conflict, some independently and others forcibly recruited, though the report did not link their actions to the South Sudanese government. Former South Sudanese Interior Minister Angelina Teny had acknowledged in November 2023 that South Sudanese nationals were participating in the conflict. Al-Atta also accused the UAE and Israel’s Mossad of supporting the RSF.

Al-Atta sought to distance the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) military campaign from ethnic targeting in Darfur and Kordofan, stating the army’s intention is to fight the RSF and its “mercenaries” without targeting communities. He indicated that attacks on electricity and infrastructure would not deter the army from defeating the RSF and capturing all Sudanese territory. The RSF and the South Sudanese government had not yet commented on al-Atta’s remarks.

Why it mattersSouth Sudanese nationals are reportedly being recruited as mercenaries to fight in Sudan's war, affecting their involvement in the conflict.

1,150 flee North Darfur villages amid insecurity

Families from five villages in Um برو locality moved to other areas or crossed into Chad between October 29 and 31.

Over 1,150 people were displaced from villages in Um برو locality, North Darfur, between October 29 and 31. The International Organization for Migration's Displacement Tracking Matrix reported the movement from Qary, Muzbid, Awari, Tari, and Um Haraz. Rising insecurity prompted the displacement.

Affected families moved to other locations within Um برو locality or crossed the border into Chad. This displacement occurred after another incident reported on October 24.

Separately, drone attacks attributed to the Rapid Support Forces on Sunday disabled the power stations in El Dabba and Abu Hamad in northern Sudan. This cut electricity to the two cities and surrounding areas.

Why it matters1,150 people were displaced from their homes in North Darfur and some crossed into Chad due to insecurity.

Sudan leader Burhan risks repeating Bashir's Russia gambit, analyst warns

Journalist Rasha Awad warns that seeking Russian investment mirrors former president Omar al-Bashir's failed strategy.

Sudanese journalist Rasha Awad warned on Saturday that military leader Abdel Fattah al-Burhan risks repeating former president Omar al-Bashir's mistakes by seeking political and economic support from Russia. Awad stated that Burhan's proposals for Russian investment in mining, infrastructure, and a Red Sea economic zone echo al-Bashir's past overtures to Moscow. Al-Bashir visited Russia in November 2017, seeking protection from American actions and proposing military cooperation.

Awad told Radio Tamazuj that al-Bashir's pursuit of closer ties with Russia did not prevent his overthrow in April 2019 following nationwide protests. She argued that renewed engagement with Moscow is a reaction to Burhan's failure to achieve peace and his lack of cooperation with international efforts, particularly those led by the United States. Awad believes Burhan is using the prospect of Russian alliance as a political maneuver to counter Western pressure.

The analyst questioned Russia's ability to provide the expected political and military backing, citing international and regional balances that she believes will prevent Russia from establishing a presence on the Red Sea. Awad pointed to Russia's military base in Syria, noting it did not prevent Bashar al-Assad's rule from collapsing in December 2024. She suggested Moscow's broader interests with the United States and Gulf countries would limit its unconditional support for Sudan's authorities.

Why it mattersSudanese citizens are affected as their leader's pursuit of Russian support risks deepening international isolation and failing to secure peace.

Blue Nile IDPs face food, medicine shortages

Displaced people in Sudan's Blue Nile region are struggling with hunger, disease, and a lack of healthcare.

Displaced people in Sudan’s Blue Nile region are facing severe shortages of food and medicine, alongside increased mosquito-borne diseases. Approximately 27,400 individuals have been displaced from various localities in the region. Humanitarian organizations like the World Food Programme and Adventist Development and Relief Agency are providing food baskets and cash assistance, while Islamic Relief distributes hygiene supplies to about 10,600 pregnant and breastfeeding women.

However, the aid provided is insufficient to meet basic needs, according to displaced individuals. Issa Khamis, who fled the Balaro area, described struggling with food scarcity and difficult living conditions due to disease. Al-Radhiya Arbab, a mother of 12 from Kurmuk locality, called for improved healthcare services and the availability of medicines.

Beyond immediate needs, displaced families from Kurmuk locality are experiencing worsening hunger. There is also a call for educational services for children and employment opportunities for young people to help them support their families. The Blue Nile Humanitarian Aid Commissioner, Gisma Abdel-Karim, stated that efforts are underway to coordinate with partners for an immediate response.

Sudanese poet Khalil Farrah's treatment recalled

A film about English poet John Keats draws parallels to Farrah's final days and restrictive diet.

The treatment of Sudanese poet Khalil Farrah during his final illness has been recalled, drawing parallels to the life of English poet John Keats. Farrah, a famous poet of the "Haqiba" era, was accompanied on his final treatment journey to Egypt by Haddabai and Al-Zubair Pasha's son, Al-Tarifi. Haddabai had objected to the restrictive diet imposed on Farrah, which consisted of only small portions per meal, but his concerns were not heeded.

This recollection was prompted by watching the Netflix film "The Bright Star," directed by Jane Campion. Campion, who previously won the Palme d'Or for "The Piano," described "The Bright Star" as her most challenging film. She struggled to find an actor capable of reciting poetry, eventually casting Ben Whishaw for his exceptional skill in delivering verse, a talent BBC also recognizes.

Whishaw's portrayal of Keats, who suffered from tuberculosis, mirrored the fragility and artistic brilliance associated with Farrah. The film depicts Keats's physical decline and his continued creation of poetry despite his illness. This echoes accounts of Farrah's own struggles, including a severe coughing fit where he spat blood, an event that deeply moved his companions at Fawzi's salon in Omdurman.

Why it mattersSudanese cultural figures and their health struggles are brought to the attention of readers through a comparison with a foreign film.
