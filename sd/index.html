Sudan war leaves millions without shelter as funds dry up

UN agencies warn that nearly 15.4 million people displaced by conflict need emergency shelter and essential items.

Millions of Sudanese people face losing emergency shelter as humanitarian funding dwindles, the UN refugee agency (UNHCR) and the International Organization for Migration (IOM) warned. The nearly four-year conflict has forced countless families to flee their homes repeatedly as fighting advanced and front lines changed. This year alone, conflict in Blue Nile State displaced nearly 120,000 people since January.

Abubakarr Jalloh, UNHCR’s deputy representative, stated that three in four displaced individuals are women and children, emphasizing the critical need for shelter and protection. He noted that tents offer privacy and safety, while solar lamps enable nighttime access to latrines. With winter approaching, many families face harsh conditions in damaged or makeshift shelters, with two in five living under plastic sheeting.

Food prices remain high despite harvests, with staple grains costing up to 40% more in September. Displaced households must prioritize food over blankets, supplies, and safe shelter. Heavy rains in summer destroyed hundreds of tents in Tawila, which housed about 900,000 people.

Why it mattersMillions of Sudanese displaced by war risk losing essential shelter and protection due to dwindling humanitarian funding.

Australia sanctions Sudan's El Burhan and Hemedti

Canberra imposes travel bans and financial sanctions on four Sudanese individuals over war and rights abuses.

Australia has imposed targeted sanctions and travel bans on four Sudanese individuals, including SAF Commander Lt Gen Abdelfattah El Burhan and RSF commander Mohamed Hamdan Dagalo, known as ‘Hemedti’. The Australian Foreign Minister Penny Wong announced the measures on Thursday, also sanctioning the El Bara Bin Malik Brigade. Al-Tijani Al-Tahir Karshoum, the RSF-aligned de facto governor of West Darfur, and RSF field commander Hussein Barsham also face financial sanctions and travel bans.

Minister Wong stated that the RSF and the SAF have committed serious human rights violations and abuses, including attacks on civilians, torture, sexual and gender-based violence, and extrajudicial killings. She added that those responsible for these violations must be held to account. Dealing with the assets of the listed individuals or the El Baraa Bin Malik Brigade without ministerial authorisation is now a criminal offence in Australia.

These sanctions build on Australia's previous measures against 13 individuals under the UN Security Council's Sudan sanctions framework. Australia has provided over $67 million in humanitarian aid to Sudan, supporting food, clean water, shelter, healthcare, and protection services. A humanitarian permit was also issued to ensure sanctions do not obstruct relief efforts.

Why it mattersSudanese leaders involved in the ongoing war and human rights abuses face Australian travel bans and financial sanctions.

Sudan formed RSF in 2013 to counter rebels

The Rapid Support Forces were created from informal militias to combat Darfur insurgents and bolster the government.

Sudan officially formed the Rapid Support Forces (RSF) in 2013, attaching them to the National Intelligence and Security Service. This move followed the successful but damaging counterinsurgency campaign in Darfur that began in 2003, which saw the government mobilise Darfurian Arabs, including RSF leader Mohammad Hamdan Dagalo, against non-Arab rebel groups. The RSF's creation was partly a strategy to prevent coups, as President Al-Basheer feared a strong army.

The RSF grew from informal militias that were deployed outside Darfur after the initial conflict. The government admired the model despite ethical and reputational costs, including an International Criminal Court arrest warrant in March 2009 for Al-Basheer. The RSF's formalisation aimed to provide an official identity and a tool to secure the regime, leading to internal tensions with the Sudanese Armed Forces.

This institutionalisation occurred between 2013 and 2014, a period when Sudan faced international sanctions hindering poverty alleviation efforts. Simultaneously, Europe was grappling with a migration crisis, leading to the establishment of the EU-Horn of Africa Migration Route Initiative, known as the Khartoum Process, in November 2014.

Why it mattersThe formation of the RSF directly impacts Sudanese citizens through its role in the ongoing war and its origins in internal conflict.

Drones strike civilian targets across Sudan

At least 42 drone strikes monitored since October, with only one targeting military assets.

Since October, Sudan has experienced at least 42 drone strikes, with nearly all targeting civilian infrastructure. In the first three weeks of October alone, these strikes killed at least 306 people. Warring parties have attacked 28 civilian targets in recent days, including power stations, markets, and a hospital.

On Monday, drones hit two power stations in Kassala and a sub-station in Merowe, causing widespread outages. A market in Nyala was struck, killing 102 civilians and injuring 113, according to the Emergency Lawyers group. Attacks have focused on commercial markets in Darfur and Kordofan, and power infrastructure across north, east, and central Sudan.

Economic expert Hossam El-Deen estimates losses from damaged electricity substations at $4 to $5 million, with repairs taking at least four months. Farmers in Shendi reported losing nearly $150,000 on a farm due to lack of pumped water, turning fertile land barren and destroying vital crops amid food insecurity.

Why it mattersSudanese civilians are directly affected by drone strikes that kill people, destroy essential infrastructure like power stations and markets, and devastate agricultural land, exacerbating food insecurity.
