Khartoum Returnees Face Housing, Health, and Security Woes

Residents returning to Sudan's capital after years of conflict encounter significant obstacles in basic services and economic stability.

Khartoum is struggling to recover from over three years of war, with returnees facing severe challenges in housing, health, water, livelihoods, security, and education, according to eyewitnesses and the UN. Power and water cuts persist, hindering the restoration of normal life, while unemployment is widespread as most economic activities have ceased. Rising transport costs, movement restrictions after sunset, and fluctuating goods prices add to the burden, exacerbated by blocked drainage channels from accumulated rubbish during the rainy season.

The education sector faces immense difficulties, including teacher wage crises that lead to schools imposing monthly charges on pupils or relying on parental contributions, contributing to increased educational loss and school dropouts. Repeated teachers' strikes and the displacement of students have disrupted academic progress. Furthermore, the proliferation of private and commercial schools lacking adequate facilities and qualified staff presents additional concerns for the learning environment.

The health system in Khartoum is in crisis, with widespread destruction of facilities and a decline in service quality due to shortages of medical supplies and ongoing power outages. These issues collectively paint a grim picture for residents and those attempting to rebuild their lives in the capital amidst ongoing instability and a fragile recovery.

Why it mattersResidents and returnees in Khartoum are affected by the lack of essential services like power, water, and healthcare, alongside economic hardship and educational disruptions.

Sudan Emergency Response Rooms Face Internal Power Imbalance

A newly formed coordination council grants disproportionate influence to Khartoum-based groups over aid distribution and agenda-setting.

Sudan's Emergency Response Rooms (ERRs), lauded for their grassroots humanitarian efforts, are grappling with an internal structural imbalance. The Localisation Coordination Council (LCC), established in November 2023 to manage state-level networks and engage with international donors, has a founding charter that allocates seven seats to Khartoum's localities while granting only one seat to each of the other thirteen states. This arrangement, despite the scale of need in regions like Darfur, gives Khartoum disproportionate influence over crucial decisions.

This imbalance was not an organic development but was written into the LCC's initial charter, a fact that has received little public attention. Volunteers from states outside the capital have reported feeling that their priorities are not adequately represented in LCC discussions. The concerns are particularly acute for Women's Response Rooms, groups formed to address the specific needs of women and girls, who provide essential services in areas where general ERR facilities are not safely accessible.

The LCC was created out of necessity due to the crisis's scale and donor demands, rather than as an original intent of the ERRs. The movement's volunteers have sustained communal kitchens, health centers, water systems, and evacuation networks across thirteen states since April 2023, reaching millions where international aid cannot. The Nobel Peace Prize nomination followed, highlighting the global recognition of their work.

Why it mattersKhartoum-based ERRs have disproportionate influence over agenda-setting, donor engagement, and funding priority decisions, affecting how aid is distributed across the country.

US Arms Embargo Proposal Faces Opposition from Russia, China

A US bid to extend an arms embargo across all of Sudan, backed by European allies, faces resistance from Russia and China at the UN.

The United States has proposed expanding the existing arms embargo on Darfur to encompass all of Sudan, a move supported by Denmark, Britain, and France. This proposal aims to pressure conflict parties to reduce their capacity for war and cut off weapon sources, thereby limiting civilian harm. However, China, Russia, and Sudan's representative have openly opposed the initiative, raising questions about its potential effectiveness in reducing risks, particularly for women.

Political and human rights activist Nemat Koko stated that the widespread availability of weapons directly fuels the ongoing conflict and increases violence against civilians. She emphasized that an arms embargo should be viewed not just as a security measure, but as critical for women's safety, freedom of movement, and their ability to protect families and access essential services like markets and healthcare.

Koko further explained that the proliferation of weapons restricts women's mobility and their capacity to support their families, especially as vital services like water, electricity, health, and education facilities are targeted. This collapse of services places additional burdens on women, particularly female heads of households, who face increased responsibilities in maintaining social cohesion and securing livelihoods amidst the war.

Why it mattersWomen in Sudan are disproportionately affected by the continued flow of weapons, facing increased risks of sexual violence, restricted movement, and greater burdens as essential services collapse.

Sudan Shifts Agricultural Investment From Projects to Systems

A new integrated model aims to connect recovery, markets, institutions, finance, natural resources, and peace for post-war reconstruction.

Sudan's post-war agricultural recovery requires a shift from fragmented, project-based investments to an integrated system model. This new approach, the Integrated Agricultural Investment Model for Post-Conflict Reconstruction in Sudan (IAIM-Sudan), views agriculture as a system for rebuilding livelihoods, markets, institutions, and the state's relationship with citizens. Previous investments often failed due to a lack of connection to the broader agricultural system, leading to issues like rebuilding infrastructure without restoring markets or providing seeds without resolving land rights.

The IAIM-Sudan model emphasizes understanding the political, economic, social, institutional, and environmental context before making investment decisions. It integrates analytical lenses such as political economy, conflict sensitivity, systems thinking, market systems, value-chain analysis, spatial development, and responsible investment. This holistic approach is crucial given the challenges of displacement, damaged infrastructure, contested land, disrupted markets, weak institutions, climate stress, and limited public financing.

This integrated model is designed to address the weaknesses exacerbated by post-war reconstruction. By connecting recovery efforts with markets, institutions, finance, natural resources, and peacebuilding, it aims to ensure that investments contribute to broad-based development. The model evaluates investments not just by what they construct, but by the systemic resilience they help to establish in a fragile environment.

Why it mattersInvestors are affected by the proposed shift to an integrated agricultural investment model that requires a deeper analysis of context and fragility before committing capital.

Sudan Liberation Movement Rejects El Burhan Dialogue, Backs Quad Initiative

Abdelwahid El Nur's movement views El Burhan's proposed talks as an attempt to legitimize a coup, while eastern civil forces endorse an anti-war plan.

The Sudan Liberation Movement (SLM-AW), led by Abdelwahid El Nur, has reiterated its rejection of dialogue proposed by Sovereignty Council chair Abdelfattah El Burhan. The movement met UN envoy Pekka Haavisto in Nairobi, stating El Burhan's dialogue aims to legitimize his coup and will not resolve Sudan's crisis. They advocate for genuine, inclusive dialogue only after the war ends, excluding the National Congress Party, Islamic Movement, and their affiliates.

The SLM-AW delegation welcomed the international Quad's initiative, finding it laid sound foundations for addressing Sudan's situation. However, they expressed reservations about the international Quintet's performance, with representatives sending a letter detailing concerns and proposals. Meanwhile, the Alliance of Civil Forces for Eastern Sudan endorsed the "Peace We Want" document from anti-war civilian and political forces.

Eastern Sudan civil forces called for international sanctions against Ali Karti's group and fully supported the Addis Ababa meeting's outcome, which rejects initiatives not leading to a comprehensive end to the war. Their spokesman, Saleh Ammar, confirmed support for the Quad initiative's principles, including preserving Sudan's unity, ending the war, and placing civilians in charge of the political process, stressing the need to protect the process from warring parties and external influences.

Why it mattersThe rejection of proposed dialogues by key Sudanese factions and the endorsement of alternative peace initiatives directly impact the country's political future and the potential for ending the ongoing conflict.
