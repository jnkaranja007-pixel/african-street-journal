Central African Republic Public Finances Strain Under Wage Bill

Despite revenue growth, high personnel expenses and low external funding limit public investment and social spending.

Central African Republic's public revenues increased in the first half of 2026, reaching 166.08 billion CFA francs by the end of June, which is 45.08% of the annual forecast. This represents a nearly 29% rise compared to the same period in 2025, driven primarily by domestic resources totaling 109.8 billion CFA francs. However, external financing remains low at 56.28 billion CFA francs, only 34.82% of the annual target.

Public spending, however, outpaced revenue, reaching 179.47 billion CFA francs by June, resulting in a budget deficit of 13.39 billion CFA francs. A significant portion of these expenditures, nearly 60% of the annual allocation, went to personnel costs, which rose by 17.21% compared to the previous year. This trend threatens to exceed the annual budget for wages.

The heavy wage bill directly impacts funds available for other state priorities. Public investments and social programs are experiencing slower execution rates, with social spending at 33.08% and health sector spending at only 14.07% by mid-year. Rural development spending was even lower at 6.59%, though education showed a higher execution rate of 51.35%, largely due to personnel costs, with educational investments at a mere 0.81%.

Why it mattersFarmers and the general population are affected by the slow execution of public spending in rural development and social sectors due to the high wage bill.

Constitutional Council's Validation Faces Legal Challenges

The Central African Republic's constitutional council is becoming a battleground for legal interpretations, with the government and opposition clashing over legislative validity.

Radio Ndeke Luka has transformed into a political and media arena where the Bangui government and the main democratic opposition are engaged in a legal dispute. This situation has turned the nation into a testing ground for legal experiments, as interpretations of laws and regulations have become a widespread activity since 2016.

The current administration's primary contribution to democratic construction since its first term appears to be its interpretation of legal texts, often bypassing the constitutional council. This practice has led to a situation where legal interpretations are no longer the exclusive domain of jurists, but a national pastime.

The ongoing legal contestation raises questions about the validity of parliamentary candidacies and the potential for future disqualification of deputies based on nationality. The council's role in validating candidacies is being scrutinized amidst this broader trend of legislative interpretation.

Why it mattersThe political and legal maneuvering affects citizens by creating uncertainty around the legitimacy of elected officials and the interpretation of national laws.

Central African Republic Lacks Distinct Cultural Identity, Says Former Lawmaker

Jean-Pierre Mara states the nation has no recognizable traditional attire, architecture, or art forms that can be directly linked to its people.

Former lawmaker Jean-Pierre Mara has observed a striking absence of visible cultural elements uniquely identifiable as "Central African." He asserts that the country lacks a recognized traditional dress, an emblematic hairstyle, a typical architectural style, or any art form that could be immediately associated with the Central African Republic.

Mara attributes this void not to a lack of creativity but to insufficient public policy. He points to the absence of a proper museum, support programs for artists, and institutions tasked with preserving traditional skills as key factors hindering the emergence of cultural creation. This material void complicates cultural transmission, as visible objects and forms are primary markers of collective identity.

Unlike neighboring countries such as Cameroon, Ghana, or Nigeria, the Central African Republic has no distinct male or female costume. Furthermore, there is no traditional architecture adapted to the climate and recognized as a national symbol, nor any identifiable artistic line, motif, or emblematic sculpture. No houses are designed by Central African artists following a unique national model.

Why it mattersThe absence of identifiable cultural markers affects the transmission of collective identity and national heritage.

Ex-President Bozizé Accused of Ordering Arrest

A witness told the Special Criminal Court that former President François Bozizé ordered his imprisonment after a supermarket fire.

Bienvenu Ngaro, testifying at the Special Criminal Court (CPS) on August 27, stated that former President François Bozizé directly ordered his arrest and imprisonment following the fire at Rayan supermarket. Ngaro asserted that the accused currently before the court were not responsible for his detention. He recounted that his arrest documents cited charges of criminal association, arson, and violence.

Ngaro also testified that he did not suffer violence from Vianney Semndiro at the Bossembélé military center, describing an act of assistance instead. He confirmed that Bozizé regularly visited the military center but stated he never met him during his detention. The witness also claimed ignorance of Charles Massy, Firmin Junior Danboy, and Eugène Ngaïkosset.

The witness is seeking reparations, stating his imprisonment led to job loss and psychological distress. The CPS is hearing cases of crimes against humanity allegedly committed between February 2009 and March 2013 in Bossembélé, Bouar, and Bangui. The proceedings are set to resume with arguments from the prosecution, civil parties, and the defense.

Why it mattersThe testimony affects individuals accused of crimes against humanity, potentially impacting legal proceedings and reputations.

Ex-Minister Invokes Doctorate Amidst Fuel Crisis

Former minister Serge Ghislain Djorie attempted to deflect economic criticism by citing his academic credentials, a move rejected by civil society.

During a radio debate on the fuel shortage paralyzing Bangui and the provinces, former Communication Minister Serge Ghislain Djorie invoked his doctoral degree to counter economic arguments. Faced with data from civil society detailing a sharp decline in oil tax revenues and a deficit of nearly 10 billion CFA francs, Djorie shifted the discussion from economics to academia, suggesting a collaborative intellectual approach to national problems.

Paul Crescent Beninga, spokesperson for the Civil Society Working Group (GTSC), dismissed Djorie's attempt to trade the government's economic record for intellectual prestige. Beninga insisted that academic titles do not resolve the tangible issues of long queues at gas stations or the consequences of Neptune Oil's alleged illegal monopoly. He stressed that such debates require concrete figures, not academic posturing.

Djorie's appeal to his academic status inadvertently highlighted the disconnect between the ruling elite and the public's daily struggles. Central Africans grappling with rising transport costs and food prices are not concerned with their leaders' diplomas but with the availability of fuel at the pump, underscoring the urgent need for practical solutions to the ongoing crisis.

Why it mattersCentral African citizens are affected by the fuel crisis as they face long queues for fuel and rising costs for transportation and food, regardless of their leaders' academic qualifications.
