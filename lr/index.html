Cummings Demands Fair Trial for Howard-Taylor, Warns of Narco-State

Political leader Alexander Cummings visited former Vice President Jewel Howard-Taylor in prison and accused the government of selective justice amid a narcotics investigation.

Alternative National Congress Political Leader Alexander Cummings visited former Vice President Jewel Howard-Taylor at the Monrovia Central Prison, known as "South Beach," where she is held in connection with the country's ongoing narcotics investigation. Cummings stated his visit was personal, calling Howard-Taylor a "small sister," but acknowledged its broader political implications. He called for a speedy and fair trial, emphasizing the presumption of innocence until guilt is proven.

Speaking to reporters after his visit, Cummings accused the Liberian government of applying justice selectively and warned that the nation risks becoming a "narco-state" if authorities do not prosecute all individuals implicated in the drug trade, regardless of their political standing. He noted that the narcotics issue has become increasingly serious under the current administration and called for the arrest and prosecution of current officials involved.

Cummings also reiterated his demand for an independent investigation into the narcotics allegations, referencing comments attributed to the Inspector General of Police suggesting senior government officials were implicated. He stated that without knowing the identities of these officials, the prosecution process appears unjust and selective. Howard-Taylor, according to Cummings, feels aggrieved and believes authorities lack sufficient evidence against her.

Why it mattersPolitical leader Alexander Cummings is affected by the government's handling of the narcotics investigation and the potential for selective justice, which he believes could turn Liberia into a narco-state.

Liberia Expands Hospital Services Beyond Monrovia

The government is investing in the Jackson F. Doe Memorial Regional Referral Hospital to improve specialized care and reduce the strain on national facilities.

The Liberian government is expanding specialized healthcare services outside the capital with a significant upgrade to the Jackson F. Doe Memorial Regional Referral Hospital in Tappita, Nimba County. Newly constructed housing for healthcare workers has been dedicated, and ground has been broken for a new emergency room, intensive care unit (ICU), and additional staff duplexes. This initiative aims to transform the hospital into a major regional referral center, potentially Liberia’s second national referral hospital.

Deputy Minister for Fiscal Affairs Anthony G. Myers stated that the investment demonstrates the government's commitment to inclusive national development by extending critical public services. Since 2010, approximately US$44.4 million has been invested in the facility, with US$3.9 million allocated in the 2026 fiscal year. Myers noted that further resources will be needed as the hospital's reach expands and improved roads make Tappita more accessible.

The expansion focuses on enhancing emergency and critical care capabilities to treat life-threatening illnesses and severe injuries. This improved service is expected to alleviate pressure on Monrovia's referral hospitals by enabling patients from southeastern and central Liberia to access advanced treatment closer to home. The new staff housing is intended to help recruit and retain qualified health professionals, addressing a persistent challenge for regional facilities.

Why it mattersPatients from southeastern and central Liberia will have better access to specialized emergency and critical care closer to their homes.

Secret Recording Admitted in Taylor Case Despite Privacy Laws

A Monrovia court's acceptance of a secretly recorded call may set a precedent for the case against Jewel Howard-Taylor.

Liberian law generally prohibits secret recordings, with constitutional and penal code articles protecting privacy and penalizing eavesdropping. This legal framework typically creates an exclusionary tendency, risking the dismissal of illegally obtained evidence before trial. However, a recent ruling in the Capitol arson case against former Speaker J. Fonati Koffa allowed secret audio recordings into evidence, a decision that may influence how similar evidence is treated in Jewel Howard-Taylor's upcoming trial.

In the Koffa case, Judge Roosevelt Z. Willie admitted the recordings provisionally, distinguishing admissibility from credibility. He ruled that material could be admitted if it was potentially relevant, leaving the jury to assess its weight. This approach, which prioritized the original audio over a transcription, could be applied to a wiretapped "gift" call that forms a key piece of evidence against Howard-Taylor, despite defense objections regarding the recording's quality and authenticity.

The government considers the wiretapped "gift" call, where Sheikh Bassirou Kante allegedly informed the former Vice President of a payment from Dubai investors, to be its strongest evidence. Howard-Taylor's lawyers are expected to challenge its admissibility, likely citing the strong privacy protections in Liberian law. The precedent set by the Koffa trial, where a judge allowed questionable recordings, may now be crucial in determining whether this call reaches the jury.

Why it mattersThe admission of secret recordings in court cases affects the legal process and the potential for convictions, impacting those facing charges and the integrity of the justice system.

Nimba Elders Back Boakai’s Drug Fight, Warn Illicit Drugs Threaten Liberia’s Future

The Nimba Council of Elders has publicly endorsed President Boakai's administration's efforts to combat illegal narcotics, emphasizing the widespread societal damage caused.

The Nimba Council of Elders has declared its support for the government's ongoing campaign against illicit drugs, commending President Joseph Nyuma Boakai's administration for its resolve. The council stated that the government must remain steadfast in identifying illegal drug activities, holding offenders accountable, and ensuring legal processes are followed, irrespective of the perpetrator's standing or influence.

Acknowledging that no government can entirely prevent the smuggling of illegal substances, the Elders highlighted that true leadership is demonstrated by uncovering such activities and prosecuting those responsible. They urged the administration to pursue the drug war "without fear or favor," stressing that drugs affect all segments of society, destroy youth, weaken families, and jeopardize the nation's future.

The statement arrives amidst growing national concern over Liberia's escalating drug crisis and recent significant drug seizures. The Nimba elders also emphasized that combating illicit drugs is a shared responsibility, calling for active participation from traditional and religious leaders, parents, youth, civil society, communities, and all citizens in prevention and trafficking efforts.

Why it mattersThe Nimba Council of Elders is affected by the escalating drug crisis, which they state destroys the lives of young people, weakens families, and threatens the future of the nation.

Liberians Face Kidney Disease Crisis

Experts estimate 800,000 Liberians have kidney disease, with many unaware and facing grim survival rates.

At least 800,000 Liberians may have kidney disease, according to experts, a condition often undetected until advanced stages. The country's sole public dialysis center, established in 2022, is already overwhelmed. Many patients, like 45-year-old Anthony Gbeh, require costly weekly treatments of $US300 for dialysis, leading to job loss and financial ruin. Gbeh, who now begs for money to survive, exemplifies the devastating personal impact of the illness.

Health officials attribute the rising tide of kidney failure to a dual burden: increasing rates of diabetes and high blood pressure, coupled with a lack of public awareness regarding prevention and control. Regional factors such as malaria, HIV, hepatitis B and C, sickle cell disease, and the overuse of common painkillers and herbal remedies also contribute significantly to kidney damage. Early detection through simple urine tests is often missed.

Chronic kidney disease is frequently termed a silent killer, progressing for years without noticeable symptoms until severe damage occurs. By the time swelling, fatigue, or high blood pressure manifest, the condition is advanced. While a nationwide count is absent, regional studies suggest 1 in 6 to 8 adults in West Africa are affected, translating to nearly 800,000 individuals in Liberia potentially living with the disease, many unknowingly.

Why it mattersApproximately 800,000 Liberians are affected by kidney disease, a condition that is often preventable and detectable early but leads to severe financial strain and low survival rates due to lack of awareness and overwhelmed healthcare facilities.
