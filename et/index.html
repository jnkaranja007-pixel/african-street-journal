Security · 2026-11-17

Sudan and Ethiopia trade accusations over armed groups on their borders

Each government says the other is hosting rebel forces that fight inside its territory, and both deny it.

Sudan's military says Ethiopia has let Sudanese paramilitary forces use its territory to set up camps, launch drones and stage cross-border attacks. In early November, the Sudanese military filed complaints with the United Nations Security Council and the African Union's Peace and Security Council, saying it had submitted evidence to back its case.

Within days, Ethiopia expelled six staff members of a Sudanese consulate and ordered the mission closed. Ethiopia accuses the Sudanese military of supporting rebels fighting federal forces in the Tigray border region, where fighting returned in September and shattered a 2022 peace agreement. The Associated Press could not independently verify either country's claims.

Jervin Naidoo, a senior political economist at Oxford Economics Africa, told the AP the situation raises the risk that instability inside Ethiopia becomes another front in Sudan's war, and the reverse. Sudan's army and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces have fought since April 2023.

Why it mattersTraders and families near the Sudan-Ethiopia border face the risk of fighting spilling across it, as both governments are now trading expulsions and complaints.

Experts warn Ethiopian smallholders over single-crop farming

Hailu Araya Tedla of PELUM Ethiopia says crop diversification must be treated as a basic risk-management strategy for farmers with few resources to recover from extreme weather.

Agricultural experts in Ethiopia are warning that heavy dependence on monoculture farming is leaving smallholder farmers increasingly exposed to climate shocks, declining soil fertility and market volatility. They are calling for an urgent move towards diversified production and sustainable soil management.

Hailu Araya Tedla, executive director of PELUM Ethiopia, an agroecologist, told Capital that climate change and soil nutrient depletion threaten the sustainability of the farm sector. He said diversification lets one crop feed the household, another go into storage and another be sold, spreading risk in rain-fed regions where weather is unpredictable.

A 2026 study in the Journal of Environmental Management analysed data from 1,458 households and found that intercropping substantially reduced drought-related production and income losses. It also linked crop rotation to stronger household food security.

Why it mattersSmallholders in rain-fed regions face crop failure and unstable household incomes when they rely on a single crop.

Business · 2026-11-17

OVID Holdings sets 20% carbon emissions cut under ESG plan

The real estate arm will change how it designs, builds and sources materials for its projects to meet the target.

Under its wider Environmental, Social and Governance (ESG) commitments, OVID Holdings has committed to cutting its carbon dioxide (CO₂) emissions by 20 percent, with the pledge sitting inside its "North Star" strategy. The building and construction industry accounts for roughly 37 to 39 percent of global energy-related emissions, which the company cites as the reason its real estate work is in scope.

Engineer Muluken Mitiku, chief operating officer of OVID's real estate business unit, said the target will be met through changes to construction methods, material choices and how projects are carried out. Practical steps include avoiding unnecessary tree felling and cutting the use of plywood for scaffolding and formwork, which the company says would limit deforestation and related emissions.

Hayat Mohammed, OVID Construction Design's chief engineer, named three stages where the company can act: design, construction, and monitoring and accountability. At design stage, she said building orientation, natural light and ventilation, structural systems and the volume of concrete and steel can all be adjusted, while construction-stage plans include reducing material waste and monitoring fuel and electricity use on site.

Why it mattersClients and contractors working with OVID will see changes in how its projects are designed, what materials they use and how waste and fuel use are tracked on site.

Business · 2026-11-17

Dodai secures $16M for Ethiopian electric motorbikes

Japanese energy giant Tokyo Gas joins $3 million extension for Ethiopian electric mobility startup Dodai, which has deployed over 2,000 electric motorbikes.

Closing its Series A at $16 million, the Ethiopian electric mobility startup Dodai took in a fresh $3 million from Tokyo Gas, the Japanese energy group, alongside Singapore-based family office Kaneto Capital Management. The new money follows a $13 million Series A round completed earlier this year, which included $8 million in equity and $5 million in debt.

Founded in 2021 by Japanese entrepreneur Yuma Sasaki, Dodai assembles electric motorbikes for Ethiopia's commercial delivery and logistics firms and runs battery-swapping stations, so couriers can exchange a flat battery for a charged one in minutes rather than hours. The company says its active fleet is close to 2,500 units and its roughly 150 staff are about 98 percent Ethiopian nationals.

Tokyo Gas is expected to provide technical and strategic guidance as Dodai scales its swapping grid across Addis Ababa, where about 10 stations operate. Under current rules, kits imported for local assembly enter with reduced or no duties, and the government plans 500,000 electric vehicles on the road by 2030.

Why it mattersTokyo Gas will advise Dodai as it expands its battery-swapping grid, which couriers in Addis Ababa use roughly once a day.
