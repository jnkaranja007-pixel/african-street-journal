Nigeria warns citizens over Addis Ababa airport altitude

Abuja says 16 Nigerians with underlying health problems died this year while transiting through the Ethiopian capital's main airport.

Nigeria's government has cautioned its citizens, particularly those with medical conditions, against travelling via Bole International Airport in Ethiopia because of the altitude of Addis Ababa, which sits at 2,334m (7,657ft) above sea level.

The foreign ministry in Abuja said 16 Nigerians with underlying health problems had died this year while transiting through the city. It said travellers with cardiovascular and respiratory conditions face an increased risk and urged them to "obtain appropriate medical guidance" before planning a journey via Addis Ababa.

The ministry stressed that it did not want to discourage Nigerians from travelling through the Ethiopian capital. It did not disclose the identities of those who died or the causes of their deaths.

Why it mattersNigerians with cardiovascular or respiratory conditions are advised to seek medical guidance before transiting through Addis Ababa's airport, which serves millions of passengers a year.

Security · 2026-11-16

Sudan and Ethiopia trade accusations over armed groups

Sudan's military and Ethiopia each accuse the other of harbouring armed groups that fight inside their borders, and each denies the charges.

Sudan's military says Ethiopia has let Sudanese paramilitary forces use its territory to set up camps, launch drones and stage cross-border attacks. Ethiopia accuses the Sudanese military of backing rebels who fight Ethiopian federal forces in the Tigray border region, where fighting returned in September and broke a 2022 peace agreement.

In early November, the Sudanese military filed complaints with the United Nations Security Council and the African Union's Peace and Security Council, saying it had submitted evidence. Within days, Ethiopia expelled six staff members of a Sudanese consulate and ordered the mission closed. The Associated Press could not independently verify either side's claims.

Jervin Naidoo, a senior political economist at Oxford Economics Africa, told the AP that instability inside Ethiopia could become another front in Sudan's war, and the reverse. Sudan has been at war since April 2023, when long-running tensions between the army and the paramilitary Rapid Support Forces turned into all-out fighting.

Why it mattersResidents of Ethiopia's Tigray region and Sudanese civilians near the border face the fighting now under way, and the consulate closure has cut off a Sudanese diplomatic post in Ethiopia.

UN economic commission urges African states to fund health data systems

Africa spends about US$145 billion a year on health, but planners often work from incomplete birth, death and population figures.

The United Nations Economic Commission for Africa (ECA) has asked African governments to treat health information systems as essential public infrastructure. At the opening of the First Africa Health Data Forum in Nairobi, ECA Chief of Staff Aboubakri Diaw, speaking for Executive Secretary Claver Gatete, said gaps in birth registration, mortality records and health statistics weaken how healthcare is planned, financed and delivered.

Mr Diaw said that accurate health records underpin sound economic planning and the fair use of public money. To make the point he described Tumaini, a fictional newborn girl in Nairobi whose registered birth means her needs can appear in public plans. He said millions of Africans remain invisible to national health information systems. "A child who is not counted is a child who is not planned for," he said. "A death that is not explained is money spent without learning."

Decisions on health workers, vaccines and public investment are often taken on population and mortality figures that are out of date or incomplete, even though the continent spends roughly US$145 billion on health each year. "When the denominator is an estimate, the budget is partly fiction," Mr Diaw warned.

Why it mattersGovernments that budget for health workers and vaccines on estimated population figures risk misallocating spending of roughly US$145 billion a year.
