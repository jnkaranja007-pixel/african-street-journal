Ethiopia taxes digital creators

New rules require influencers and online freelancers to declare earnings and pay taxes.

Ethiopia has introduced new income tax rules for digital content creators, including social media influencers and online freelancers, requiring them to declare earnings and pay taxes. Regulation No. 597/2026, published on October 28, mandates that income from digital platforms will be subject to business income tax or a 15 percent tax rate on other income.

Creators must maintain accurate financial records of all income, both domestic and foreign, including cash and non-cash payments from advertisements, sponsorships, and subscriptions. Those taxed under Schedule D, with annual earnings exceeding 50,000 birr, must pay a 15 percent tax quarterly.

Online platforms and payment facilitators must report Ethiopian-resident creators and their earnings to the Tax Authority quarterly. The Tax Authority can monitor creators' activities and cooperate with international platforms to gather earnings information, clarifying that foreign registration or payment locations do not exempt residents from tax obligations.

Why it mattersDigital content creators earning over 50,000 birr annually must now register, declare income quarterly, and pay a 15 percent tax.

Ethiopia finalizes six deals for WTO accession

With 11 of 17 bilateral negotiations concluded, Ethiopia moves closer to joining the World Trade Organization after two decades.

Ethiopia has six bilateral market access negotiations remaining before the World Trade Organization (WTO) Secretariat can draft its schedules on goods and services. During a meeting in Geneva on November 9 and 10, Minister of Trade and Regional Integration Yasmin Wohabrebbi Saeed announced that 11 of 17 initiated negotiations are signed. The new government, formed in October, has prioritized WTO membership for its economic reforms.

Once the remaining six agreements are finalized, the WTO Secretariat will consolidate Ethiopia’s commitments for technical verification. Minister Yasmin stated that WTO membership will anchor reforms and reinforce transparency for Africa’s fifth-largest economy. She urged negotiating partners to adopt a pragmatic approach to conclude the remaining deals.

The Ethiopian delegation briefed WTO members on legislative reforms enacted since April, covering foreign exchange, customs, taxation, and trading rights. WTO Deputy Director-General Xiangchen Zhang commended Ethiopia's reform momentum, pledging continued technical assistance.

Why it mattersEthiopia's bid to join the WTO, involving 17 bilateral negotiations, enters a verification phase after finalizing six more deals.

AMG Investment Bank gets licence to boost Ethiopia's capital market

The new bank plans to offer services including IPOs, bond issuance, and mergers and acquisitions advisory.

AMG Investment Bank PLC received its investment banking licence from the Ethiopian Capital Market Authority (ECMA) on November 13, 2026, at a ceremony in Addis Ababa. The bank, established with Birr 50.1 million in initial stated capital, aims to help businesses raise capital and access Ethiopia's developing securities market.

The bank's planned services include supporting initial public offerings, bond and sukuk issuance, underwriting and private placements. Abera Bekele Kejela, AMG Investment Bank's chief executive officer, said the bank will focus on sectors including manufacturing, agro-processing, logistics and energy, as well as emerging areas such as financial technology and green finance.

The bank's next step is to secure membership in the Ethiopian Securities Exchange (ESX). ECMA also licensed NileBridge Investment Bank PLC, bringing the total number of licensed Capital Market Service Providers in Ethiopia to 23, with 11 licensed investment banks.

Why it mattersBusinesses seeking to list shares or sell bonds will have another licensed bank to turn to, and the number of licensed investment banks in Ethiopia rises to 11.

Ethiopian bone-setter treats injuries without charge

Wogesha Birqe Yibre in Bahir Dar says she prioritizes treating poor patients and refers serious cases to hospitals.

In Bahir Dar, Ethiopia, traditional bone-setter Wogesha Birqe Yibre treats patients in her home for injuries. Birqe, who has practised for over 20 years, says she charges between 150 and 200 Ethiopian birr for those who can pay, but treats poorer patients for free. One patient, Yidersal Mengistu, chose her care over a hospital for a foot injury, stating he prefers the traditional way and had a good experience previously.

Birqe distinguishes between injuries she can manage, such as muscle pulls and minor dislocations, and those requiring hospital attention. She stated that if a patient presents with a serious, displaced bone or deep fracture, she refers them directly to a hospital. She expressed concern about other bone-setters who accept complex cases without the necessary skills, sometimes binding limbs tightly with bamboo splints called Timaq.

A study in the Mecha district found that 46.05 per cent of 836 people with injuries had used traditional bone-setting services, with social acceptance being the main reason. Birqe believes some practitioners delay referring patients to hospitals even when their condition worsens, sometimes only advising hospital care after multiple visits.

Why it mattersPatients like Yidersal Mengistu are choosing traditional bone-setters over hospitals for injuries, with some receiving free treatment.
