UN Security Council Faces Scrutiny Over Global Justice Record

Decades of conflict and crises, including in Rwanda, Bosnia, and Gaza, question the Council's 1945 structure's effectiveness in the 21st century.

The United Nations Security Council's authority, accumulated over eight decades, is under renewed scrutiny due to its handling of global crises. Historical examples, such as limited involvement in Vietnam, continued conflict in the Democratic Republic of Congo despite a UN presence since 1960, and the reduction of peacekeepers during the Rwandan genocide, raise questions about its suitability for contemporary challenges. The massacre in Bosnia's Srebrenica safe area and the 2003 Iraq invasion without Council authorization further highlight these concerns.

The Council's primary responsibility for international peace and security, including imposing sanctions and authorizing force, is concentrated among five permanent members with veto power: the United States, Russia, China, France, and the United Kingdom. This structure faced a significant test in Gaza since October 2023, where Palestinian authorities reported tens of thousands of deaths and extensive civilian suffering, while Israel rejects allegations of war crimes and crimes against humanity.

The effectiveness of an institution designed in 1945 in responding to 21st-century political realities is being debated. The Libyan intervention in 2011, authorized to protect civilians, ultimately led to Muammar Gaddafi's overthrow and influenced subsequent approaches by Russia and China to international interventions. These cases collectively demonstrate recurring challenges in achieving lasting peace and justice through the current Security Council framework.

Why it mattersThe UN Security Council's structure and decision-making processes are questioned for their ability to effectively manage current global conflicts and ensure international justice.

Tanzania Offers Two-Month Amnesty for Illegal Firearms Possession

The government is providing an opportunity for individuals to surrender illegal weapons without facing prosecution during September and October.

The Tanzanian government has announced a two-month amnesty period, from September 1 to October 31, for individuals illegally possessing firearms. This initiative allows people to voluntarily surrender their weapons at police stations, local government offices, or ward executive offices without facing prosecution for unlawful possession. Minister for Home Affairs Patrobas Katambi stated that the amnesty is a national campaign to remove illegal firearms from communities and prevent their use in criminal activities, aiming to strengthen peace and security across the nation.

Minister Katambi emphasized that those who surrender firearms obtained without proper permits or through illegal means will be protected from prosecution. He urged all individuals holding firearms unlawfully to take advantage of this opportunity. However, he issued a stern warning that anyone found with an illegal firearm after the amnesty expires on October 31 will face swift legal action, indicating malicious intent.

The campaign, implemented by the Tanzania Police Force in collaboration with other security and defense organs, is designed to complement ongoing police operations against illegal firearms. Inspector General of Police Camillus Wambura described the amnesty as an additional tool to prevent firearms from being used in crimes before harm occurs. The initiative also involves media and other stakeholders to raise public awareness about the program and its objectives.

Why it mattersIndividuals illegally possessing firearms are affected by this amnesty as they can surrender their weapons without facing prosecution.

Government Rules Pension Calculation Applies to All Qualified Workers

The Minister of State in the Prime Minister's Office stated that the current pension calculation formula is applied uniformly to all eligible public and private sector employees.

The Tanzanian government has clarified that the pension calculation rules, known as 'kikokotoo', are applied to all eligible employees across both public and private sectors. Minister of State in the Prime Minister's Office (Labour, Employment and Relations), Deus Sangu, made this statement in parliament on August 31, 2026, responding to a question about potential changes for police retirees.

Sangu emphasized the government's commitment to improving retirement benefits for all, including police personnel, as pension funds strengthen. He explained that pension funds utilize specific rules or calculation methods to pay benefits to their contributing members. These rules are applied equally to all employees in the public and private sectors, including those in the police force.

The minister further stated that the regulations do not permit different calculation methods within the same pension fund for members who meet the same eligibility criteria. This ensures consistency in how retirement benefits are determined for all qualified individuals.

Why it mattersAll qualified retirees in the public and private sectors are affected by the uniform application of pension calculation rules, ensuring consistent benefit payouts.

Tanzanian Vice President's Parentage and Faith Questioned

A public message circulating online has raised questions about Vice President Deogratius Ndejembi's ancestry and religious background.

Questions regarding the parentage and religious background of Tanzania's Vice President Deogratius Ndejembi have surfaced following his recent appointment. A widely shared WhatsApp message, attributed to Maria Sarungi-Tsehai, reportedly discussed Ndejembi's mother's origins in Zanzibar and a claimed change of religion while he lived in the United Kingdom. The authorship of the message and the veracity of its claims remain unverified.

Ndejembi was sworn in as Vice President on August 29, after his nomination and parliamentary approval. The constitutional requirements for the office, as outlined in Article 47, do not stipulate a candidate's mother's birthplace or religious identity. Key criteria include Tanzanian citizenship by birth, a minimum age of 40, political party membership, nomination, and eligibility for Parliament or the House of Representatives. The constitution also mandates that the President and Vice President must originate from different parts of the Union.

The discussion highlights concerns that focusing on ancestry and faith could undermine the principle of public office as a trust for all citizens, rather than a prize for a specific group. While freedom of expression is constitutionally protected, the impact of such discourse on political culture is noted. The focus, it is argued, should be on relevant qualifications such as integrity, experience, and public record, rather than immutable characteristics like parentage or personal faith.

Why it mattersThe focus on Vice President Ndejembi's parentage and faith, rather than his qualifications, risks undermining public trust and potentially discriminating against citizens based on their background.

Girl Flees Forced Marriage to Pursue Education

A thirteen-year-old orphan escaped her village to avoid an arranged marriage and continue her studies in Dar es Salaam.

Monica Msuta, aged thirteen, fled her home in Mbwewe village, Coast region, in two thousand twenty to escape a marriage arranged by her uncle. Orphaned and facing pressure to marry an older family friend, Monica prioritized her dream of education over the impending union. She secretly contacted her sister in Dar es Salaam, who provided bus fare for her escape.

Arriving in Dar es Salaam, Monica was supported by her sister, who despite her own modest income from a small business, ensured Monica could attend school. Now a fourth-year student at Vijaliwa Vingi Secondary School, Monica is excelling, particularly in Geography, and is preparing for her final examinations in October. Her sister has taken on a parental role to guarantee Monica's right to education.

Monica's story reflects the reality for many girls in Tanzania, where approximately twenty-nine percent of girls marry before eighteen, according to UNICEF. A significant legal disparity exists between the 2009 Children Act, which defines a child as under eighteen, and the 1971 Marriage Act, which permits girls to marry below this age under certain conditions, impacting over six million women and girls.

Why it mattersGirls in Tanzania are affected by conflicting laws that permit child marriage, hindering their access to education and future opportunities.
