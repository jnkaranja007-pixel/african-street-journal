Teachers Prepare for School Year Amidst Unfinished Renovations and Staffing Concerns

Comorian educators returned to schools on September 1 to plan for the upcoming academic year, facing challenges with ongoing construction and potential staff shortages.

Teachers across Comoros returned to their posts on Tuesday, September 1, a week before students are scheduled to resume classes for the 2026-2027 academic year. Preparations took place in a calm atmosphere on Mwali, Ndzuani, and Ngazidja islands. In Ndzuani, educators at the Mutsamudu mixed high school and Ongoju primary school reviewed the past academic year's performance and baccalaureate results. The headmaster urged a shared commitment from families, teachers, and administrators to ensure student success.

In Ngazidja, 68 teachers at Moroni High School, responsible for 30 divisions including ten final year classes, met to prepare for the return to school. While the institution aims to improve its 69% success rate, the headmaster expressed concern that unfinished renovation work could delay the start of classes. At the Moroni-Mbueni urban college, 36 out of 40 teachers attended a meeting focused on adhering to schedules and curricula. The school currently uses space provided by a private Franco-Arab school due to a shortage of classrooms for its 17 divisions.

At Singani ya Hambu school, teachers also reported for duty. The school serves 280 students across nine divisions, but its director anticipates a shortfall in staff following planned retirements in December and is requesting new furniture. The academic year officially begins on Monday, with public and private institutions preparing to welcome students.

Why it mattersTeachers and students are directly affected by the unfinished renovations and potential staffing shortages that could disrupt the start of the academic year.

Diaspora Transfers Fuel Comorian Economy, Exceeding 20% of GDP

Remittances from abroad now represent over 20% of the Comorian GDP, significantly impacting household consumption and economic activity.

Diaspora remittances have become a cornerstone of the Comorian economy, now accounting for more than 20% of the nation's Gross Domestic Product. This influx of funds, while not directly representing production, significantly drives household consumption and overall economic activity. The Central Bank of Comoros projects economic growth of 3.8% for 2025, a slight increase from 3.3% in 2024, alongside a slowdown in inflation, with diaspora contributions playing a crucial role in these figures.

Historical data from the World Bank reveals a dramatic increase in the significance of these transfers. In 2000, diaspora remittances represented approximately 3.7% of the Comorian GDP. This figure steadily rose to 6.2% in 2005 and reached about 9.4% in 2010. By 2023, these transfers constituted around 23% of the GDP, demonstrating a multiplication of their economic weight over two decades.

This reliance on diaspora funds highlights a powerful sense of Comorian solidarity, with thousands of families depending on these remittances for essential needs such as food, housing, healthcare, education, and construction. Economically, this trend positions the diaspora as a vital pillar supporting the nation's financial stability and growth, prompting a closer examination of the 3.8% projected economic growth.

Why it mattersThe Comorian economy is heavily reliant on money sent from abroad, which supports household consumption and contributes significantly to the nation's GDP.
